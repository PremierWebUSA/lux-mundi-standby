Prophecy and Fulfilment

Thirty-six passages from Israel’s scriptures set beside the New Testament scenes that read them as fulfilled in Jesus, from Bethlehem to the right hand of God.

About this study

The first Christians were Jews who read their own scriptures as pointing to Jesus. This page sets thirty-six of the best-known connections side by side: on the left the Old Testament passage, on the right the New Testament text that applies it to him. Each note gives the passage’s original setting in brief and how the New Testament reads it, so the reader can weigh both.

Each pair carries one of three labels. Explicit means the New Testament itself quotes the passage and applies it to Jesus, often with the words “that it might be fulfilled”. Allusion means the New Testament echoes the passage’s words or images without a formal citation. Typology means an earlier person, event or ritual is read as a pattern that foreshadows him.

His birth and line

6
I

Born of a virgin

Cited as fulfilled

Isaiah gave this sign to Ahaz, king of Judah, during a war crisis in the eighth century BC, and the child’s growth set a near horizon for it. The Hebrew word almah means a young woman of marriageable age, while the Greek translation Matthew follows has parthenos, “virgin”. Matthew reads the sign as reaching its fullness in Jesus, conceived by the Holy Spirit and named “God with us”.

P05P06

II

Born in Bethlehem

Cited as fulfilled

Micah, an eighth-century BC prophet, looked for a ruler from Bethlehem, the home town of King David, whose roots reach back to ancient times. In Matthew it is the chief priests and scribes who give this answer to Herod the Great, the Roman-backed king of Judea, joining Micah’s words to the shepherd language of 2 Samuel 5:2.

P07

III

Heir to David’s throne

Echoed

Through the prophet Nathan, God promised David a son who would build the temple and a dynasty that would last forever; its first fulfilment was Solomon and the kings after him. Luke’s angel speaks to Mary in the same terms, and Acts 2:30 and Romans 1:3 make the Davidic claim explicit.

IV

A star out of Jacob

Echoed

Balaam, a foreign seer hired to curse Israel, instead foresaw a ruler rising from Jacob like a star. Matthew does not quote the oracle, but the star that leads eastern wise men to a newborn king draws on the same royal image, and Revelation 22:16 calls Jesus “the bright and morning star”.

V

Called out of Egypt

Cited as fulfilled

Hosea looks back to the exodus, when God called Israel, his son, out of slavery. Matthew uses his fulfilment formula for a pattern rather than a forecast: Jesus, the Son, relives Israel’s story, going down into Egypt and coming out again.

P42

VI

Rachel weeping for her children

Cited as fulfilled

Rachel, wife of the patriarch Jacob and mother of Israel’s tribes, was buried on the road to Bethlehem; Jeremiah pictures her mourning the exiles of his own day, and the following verses promise their return. Matthew hears her lament again in Herod’s killing of the Bethlehem children.

His ministry

5
VII

A voice in the wilderness

Cited as fulfilled

Isaiah announces a highway through the desert for God to lead the exiles home from Babylon. All four Gospels apply the voice to John the Baptist, the desert preacher who baptised Jesus, so that the way prepared for Israel’s God becomes the way prepared for Jesus.

VIII

A great light in Galilee

Cited as fulfilled

Zebulun and Naphtali, the northern tribal lands around the Sea of Galilee, had been humbled in war; Isaiah promises that they will be the first to see the light of deliverance. Matthew finds that promise in Jesus making Capernaum, a fishing town on the lake, the base of his ministry.

IX

Good news to the poor

Cited as fulfilled

Isaiah’s anointed herald proclaims release and comfort to the afflicted of Zion. In the synagogue of Nazareth, his home town, Jesus reads the passage, blending in a line from Isaiah 58:6 and stopping before “the day of vengeance of our God”, and declares it fulfilled that day.

P10P11

X

He took our infirmities

Cited as fulfilled

This line comes from Isaiah’s poem of the suffering servant (Isaiah 52:13–53:12). Matthew applies it first not to the cross but to an evening of healing in Capernaum, where Jesus takes the people’s sicknesses on himself.

XI

The serpent lifted up

Foreshadowed

In the wilderness, Israelites bitten by snakes lived when they looked at the bronze serpent Moses set on a pole. Speaking to Nicodemus, a Pharisee and member of the Jerusalem council, Jesus takes the scene as a figure of his own lifting up on the cross, and of the life that comes from looking to him.

Entry to Jerusalem and betrayal

5
XII

The king on a donkey

Cited as fulfilled

Zechariah, a prophet of the return from exile, pictures Jerusalem’s king arriving humbly, bringing peace to the nations. Jesus deliberately acts the sign out, sending for the animal and riding into the city at Passover; Matthew names both a donkey and a colt where Mark has one colt, following the doubled line of the Hebrew poem.

P17

XIII

The stone the builders rejected

Cited as fulfilled

Psalm 118, sung by pilgrims at the festivals, celebrates a reversal: what was thrown aside becomes the stone that holds the building together. Jesus quotes it in the temple at the close of his parable of the wicked tenants, and Peter returns to it in Acts 4:11 and 1 Peter 2:7.

XIV

Betrayed by a friend at table

Cited as fulfilled

The psalmist, sick and surrounded by enemies, grieves most over a trusted friend who shared his bread and turned against him. At the Last Supper Jesus applies the line to Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, who would hand him over that night.

XV

Thirty pieces of silver

Cited as fulfilled

Zechariah, acting the part of a shepherd rejected by the flock, is paid an insulting wage of thirty pieces of silver and throws it to the potter in the temple. Matthew sees the same price in Judas’s payment, flung back into the temple and spent on a potter’s field. Matthew names Jeremiah, though the clearest wording is Zechariah’s; the usual explanation is that he blends Jeremiah’s passages about a potter and a field.

P18

XVI

The shepherd struck, the sheep scattered

Cited as fulfilled

Zechariah’s oracle speaks of God’s own shepherd struck down and the flock scattered before a people is refined. On the way to Gethsemane, the olive grove where he was arrested, Jesus applies it to himself and his disciples, who that night all fled.

His suffering and death

11
XVII

Silent as a lamb

Cited as fulfilled

Isaiah’s servant goes to his death without protest. The Gospels record Jesus keeping silence before the high priest and before Pilate (Mark 14:61; 15:5), and in Acts Philip, one of the seven chosen to serve the Jerusalem church, explains the passage to an Ethiopian court official as a word about Jesus.

P25

XVIII

Counted with the transgressors

Cited as fulfilled

The servant poem ends with one who dies among the guilty while bearing their sins and praying for them. On the night of his arrest Jesus quotes the line of himself, and Luke goes on to tell of his crucifixion between two criminals (Luke 23:32–33).

P43

XIX

His hands and feet

Echoed

Psalm 22 is the lament of a man surrounded by enemies who is finally rescued and praises God. The received Hebrew text reads “like a lion” at this point, while the ancient Greek translation has a verb meaning “dug” or “pierced”, and the reading is debated. The New Testament does not quote this line, but its accounts of the nail wounds shown to Thomas, the doubting disciple, led Justin Martyr, the second-century Christian writer, to make the connection explicit.

P20P21

XX

Lots cast for his clothing

Cited as fulfilled

In the psalm the sufferer’s enemies share out his clothes as though he were already dead. All four Gospels report the soldiers dividing Jesus’s garments, and John, who quotes the verse, sees its two parallel lines in two acts: the garments shared out, and lots cast for the seamless tunic.

P22

XXI

Vinegar for his thirst

Echoed

Psalm 69 is the cry of an innocent man insulted by those around him, given poison and vinegar in place of food and drink. John says Jesus spoke of his thirst so that Scripture would be fulfilled without naming the passage; this psalm, with Psalm 22:15, is the likely background, and the Gospels each tell of the sour wine offered at the cross.

XXII

“My God, why have you forsaken me?”

Cited as fulfilled

Jesus cries the opening words of Psalm 22 from the cross in Aramaic, the language he spoke. The psalm moves from desolation to public praise and the nations turning to God (Psalm 22:22–31), and readers have long debated whether the cry voices only the anguish or also calls to mind the whole psalm’s ending.

P44

XXIII

Not one bone broken

Cited as fulfilled

The Passover law forbade breaking any bone of the lamb, and Psalm 34 promises that God guards every bone of the righteous. When the soldiers break the legs of the men crucified with Jesus but find him already dead, John sees both texts fulfilled at once: Jesus is the righteous one and the true Passover lamb.

P22

XXIV

They will look on him whom they pierced

Cited as fulfilled

Zechariah foresees Jerusalem, given a new spirit, mourning over one it has pierced as a family mourns an only son. The Hebrew reads “look to me” where John has “look on him”, and the shift in pronouns is discussed. John applies the verse to the soldier’s spear thrust, and Revelation 1:7 to the day when every eye will see him.

P23

XXV

His grave with a rich man

Echoed

Isaiah’s servant, though innocent, is buried as one of the wicked and “with a rich man”; the Hebrew of this line is difficult and translations vary. Matthew alone calls Joseph of Arimathea rich, and tells how he laid Jesus in his own new rock-cut tomb after asking Pilate, the Roman governor, for the body.

P24

XXVI

Wounded for our transgressions

Echoed

At the heart of Isaiah’s servant poem, onlookers realise that the one they despised was suffering in their place. Whether the servant is the prophet, Israel or a single figure to come is an old question of interpretation. 1 Peter weaves the poem’s words into its account of the cross without a formal citation, and this reading of the servant became central to the Christian understanding of Jesus’s death.

P25

XXVII

The Passover lamb

Foreshadowed

On the night of the exodus, the blood of a spotless lamb marked the houses God passed over and spared. Paul calls Christ “our Passover”, John the Baptist hails him as “the Lamb of God” (John 1:29), and John’s Gospel places his death on the day the Passover lambs were prepared.

Resurrection and exaltation

5
XXVIII

The sign of Jonah

Foreshadowed

Jonah, the prophet who fled God’s call, was swallowed by a great fish and brought out alive on the third day. Asked for a sign, Jesus offers only this one: his own time in the grave and return. The span from Friday to Sunday follows the ancient habit of counting part days as whole ones.

XXIX

Not abandoned to the grave

Cited as fulfilled

The psalmist trusts that God will not hand him over to Sheol, the realm of the dead; whether he first meant rescue from death or life beyond it is debated. Peter at Pentecost, and Paul in Acts 13:35–37, argue that David died and his tomb is still there, so the words find their full sense in Jesus raised from the dead.

P31

XXX

“You are my Son”

Cited as fulfilled

Psalm 2 is a royal psalm, probably sung when a king of Judah was enthroned and God declared him his son. Preaching in a synagogue in Asia Minor, Paul reads the decree as spoken over Jesus at his resurrection, and Hebrews 1:5 uses it to set the Son above the angels.

P04

XXXI

At God’s right hand

Cited as fulfilled

A royal psalm in which God seats the king beside him in the place of honour. Jesus himself asks how David can call his own descendant “Lord” (Mark 12:35–37), and from Peter’s first sermon onward it is one of the passages the New Testament returns to most often to describe his exaltation.

P32

XXXII

The Son of Man on the clouds

Echoed

In Daniel’s vision the empires of the world appear as beasts, and then one like a son of man receives an everlasting kingdom from God, the Ancient of Days. Whether the figure was first an individual or stood for “the holy ones” of Israel is debated. On trial before the high priest Caiaphas, Jesus joins Daniel’s words to Psalm 110, and it is this answer that brings the charge of blasphemy.

P33

The nations and the kingdom

4
XXXIII

A blessing for all nations

Cited as fulfilled

When God called Abraham, he promised that through him and his descendants all the families of the earth would be blessed. Paul reads that promise as the gospel announced in advance, fulfilled as non-Jews come to share Abraham’s blessing through faith in Christ.

P02

XXXIV

A light to the nations

Echoed

God tells his servant that restoring Israel is too small a task: he is to be a light to the nations and salvation to the end of the earth. Simeon, an old man waiting in the temple for Israel’s comfort, takes the infant Jesus in his arms and greets him in Isaiah’s words; in Acts 13:47 Paul and Barnabas take the same commission for their mission.

P14

XXXV

A new covenant

Echoed

Jeremiah, the prophet of Jerusalem’s fall to Babylon, promised a covenant written on the heart, with sins forgiven and remembered no more. At the Last Supper Jesus calls the cup “the new covenant in my blood”, and Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jeremiah’s promise in full as fulfilled in him.

P27

XXXVI

The Spirit poured out

Cited as fulfilled

Joel promised that God’s Spirit, once given to a few prophets, would be poured out on young and old, slave and free. At Pentecost, the Jewish harvest festival fifty days after Passover, Peter tells the crowd in Jerusalem that this is what they are witnessing in the disciples, poured out, he says, by the exalted Jesus (Acts 2:33).
