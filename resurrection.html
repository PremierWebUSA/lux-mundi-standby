The Resurrection

Eight lines of evidence, drawn from the Gospels, Paul, Roman and Jewish writers and the first Christian leaders, set side by side for the reader to weigh.

About this study

Christianity stands or falls on one claim: that Jesus of Nazareth, crucified under Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor of Judea, was raised from the dead on the third day. Paul put it bluntly to the church at Corinth: if Christ has not been raised, the faith is empty.

The evidence for that claim is spread across many documents in this archive. This study gathers it in order, from the death and burial to the empty tomb, the appearances, the sceptics who changed sides and the first witnesses who suffered for what they proclaimed. Each line quotes its sources directly, so the reader can check every step.

Raphael, The Mond Crucifixion
I

He died by crucifixion

Jesus was executed by Roman crucifixion under Pontius Pilate, a fact reported by Christian, Roman and Jewish sources alike.

All four Gospels narrate the arrest, the hearing before Pilate and the crucifixion outside Jerusalem. Their accounts agree on the essentials: a Roman execution, carried out on the eve of a Sabbath at Passover time, with Jesus dead before evening. John adds that a soldier pierced his side to make sure of it.

Writers with no sympathy for Christianity say the same. Tacitus, the Roman historian, writing c. AD 116, records that Christus suffered the death penalty under Tiberius by sentence of Pilate. Josephus, the Jewish historian, writing c. AD 93–94, reports that Pilate condemned Jesus to the cross; the passage was later expanded by Christian copyists, but most scholars hold that its core, including the execution, is his.

Two later sources may point the same way. Mara bar Serapion, a Syrian prisoner writing to his son at an uncertain date (proposals run from the late first century to as late as the fourth), speaks of the Jews putting their “wise king” to death, without naming him; most scholars take this to mean Jesus. A tradition in the Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a, says Jesus the Nazarene was hanged on Passover eve. Hostile and friendly witnesses agree: Jesus died, publicly, at the hands of the authorities.

II

He was buried

Jesus’s body was placed in a known rock-cut tomb by Joseph of Arimathea, and the earliest Christian creed records that “he was buried”.

All four Gospels name the man who buried Jesus: Joseph of Arimathea, a town in Judea, described by Mark as a prominent member of the council, the Jewish ruling body in Jerusalem that had handed Jesus to Pilate. Joseph asked Pilate for the body, wrapped it in linen and laid it in a tomb cut from rock, closing it with a stone.

Many scholars judge that a named member of the very council that condemned Jesus is an unlikely figure for his followers to invent, and a public figure in Jerusalem would have been easy to check. Mark adds that Mary Magdalene and another Mary watched and “saw where he was laid”, so the place was known.

The creed Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15, which he had received years before he wrote it, includes the burial as its own clause: Christ died, he was buried, he was raised. The burial is not a detail added later; it stands in the oldest summary of the faith.

III

The tomb was found empty

On the first day of the week the tomb was found empty, a point many historians accept; Matthew reports that opponents in his own day explained the empty tomb as a theft rather than denying it.

Mark, Matthew, Luke and John each report that the tomb was found open and empty early on the Sunday morning. They differ in the details of who went and what they saw first, as accounts of a confusing dawn handed on along different lines tend to, but they agree on the central fact: the body was gone.

Matthew reports that the chief priests paid the guards to say the disciples came by night and stole the body, and he presents this as a bribed cover story, still told among the Jews in his own day. As Matthew tells it, the opponents did not say the body was still in the tomb; their argument was over how it came to be empty.

The proclamation began in Jerusalem itself, a short walk from the tomb, within weeks of the execution. In Acts 2 Peter contrasts King David, whose tomb was still there to be seen, with Jesus, whom God raised. Many historians judge that a message of that kind would have been hard to sustain in that city if the body could be produced.

IV

Women were the first witnesses

Every Gospel names women, led by Mary Magdalene, as the first to find the tomb empty, at a time when a woman’s testimony carried little weight.

In all four Gospels the discovery is made by women: Mary Magdalene, a follower of Jesus from Magdala in Galilee, with Mary the mother of James, Salome, Joanna and others. In John, Mary Magdalene is the first to see the risen Jesus and the one who carries the news to the disciples.

In first-century Judaism a woman’s word counted for little in public matters. Josephus states that women were not to be admitted as witnesses (Antiquities 4.219), and in the second century Celsus, a pagan critic, mocked the resurrection as resting on the word of an hysterical woman (reported by the third-century scholar Origen, Against Celsus 2.55). Luke admits that the apostles themselves at first dismissed the women’s report as nonsense.

Many historians argue that writers inventing a story to persuade would hardly have chosen witnesses their audience was inclined to discount. The formal creed Paul quotes, meant for public recitation, names only men. On this view, the women stand in the Gospel accounts because that is how the first Christians remembered it happening.

V

The appearances

Individuals and groups, including Peter, the Twelve, more than five hundred at once, James and Paul, reported that they had seen Jesus alive after his death.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians c. AD 53–55, but the list of appearances in chapter 15 is older than the letter. He introduces it with the words for handing on fixed tradition, “delivered” and “received”, and its balanced clauses read like a formula learned by heart. It names Cephas (the Aramaic name of Peter), the Twelve, more than five hundred brothers at once, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul himself.

Paul tells the Galatians when he was in a position to receive this. Three years after his conversion he went to Jerusalem and stayed fifteen days with Peter, and he also met James, the brother of Jesus. That visit falls c. AD 35–37, within a few years of the crucifixion. The creed reaches back to the men it names.

Paul adds that most of the five hundred were still alive when he wrote, an open invitation to ask them. The Gospels then narrate appearances in Jerusalem and Galilee: to the women, to two disciples on the road to Emmaus, to the disciples behind locked doors, to Thomas, and by the lake.

VI

The sceptics who turned

Two men who rejected Jesus during his life, his brother James and the persecutor Paul, became leaders of the movement after reporting that he had appeared to them.

During the ministry Jesus’s own family did not follow him. Mark records that they tried to take him home, saying he was out of his mind, and John states plainly that his brothers did not believe in him. Yet soon after the crucifixion his brothers are among the believers in Jerusalem, and James becomes the leading figure of the church there.

The creed gives the reason: “then he appeared to James.” James led the Jerusalem church until his death. Josephus records that in AD 62 the high priest Ananus had “the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James” brought before the council and stoned, a passage widely accepted as genuine.

Paul was a Pharisee, a strict teacher of the Jewish law, who set out to destroy the church. By his own account he changed course because the risen Jesus appeared to him. The churches of Judea, he says, heard only that their former persecutor was now preaching the faith he had tried to destroy.

VII

The earliest proclamation

From the first weeks in Jerusalem the resurrection was the centre of the message, and within a generation Jesus was worshipped as Lord.

Acts 2 sets the first public preaching at Pentecost, the Jewish harvest festival seven weeks after Passover, in Jerusalem. Its message is short: the man you crucified, God raised, and we are witnesses. The resurrection is not a later development of the Christian message; it is where the message begins.

Paul’s letters, written within twenty-five years of the crucifixion, already assume that Jesus is worshipped. He quotes an Aramaic prayer, “Maranatha”, meaning “Our Lord, come”, which must come from the Aramaic-speaking church of Judea, and in Philippians a hymn in which every knee bows at the name of Jesus.

Outside observers confirm the picture. Pliny the Younger, Roman governor of Bithynia in north-west Asia Minor, reported to the emperor Trajan c. AD 112 that Christians met before daybreak to sing a hymn to Christ as to a god; some he questioned had left the movement twenty years earlier.

VIII

Witnesses who suffered for it

The leading witnesses held to their testimony under persecution and death, and the generation after them insisted that Jesus rose in the body.

Clement of Rome, writing for the church in Rome to the church at Corinth c. AD 96, recalls Peter and Paul as men of his own generation who were persecuted and put to death. Peter and Paul were not passing on a rumour; each claimed to have seen the risen Jesus himself. Their willingness to suffer does not by itself show they were right, but historians widely take it as strong evidence that they sincerely believed what they proclaimed.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch in Syria, wrote to the church at Smyrna c. AD 110 while being taken to Rome to die in the arena. Against teachers who said Christ only seemed to suffer, he insists that Jesus rose in the flesh, was touched by his disciples, and ate and drank with them.

Ignatius ties his own death to that conviction: if the resurrection happened only in appearance, then his chains are only an appearance too. He wrote those words on the road to his own execution. The first witnesses and the generation that knew them staked their lives on a bodily resurrection, and they did so openly, under their own names.

Taken together, these lines converge: a public execution, a known tomb, a tomb found empty, which Matthew says opponents explained rather than denied, women as first witnesses, a creed reaching back to the men who saw, a brother and a persecutor won over, worship of Jesus from the start, and witnesses who held firm to death. Any explanation has to account for all of it at once. The first Christians gave theirs in three words, “He has risen”, and the reader is invited to weigh whether any other account fits the evidence as well.

Questions and answers

Questions historians ask about the resurrection

What is the historical evidence for the resurrection of Jesus?

The evidence is a set of early claims and reports, not a physical trace: an execution under Pilate, a burial, a tomb reported empty, reported appearances, and a movement that proclaimed Jesus risen from its first years Study: lines 1–8Dossier 06. The study gathers eight lines of evidence from the Gospels, Paul, Roman and Jewish writers and the first Christian leaders, quoting each source so readers can check it Study.

What is the earliest source for the resurrection?

Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians, chapter 15, written about twenty years after Jesus’s death (c. AD 53–55), is the earliest surviving extended account Dossier 061 Corinthians pageL17. Paul quotes a tradition he had “received”: Christ died, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Cephas, the Twelve, more than five hundred at once, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul StudyCompare: resurrection-witnesses. Many scholars date the formula to within a few years of the crucifixion, since Paul met Peter and James in Jerusalem c. AD 35–37 Compare: resurrection-witnessesGalatians 1:18–19. Most scholars, including many sceptics, agree that the formula is older than Paul’s letter and goes back to the first years of the movement; the debate is mainly over where the quoted formula ends and exactly how early it took shape Dossier 06.

Do non-Christian sources mention the resurrection?

Not directly: Roman and Jewish sources confirm Jesus’s execution, but not that he rose StudyDossier 04. Tacitus and Josephus both report the death under Pilate Tacitus pageJosephus 18 page. They do show, though, that his followers’ belief did not die with him. Tacitus says the movement was checked for a moment by the execution, then broke out again Tacitus page. Pliny, c. AD 112, reports that Christians met to recite a hymn “to Christ, as though he were a god” StudyPliny page. And the second-century pagan critic Celsus attacked the resurrection claim directly, which shows that Christians were openly proclaiming it and their opponents were answering it R111.

Do historians agree that the tomb was empty?

Many historians judge the empty tomb probable, and others are considerably more sceptical Dossier 06. All four Gospels report it, with women led by Mary Magdalene as the first to find it StudyCompare: empty-tomb. Matthew adds that the guards were paid to say the disciples had stolen the body while they slept, and presents this as a cover story spread to explain the empty tomb away Matthew 28:11–15. Some historians note that this story, as Matthew reports it, takes for granted that the tomb was empty; the dispute was over why Study. Paul’s creed says Jesus “was buried” and “raised” but does not describe a tomb StudyDossier 06.

Can history prove the resurrection?

History can show that belief in the resurrection was very early and consequential; whether God raised Jesus is a further question on which historians divide Dossier 06. N. T. Wright argues that an empty tomb together with experiences of Jesus alive best explains how the movement began A13A14. Others point to what the first leaders were willing to suffer: early sources report that James was stoned, and that Peter and Paul were put to death, for what they proclaimed Josephus 20 page1 Clement 5. Sean McDowell’s study of the apostles’ deaths argues that this shows they sincerely believed they had seen Jesus alive, though sincerity alone cannot show that what they believed was true A65. Some historians set aside supernatural causes by method, while others allow a divine act as an inference to the best explanation Dossier 06.
