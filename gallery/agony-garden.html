XVI

The Agony in the Garden

Giovanni Bellini · c. 1458–1460 · National Gallery, London

Giovanni Bellini painted this small panel in Venice around 1458–60. It is closely related to a painting of the same subject by his brother-in-law Andrea Mantegna, which also hangs in the National Gallery, and the two allow a rare comparison of how two great artists treated one scene. Bellini’s version was acquired by the Gallery in 1863.

Christ kneels on a rocky outcrop, praying, his back to us. Below him Peter, James and John sleep heavily, and along the road at the left Judas leads soldiers toward the garden. A small angel appears in the sky holding a chalice, the cup of suffering Christ has asked to be spared. Behind it all the dawn breaks, flushing the clouds pink over a winding valley, one of the earliest such sunrises in Italian painting. The tender light softens Mantegna’s harder stone.

The Gospels tell how Jesus prayed in Gethsemane, “Not my will, but thine, be done,” while his friends could not stay awake (Luke 22:39–46; Matthew 26:36–46). Bellini sets that lonely obedience against the coming of day.
