Suetonius, Life of Claudius 25: the expulsion over “Chrestus”

c. AD 120Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus· 3 min read· Revised 12 November 2026

In a list of Claudius’s measures, Suetonius says he expelled from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting ‘at the instigation of Chrestus’. Most historians see a garbled reference to Christ; a minority think of an unknown agitator of that name.

About this witness

Suetonius (c. 70–after AD 122) was a Roman scholar and imperial secretary under Trajan and Hadrian. His Lives of the Caesars, biographies of Julius Caesar and the first eleven emperors, appeared around AD 120. Chapter 25 of the Life of Claudius (emperor AD 41–54) is a rapid catalogue of administrative and religious measures.

Among them is one sentence: Claudius expelled from Rome the Jews, who were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus. The expulsion is commonly dated to AD 49 on the basis of the fifth-century writer Orosius, and is often linked with Acts 18:2, where Aquila and Priscilla have recently left Rome because Claudius had ordered all Jews to leave.

Chrestus was a common slave name, and Christians were sometimes called Chrestiani. Most historians think Suetonius reflects disputes within the Jewish community over claims about Christ, misunderstood as agitation by someone present in Rome. A minority think he means an unknown agitator of that name. It does not place Jesus himself in Rome, but if Christ is meant, it is a Roman echo of disputes about him within two decades of the crucifixion.

In his Life of Nero (chapter 16) Suetonius also lists, among Nero’s measures, punishments inflicted on the Christians, whom he calls adherents of a new and harmful superstition. That passage refers more clearly to Christians but says nothing about their founder. It is not part of this archived page.

The text

Chapter 25 of the Life of Claudius in J. C. Rolfe’s Loeb translation (1914), with Rolfe’s note on “Chrestus”. The rest of the Life, the other footnotes and the LacusCurtius page furniture are omitted; Nero 16 is not in the archive.

Life of Claudius, chapter 25

25.1 He rearranged the military career of the knights, assigning a division of cavalry after a cohort, and next the tribunate of a legion. He also instituted a series of military positions and a kind of fictitious service, which is called “supernumerary” and could be performed in absentia and in name only. He even had the Fathers pass a decree forbidding soldiers to enter the houses of senators to pay their respects. He confiscated the property of those freedmen who passed as Roman knights, and reduced to slavery again such as were ungrateful and a cause of complaint to their patrons, declaring to their advocates that he would not entertain a suit against their own freedmen.

25.2 When certain men were exposing their sick and worn out slaves on the Island of Aesculapius because of the trouble of treating them, Claudius decreed that all such slaves were free, and that if they recovered, they should not return to the control of their master; but if anyone preferred to kill such a slave rather than to abandon him, he was liable to the charge of murder. He provided by an edict that travellers should not pass through the towns of Italy except on foot, or in a chair or litter. He stationed a cohort at Puteoli and one at Ostia, to guard against the danger of fires.

25.3 He forbade men of foreign birth to use the Roman names so far as those of the clans were concerned. Those who usurped the privileges of Roman citizenship he executed in the Esquiline field. He restored to the senate the provinces of Achaia and Macedonia, which Tiberius had taken into his own charge. He deprived the Lycians of their independence because of deadly intestine feuds, and restored theirs to the Rhodians, since they had given up their former faults. He allowed the people of Ilium perpetual exemption from tribute, on the ground that they were the founders of the Roman race, reading an ancient letter of the senate and people of Rome written in Greek to king Seleucus, in which they promised him their friendship and alliance only on condition that he should keep their kinsfolk of Ilium free from every burden.

25.4 Since the Jews constantly made disturbances1 at the instigation of Chrestus2, he expelled them from Rome3.

He allowed the envoys of the Germans to sit in the orchestra, led by their naïve self-confidence; for when they had been taken to the seats occupied by the common people and saw the Parthian and Armenian envoys sitting with the senate, they moved of their own accord to the same part of the theatre, protesting that their merits and rank were no whit inferior.

25.5 He utterly abolished the cruel and inhuman religion of the Druids among the Gauls, which under Augustus had merely been prohibited to Roman citizens; on the other hand he even attempted to transfer the Eleusinian rites from Attica to Rome, and had the temple of Venus Erycina in Sicily, which had fallen to ruin through age, restored at the expense of the treasury of the Roman people. He struck his treaties with foreign princes in the Forum, sacrificing a pig and reciting the ancient formula of the fetial priests. But these and other acts, and in fact almost the whole conduct of his reign, were dictated not so much by his own judgment as that of his wives and freedmen, since he nearly always acted in accordance with their interests and desires.

Translator’s note on “Chrestus”

Another form of Christus; see Tert. Apol. 3 (at the end). It is uncertain whether Suetonius is guilty of an error in chronology or is referring to some Jew of that name. The former seems probable because of the absence of quodam. Tac. Ann. 15.44, uses the correct form, Christus, and states that He was executed in the reign of Tiberius.
