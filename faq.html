Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers to the questions people most often ask about the historical Jesus, each pointing to the sources.

Did Jesus exist?

Did Jesus really exist?

Almost all historians, Christian and non-Christian, hold that Jesus of Nazareth was a real person A03A61. The evidence comes from Paul’s letters, written beginning about twenty years after the crucifixion, the Gospels, and Jewish and Roman writers such as Josephus and Tacitus Dossier 01. The view that Jesus never existed is held by a small minority. The archive treats it as a proposal to weigh on its arguments, not as an equal default Dossier 01A03. → What the evidence can establish

What do historians generally agree about Jesus?

Most agree on a narrow core: Jesus was a Jewish teacher from Galilee, associated with Nazareth, who preached the kingdom of God and gathered followers Dossier 01. He was known for healing and exorcism, came into conflict with the authorities, and was crucified under Pontius Pilate Dossier 06. His followers proclaimed very early that God had raised him Dossier 01. Confidence in particular episodes varies a great deal Dossier 01Dossier 06. → Guide and the world before Jesus

Sources outside the Bible

What non-Christian sources mention Jesus?

Several, written between about AD 93 and 165 by men who had no reason to promote Christianity, and some who openly disliked it. The Jewish historian Josephus mentions Jesus twice, around AD 93–94 R22R23. The Roman historian Tacitus records his execution under Pontius Pilate, around AD 115–117 R27. Pliny the Younger, a Roman governor, describes Christians meeting to recite a hymn “to Christ, as though he were a god” around AD 112 R26, and the Greek satirist Lucian, in the second century, mocks them for worshipping a “crucified sage” R29. → Witnesses Outside the Church · Roman writers compared

What did Tacitus write about Jesus?

Tacitus is regarded by many historians as the greatest of the Roman historians Tacitus page. In his Annals, written around AD 115–117, he says that “Christus”, from whom the Christians took their name, was executed under Tiberius by sentence of Pontius Pilate R27. The remark comes in his account of how Nero blamed the Christians for the great fire of Rome in AD 64. Tacitus is openly hostile towards the Christians, calling their faith a “pernicious superstition”, which is one reason historians accept the passage as genuine: it is hard to imagine a Christian copyist writing it that way Tacitus page. → Tacitus, Annals 15.44, with follow-up questions on that page

Did Josephus write about Jesus, and is the passage genuine?

Yes, in two places, both in his Jewish Antiquities (c. AD 93–94). In one he records the death of “James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ”, a reference almost all scholars accept as genuine Josephus 20 pageR23R100. In the other, the longer paragraph known as the Testimonium Flavianum, he describes Jesus as a wise man and teacher who was crucified under Pilate and whose followers did not abandon him. Most historians agree that this paragraph has a genuine core written by Josephus Dossier 04R24. Some phrases in it, such as “he was the Christ”, were probably added or expanded by later Christian copyists Josephus 18 pageR22. → Josephus, Antiquities 18 · The Testimonium compared · Antiquities 20

The Gospels and the manuscripts

When were the Gospels written?

Most scholars date them between about AD 65 and 110: Mark c. 65–75, Matthew c. 80–95, Luke c. 80–100 and John c. 90–110 R04Gospel pages. Some scholars date Luke as early as the 60s Luke page. Paul’s letters are earlier still: 1 Thessalonians, c. AD 50–51, is probably the oldest surviving Christian writing 1 Thessalonians page. The dossier stresses that these ranges are orientation points, not a consensus on every boundary Dossier 04. → The New Testament

Who wrote the Gospels?

The Gospels do not name their authors in the text. The names Matthew, Mark, Luke and John are attested from the second century R03Gospel pages. Papias, writing early in the second century, links Mark with Peter’s recollections R17. Their authorship is debated, and scholars differ on how far the traditional names can be trusted Dossier 06R03. → Papias, Fragments

Are the Gospels historically reliable?

Historians give different answers, and most judge episode by episode rather than all at once Dossier 06R07. The Gospels draw on earlier traditions and agree on the main outline, from the baptism by John to the crucifixion under Pilate, but they differ in detail and order Dossier 04Compare pages. Scholars such as Richard Bauckham argue that they rest closely on eyewitness testimony, and Craig Keener that they fit the conventions of ancient biography and the world of first-century Palestine A50A63. More sceptical scholars, such as Bart Ehrman, stress how the stories changed as they were retold before being written down A62. → Compare the accounts

What is the earliest New Testament manuscript?

It depends whether you mean the earliest writing or the earliest surviving copy. The oldest New Testament writings are Paul’s letters, written from about AD 50, some twenty years after the crucifixion; 1 Thessalonians is probably the first 1 Thessalonians page. As with every ancient book, the originals are lost. The oldest surviving copy of any New Testament text is usually taken to be Papyrus 52, a credit-card-sized fragment of John 18 in Manchester, dated anywhere from about AD 100 to 200 Manuscripts pageR08. The oldest collection of Paul’s letters, Papyrus 46, dates from about AD 200, and the oldest complete New Testament, Codex Sinaiticus, from about AD 330–360 Manuscripts pageR09R11. → How the Text Survived

How many New Testament manuscripts are there?

About 5,800 Greek manuscripts are catalogued, from small fragments to complete Bibles INTF Münster. Thousands more survive in early translations such as Latin, Syriac and Coptic, alongside quotations in early Christian writers Manuscripts page. No other ancient text has nearly so many Manuscripts page. Tacitus’s Annals, Rome’s great history of the same period, rests on just two manuscripts, both copied in the Middle Ages, one for each surviving half; his passage on Christus survives in a single copy Manuscripts pageTacitus page. No original of any ancient book survives, but this wealth of copies lets scholars trace the New Testament text back very close to its source Manuscripts page. → How the Text Survived

What about the other gospels, such as Thomas?

They are real ancient texts, but most were written later than the four Gospels in the New Testament, in the second century or after, and often in groups with their own distinctive teaching Dossier 04. The Gospel of Thomas, a collection of 114 sayings, is the one most often argued to be early. Many scholars date it in its present form to the second century and see signs that it draws on Matthew and Luke, while others think some sayings preserve early tradition Thomas page. The four Gospels are generally regarded as the earliest and fullest accounts of Jesus’s life. The archive reproduces the other gospels in full so readers can judge them for themselves. → The Other Gospels · Gospel of Thomas

Archaeology

What archaeological evidence is there for Jesus?

No object found so far names Jesus beyond doubt, which is not surprising: very few people of his time left any trace in stone. But many finds match the people, places and customs described in the Gospels Dossier 06. They include the stone naming Pontius Pilate, a bone box most scholars link to the high priest Caiaphas, the heel bone of a crucified man named Yehohanan, and the Pool of Siloam Archaeology pagesA16A17A18A20. They show that the Gospels are set in the real first-century world, though they do not by themselves prove particular events Dossier 06. → Stones and Scrolls

Who was Pontius Pilate?

Pilate was the Roman prefect of Judea, conventionally dated AD 26–36, under whom Jesus was crucified Pilate pageA16. The Gospels, Tacitus and Josephus all name him, and an inscription from Caesarea records his title Pilate page. Josephus describes clashes with the Jews over military standards and an aqueduct. He also records that Pilate was sent to Rome after attacking a Samaritan crowd, having governed for ten years Josephus 18 page, 18.3.1–2, 18.4.1–2. Today no historian doubts that he existed or that he governed Judea; the inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 settled any question about his title Pilate pageA16. → The Pilate inscription · Jesus before Pilate

Is the James ossuary genuine?

Unproven. The box itself is a genuine ancient Jewish bone box from first-century Jerusalem. The question is its inscription, “James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus”, and above all the words “brother of Jesus” James ossuary page. In 2003 an Israel Antiquities Authority committee concluded that parts of the inscription were modern. The collector was tried for forgery, but in 2012 he was acquitted of forgery because the case was not proved, and the verdict did not declare the inscription genuine James ossuary page. The box surfaced in 2002 from a private collection with no record of where it was found, so the question may never be settled James ossuary page. → The James ossuary

The crucifixion and the resurrection

Was Jesus crucified?

Yes, and it is one of the most secure facts we have about Jesus. All four Gospels and Paul report it, as do the Roman historian Tacitus and, on the majority view, Josephus Compare: crucifixionR27R22. Even sceptical scholars accept it: John Dominic Crossan called it “as sure as anything historical can ever be” A64. It also fits what is known of Roman practice, including physical evidence from the Yehohanan burial Dossier 06A18. Most scholars place it in AD 30 or 33 Dossier 01Life study. → The crucifixion compared

What do historians say about the resurrection?

Historians agree that Jesus’s followers proclaimed his resurrection very soon after his death; they disagree over what lay behind that belief Dossier 06. The earliest evidence is a short summary of the faith that Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15: that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Peter, to the Twelve, to more than five hundred people at once, to James and to all the apostles L17. Paul wrote the letter around AD 53–55, roughly twenty years after the crucifixion (which most scholars place in AD 30 or 33), and he says he is handing on what he had himself received, so the summary is older than the letter L17Dossier 06. Scholars such as N. T. Wright argue that an empty tomb and real appearances best explain the evidence A13A14. Others are more sceptical about the empty tomb or explain the experiences in other ways Dossier 06. → The Resurrection, with follow-up questions on that page · The earliest witness list
