The Megiddo mosaic (“God Jesus Christ”)

c. AD 230Christian community at Megiddo (Kefar Othnay)· Published 5 November 2026

A third-century mosaic floor from a Christian prayer hall near Megiddo, found in 2005, with a Greek dedication “to God Jesus Christ”. It is among the earliest archaeological witnesses to Christian worship of Jesus as God.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

The Megiddo mosaic is the floor of a building identified as an early Christian prayer hall. It was exposed in 2005 during construction of a new wing at Megiddo prison, near ancient Megiddo in the Jezreel Valley of northern Israel, in an area that in the Roman period lay beside a legionary camp.

The mosaic carries Greek inscriptions, including a dedication of a table “to God Jesus Christ” (Theō Iēsou Christō). Others record a Roman officer named Gaianus who paid for the work and name women who were benefactors. Fish, an early Christian symbol, appear in the floor’s decoration.

The excavators date the floor by its pottery, coins and style to about AD 230, long before Constantine; some scholars have argued for a somewhat later date. On either date it is one of the earliest archaeological witnesses to Christians addressing Jesus as God, and to a community able to commission mosaic work.

It is evidence for the history of Christian worship and belief about Christ, not for the events of Jesus’s own life. The floor was lifted and conserved, and was shown at the Museum of the Bible in Washington in 2024-2025 under an agreement with the Israel Antiquities Authority.

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The find

In 2005, while a new wing was being built at Megiddo prison, excavation exposed a mosaic floor in a building identified as an early Christian prayer hall.

The inscriptions

The floor carries Greek inscriptions. One dedicates a table to Christ with the words:

Θεῷ Ἰησοῦ Χριστῷ: “to God Jesus Christ”

Beside it are a dedication by a Roman officer named Gaianus and the names of women who funded the work. Fish, an early Christian symbol, appear in the decoration.

Date

The excavators date the mosaic by its pottery, coins and style to about AD 230, before Constantine. Some scholars have argued for a later date.

What it establishes

A Christian meeting place with an explicitly christological inscription in the third century, and a community in the Jezreel Valley with sufficient means to commission mosaic work. The inscription addresses Christ as God, which is a datum for the history of Christology.

Open questions

The floor was laid about two centuries after Jesus’s ministry, so it does not speak to the events of his life. What it records, in its own words, is a third-century community’s worship of Jesus Christ as God.

Where it is now

The floor was lifted and conserved. It was shown at the Museum of the Bible in Washington in 2024-2025 under an agreement with the Israel Antiquities Authority.
