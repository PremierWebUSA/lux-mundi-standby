The James ossuary (disputed)

1st century AD (claimed); surfaced 2002Unknown

A limestone bone box inscribed “James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus”, announced in 2002 without an excavation record. Experts still debate whether the whole inscription is ancient; its owner’s 2012 acquittal left that question to the evidence.

About this artifact

The James ossuary is a limestone bone box of a type used in Jerusalem in the first century. It bears an Aramaic inscription read as Ya’akov bar Yosef akhui diYeshua, “James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus”. It was presented to the public in 2002. It came from a private collection and has no recorded findspot or excavation context.

If the whole inscription were ancient and referred to James of Jerusalem, it would be the earliest object to mention Jesus. Naming a brother on an ossuary is rare, which is why the phrase “brother of Jesus” is the crux of the debate.

A committee of the Israel Antiquities Authority reported in 2003 that parts of the inscription, and its patina, had been added in modern times. The collector, Oded Golan, was tried in Jerusalem for forgery. In 2012 the court acquitted him because the prosecution had not proved its case beyond reasonable doubt; the verdict ruled on the charge, not on the inscription, leaving authenticity to the evidence itself.

Specialists remain divided, and later patina and isotope studies have been read both ways. Because the object is unprovenanced, the normal archaeological tests cannot be applied. Whether the phrase “brother of Jesus” is ancient and names James of Jerusalem is therefore an open question, and the evidence is set out here for your own discernment.

The text

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

A limestone ossuary (a box for the secondary burial of bones) of a first-century type. It was presented to the public in 2002. It came from a private collection and had no recorded excavation context.

The inscription

The box bears an Aramaic inscription:

Ya’akov bar Yosef akhui diYeshua: “James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus”

The phrase “brother of Jesus” is the crux. Naming a brother on an ossuary is rare, which is exactly why the inscription would matter if genuine, and why it attracted forgery allegations if not.

The authenticity dispute

The Israel Antiquities Authority convened a committee, which reported in 2003. It held that parts of the inscription, and the patina, had been added in modern times. The collector, Oded Golan, was tried in Jerusalem; in 2012 the court acquitted him of the forgery charges, finding that the prosecution had not proved the case beyond reasonable doubt.

That verdict is not a finding of authenticity, and specialists remain divided. Later patina and isotope studies have been read both ways.

What it establishes

That a first-century-style ossuary with this inscription exists, and that the legal case against the collector failed.

Open questions

Two questions remain open: whether the inscription is ancient as it stands, and whether the James it names is James of Jerusalem. Because the object is unprovenanced, the normal archaeological tests cannot be applied, and the 2012 acquittal ruled on a criminal charge rather than on authenticity.

Where it is now

The ossuary is privately owned and is not in a public museum collection.
