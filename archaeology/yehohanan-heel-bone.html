The heel bone of Yehohanan (Giv’at HaMivtar)

1st century AD; found 1968· Published 5 November 2026

The right heel bone of a young Jewish man named Yehohanan, still pierced by the iron nail that fixed him to a cross. Found in a family tomb in Jerusalem in 1968, it is the only skeletal evidence of crucifixion from Roman Judea.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

In 1968 builders at Giv’at HaMivtar, a neighbourhood in north-east Jerusalem, broke into a group of Jewish rock-cut tombs of the Second Temple period. The archaeologist Vassilios Tzaferis excavated them for Israel’s Department of Antiquities. One stone ossuary, a box used to gather the bones of the dead about a year after burial, bore the name Yehohanan son of Hagakol.

Among Yehohanan’s bones was his right heel, still transfixed by an iron nail about 11.5 cm long. The point of the nail had bent, probably on striking a knot in the upright, so it could not be drawn out when the body was taken down. Fragments of wood between the nail head and the bone come from a small plaque that held the foot in place. He was a man in his twenties.

Crucifixion was the Roman punishment for slaves, rebels and provincials, and Josephus reports thousands of crucifixions around Jerusalem. Yet nails were usually pulled out and reused, and the bodies of the crucified were often left unburied, so the method has left almost no trace in the ground. This heel is the only skeletal evidence of crucifixion from Roman Judea. A second example, from Fenstanton in England, was published in 2021.

The find also bears on the Gospel account of Jesus’ burial. A man executed by crucifixion in first-century Jerusalem was taken down, laid in a family tomb and later gathered into an ossuary with his name on it. Burial of the crucified was possible under Roman rule, as the Gospels say happened when Joseph of Arimathaea, a member of the Jewish council, asked Pilate for the body of Jesus.

The text

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

The right heel bone (calcaneus) of an adult man, pierced from the side by an iron nail whose tip is bent back. Remains of wood survive between the nail head and the bone.

The inscription

The ossuary that held the bones is inscribed with the dead man’s name:

יהוחנן בן חגקול: “Yehohanan son of Hagakol”

The second name is not a known personal name, and its reading and meaning are debated. The first, Yehohanan, is the Hebrew form of John.

Discovery

The tomb was found during building work at Giv’at HaMivtar in 1968 and excavated by Vassilios Tzaferis. His report and the first anatomical study, by Nicu Haas, appeared in the Israel Exploration Journal in 1970.

How he died

Haas proposed that both heels were fixed with a single nail and that the man’s legs had been broken. A re-examination by Joseph Zias and Eliezer Sekeles, published in 1985, found that the nail passed through the right heel alone, from the side, and that there was no clear evidence of broken legs or of nails through the arms. They concluded that his arms were probably tied to the crossbeam and that each foot was nailed to the side of the upright. Their reading is the one generally followed today.

The Gospels

The Gospels describe the breaking of legs to hasten death (John 19:31–33) and the marks of nails in the risen Jesus’ hands:

“Unless I see in his hands the print of the nails, put my finger into the print of the nails, and put my hand into his side, I will not believe.”

They also describe Joseph of Arimathaea receiving the body from Pilate and laying it in a rock-cut tomb (John 19:38–42). Yehohanan’s tomb is an example of the same practice: a crucified man buried by his own people in a family tomb near Jerusalem.

What it establishes

That crucifixion with nails was practised in Roman Judea in the first century, how one victim’s feet were fixed, and that a crucified man could receive a normal Jewish burial.

Open questions

Yehohanan’s crime and the date of his death within the first century are unknown, and a single skeleton cannot show that every crucifixion followed the same method. The heel speaks to the practice, not to any particular execution.

Where it is now

Israel Museum, Jerusalem.
