The Magdala Stone (Galilee)

1st century AD; found 2009

A carved stone block found in 2009 at the centre of a first-century synagogue at Magdala, on the Sea of Galilee. Its seven-branched menorah, carved while the Temple still stood, makes it one of the earliest Jewish images of the Temple lampstand, in a synagogue from the town of Mary Magdalene.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

Magdala was a prosperous fishing town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, a few miles north of Tiberias. Its name comes from the Aramaic and Hebrew word for a tower, and it is the home town implied by the name of Mary Magdalene, one of the women who followed Jesus and among the first witnesses of the empty tomb in the Gospels.

In 2009 the Israel Antiquities Authority excavated the site ahead of building work and found a synagogue of the first century AD, one of the few known from the time of Jesus. Pottery and coins date it to the early Roman period, and it went out of use around the Jewish revolt of AD 66–70. In the centre of its main hall stood a block of limestone carved on every side.

On the front is a seven-branched menorah standing on a base, flanked by two jars. The top bears a large rosette, and the sides and back are carved with arches, columns and other motifs. Many scholars, among them the art historian Rina Talgam, read the whole as a symbolic picture of the Jerusalem Temple. Since the stone was carved while the Temple still stood, its menorah may be drawn from the lampstand itself.

The Gospels say that Jesus taught in the synagogues of Galilee. The Magdala synagogue is such a building, from his lifetime and in a town on the shore where much of his ministry took place. Its benches, mosaic floor and carved stone show the setting in which a Galilean teacher would have read and expounded the Scriptures.

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

A limestone block, carved on its top and all four sides, found in the centre of the main hall of the first-century synagogue at Magdala. Its front shows a seven-branched menorah on a base between two jars.

Discovery

The synagogue was found in 2009 when the Israel Antiquities Authority excavated the site before construction of a pilgrim centre. The excavation was directed by Dina Avshalom-Gorni and Arfan Najar, and further seasons have since uncovered much of the surrounding town.

The carving

Besides the menorah, the stone bears a rosette on its top, arches and columns on its sides, and on its back a design that some read as wheels of fire. The art historian Rina Talgam has argued that the carving represents the Temple in Jerusalem, from the outer courts to the Holy of Holies. Its exact purpose is unknown; one suggestion is that it served as a table on which scrolls were laid for reading.

The Gospels

The Gospels describe Jesus teaching in the synagogues of Galilee:

Jesus went about in all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the Good News of the Kingdom, and healing every disease and every sickness among the people.

Among the women who travelled with him, Luke names one from this town:

and certain women who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities: Mary who was called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out;

What it establishes

That a synagogue with a richly carved stone stood at Magdala in Jesus’ lifetime, that Galilean Jews of his day held the Temple at the centre of their worship, and what the menorah looked like to someone who could have seen it.

Open questions

The stone’s function and the meaning of several of its motifs are debated. No record survives of who taught in this synagogue, and the site shows the kind of building in which Jesus taught, not a particular visit.

Where it is now

A replica stands in the excavated synagogue at Magdala; the original is held by the Israel Antiquities Authority.
