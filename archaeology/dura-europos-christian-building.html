The Christian building at Dura-Europos

c. AD 235–256Christian community of Dura-Europos· Published 5 November 2026

A private house on the Euphrates remodelled for Christian worship, with a baptistery painted with scenes of Christ healing and the women at the tomb. Sealed by the city’s fall around 256, it is a fixed anchor for early Christian art.

Read the text
About this artifact

Dura-Europos was a Roman frontier city on the Euphrates, in what is now eastern Syria. In the 1920s and 1930s the Yale–French Academy excavations uncovered there a private house that had been remodelled for Christian use, with one room adapted as a baptistery.

The baptistery walls carry some of the earliest surviving Christian paintings: the good shepherd, the healing of the paralytic, the women coming to the tomb, and Adam and Eve, among other scenes. The building was destroyed when the city fell to the Sasanian Persians around 256/AD 257, which fixes its Christian phase to roughly 235-256.

The house shows a Christian community worshipping in a purpose-adapted domestic building, and using figurative art, generations before Constantine. The paintings were removed and are now in the Yale University Art Gallery. They are images of Jesus made two centuries after his lifetime; rather than new facts about his life, they record a community’s faith and practice.

The building

At Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, excavators in the 1920s and 1930s uncovered a private house that had been remodelled for Christian use. One room was adapted as a baptistery.

The paintings

The baptistery walls carry some of the earliest surviving painted Christian images: the good shepherd, the healing of the paralytic, the women at the tomb, and Adam and Eve. Graffiti and inscriptions at the site include the name “Christ”, and Old Testament scenes appear to be used typologically.

Date

The building was destroyed when the city fell to the Sasanians around 256/AD 257. Its dating rests on the city’s destruction layer and on coins and inscriptions from the occupation, not on the paintings themselves.

What it establishes

Christian worship in a purpose-modified domestic building in the window 235-256, with figurative art well before the Constantinian turn. It is a fixed chronological anchor for early Christian iconography and architecture.

Open questions

The community’s gospel texts have not survived at the site, so the building cannot tell us exactly what they read, and its paintings were made long after Jesus’s lifetime rather than from life. What they preserve is how a third-century church pictured him: as shepherd and healer, and in the women’s visit to his tomb.

Where it is now

The wall paintings were removed and are in the Yale University Art Gallery, which holds the Dura-Europos collection from the Yale–French Academy excavations.
