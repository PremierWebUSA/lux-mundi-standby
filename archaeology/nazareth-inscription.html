The Nazareth inscription

Early 1st century AD (letter forms)Unnamed Roman emperor (edict)· Revised 18 November 2026

A Greek imperial edict on marble forbidding the disturbance of tombs on pain of death, recorded as “sent from Nazareth” in 1878. A 2020 isotope study matched the marble to Kos; where the edict was first set up, and whether it bears on Jesus’s empty tomb, remain open questions.

About this artifact

The Nazareth inscription is a marble slab with a 22-line Greek text headed “Edict of Caesar”. An unnamed emperor orders that tombs and grave goods must not be disturbed and threatens capital punishment for violation. Its letter forms are dated to the early first century AD.

The stone was acquired by the collector Wilhelm Fröhner, whose note records it as “a marble slab sent from Nazareth in 1878”. That note says where it was sent from, not where it was found. It has been in the Bibliothèque nationale de France since 1925 and was published by Franz Cumont in 1930.

Because of its label, its date and its unusually harsh penalty, the edict has often been connected with the empty tomb of Jesus and the rumour, reported by Matthew, that the disciples stole the body (Matthew 28:11–15). No direct link has been shown. In 2020 Kyle Harper and colleagues drilled two small samples from the back of the slab and measured their carbon and oxygen isotopes; of the quarries previously studied, the closest match was the island of Kos. They proposed that the edict answered the desecration of the tomb of Nikias, a ruler of Kos, under Augustus, a suggestion Harper himself called the best explanation available rather than a certainty.

What the study really tells us is where the marble was dug out of the ground, not where the edict was carved or put on display. That difference matters: Judea and Galilee had no marble of their own, so every marble inscription there was shipped in from somewhere else. The result fits either story, and the rest of the evidence lines up like this:

  • Pointing to Galilee: the 1878 note says the stone was sent from Nazareth; its lettering dates it to the first century, and Galilee came under direct Roman rule in AD 44; and its penalty is far harsher than usual, as explained below.
  • Pointing to Kos: Kos was a small quarry, not a major exporter, so a slab of its marble is more likely to have stayed on the island; nobody knows where the stone was actually found, because it came through an antiquities dealer; and Nazareth was a tiny village, not the kind of place where emperors posted their edicts.
  • Still open: specialists want further tests on the marble, and the link to Nikias has been questioned, since Nikias had backed Augustus’s enemy, Mark Antony.

The penalty is the strangest thing about the stone. In Roman law of the time a violated tomb was a matter for a civil suit, the “action for a violated tomb”, which ended in a fine (Digest 47.12), and hundreds of epitaphs from the Greek East name the fine due from anyone who disturbs the grave. Death for removing bodies appears only around AD 200 or later, in the writings of the jurist Paul, who reserves it for people of low rank. An early first-century edict threatening death runs ahead of the known pattern by roughly two centuries, which is why scholars assume that something specific provoked it. There is good reason to think so:

  • Edicts answered events. Emperors governed mostly by responding to petitions and incidents, not by issuing codes. A harsh edict implies a specific outrage that local fines had not deterred.
  • There was no single criminal code. Provinces and Greek cities ran on their own local rules. An imperial edict lifted the offence from a local fine to a crime against Caesar’s own order.
  • Its wording is pointed. Besides breaking into tombs, it singles out anyone who has “transferred” bodies “with wicked intent to other places”. That fits a case in which a body went missing, not ordinary looting for grave goods.

That last clause sits closer to the charge Matthew records than the scattering of Nikias’s bones does. The chief priests asked Pilate to seal the tomb in case the disciples should “come at night and steal him away” (Matthew 27:62–66), and afterwards paid the guards to say that this is exactly what happened (Matthew 28:11–15). Neither case fits the edict perfectly, and the edict names no event.

Kos marble is probable; a Kos setting is plausible; a tie to the empty tomb is neither proven nor ruled out. Whatever its origin, the edict shows that in the century of the crucifixion Roman authority treated disturbing a tomb or removing a body as a capital crime. That is why the guarded tomb in Matthew, and the charge that the disciples had stolen the body, would have been taken with deadly seriousness. If the slab did stand in Galilee, it may even be an imperial echo of that rumour; if not, it still shows the legal world in which the Gospel account sits.

The text

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

A marble slab bearing a 22-line Greek edict of an unnamed emperor. It orders that tombs and grave goods must not be disturbed, and threatens capital punishment for violation. Its date is assigned by letter forms to the early first century AD.

The inscription

The text opens:

Διάταγμα Καίσαρος: “Edict of Caesar”

Provenance

The stone’s history is the reason it is discussed at all. Wilhelm Fröhner acquired it and recorded it as “a marble slab sent from Nazareth in 1878”. That note says where it was sent from, not where it was found. It has been in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, in the Cabinet des Médailles, since 1925, and Franz Cumont published it in 1930.

The 2020 isotope study

A study by Kyle Harper and colleagues in the Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports 30 (2020), article 102228, analysed isotopes in the marble and found that the marble most closely matches a quarry on Kos. That shows where the stone was probably quarried, not where the edict was carved or displayed: Judea had no marble of its own, so any marble inscription there was imported. The study’s proposed link to the tomb of Nikias on Kos is a hypothesis, and whether the stone ever stood in Galilee remains open.

What it establishes

Roman imperial interest in punishing tomb robbery, an offence relevant to how burial and sealed tombs were policed.

Open questions

Was the stone ever in Galilee, and does it say anything about Jesus’s tomb? Its label records only where it was sent from, the isotope evidence points to Kos, and no direct link to Christianity has been shown. Its severe penalty, far ahead of the fines usual in its day, suggests a reaction to a particular scandal.

Where it is now

Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris (Cabinet des Médailles).
