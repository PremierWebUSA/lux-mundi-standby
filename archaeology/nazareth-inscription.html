The Nazareth inscription

Early 1st century AD (letter forms)Unnamed Roman emperor (edict)· Revised 12 November 2026

A Greek imperial edict on marble forbidding the disturbance of tombs, once linked to Jesus’s empty tomb because it was “sent from Nazareth” in 1878. Isotope analysis (2020) points to marble from Kos; it remains a vivid witness to Roman law on tomb robbery.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

The Nazareth inscription is a marble slab with a 22-line Greek text headed “Edict of Caesar”. An unnamed emperor orders that tombs and grave goods must not be disturbed and threatens capital punishment for violation. Its letter forms are dated to the early first century AD.

The stone was acquired by the collector Wilhelm Fröhner, whose note records it as “a marble slab sent from Nazareth in 1878”. That note says where it was sent from, not where it was found. It has been in the Bibliothèque nationale de France since 1925 and was published by Franz Cumont in 1930.

Because of its label and its subject, the edict has sometimes been connected with Christian claims about Jesus’s empty tomb, though no direct link has been shown. In 2020 Kyle Harper and colleagues analysed the marble’s isotopes and argued that it most likely came from the island of Kos, not from Judea.

If that holds, it is a Roman edict about tomb violation with no tie to Nazareth or to the resurrection narratives. Its value is real all the same: it shows how seriously Roman authorities treated tomb robbery, and how careful testing can settle where an object came from.

The object

A marble slab bearing a 22-line Greek edict of an unnamed emperor. It orders that tombs and grave goods must not be disturbed, and threatens capital punishment for violation. Its date is assigned by letter forms to the early first century AD.

The inscription

The text opens:

Διάταγμα Καίσαρος: “Edict of Caesar”

Provenance

The stone’s history is the reason it is discussed at all. Wilhelm Fröhner acquired it and recorded it as “a marble slab sent from Nazareth in 1878”. That note says where it was sent from, not where it was found. It has been in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, in the Cabinet des Médailles, since 1925, and Franz Cumont published it in 1930.

The 2020 isotope study

A study by Kyle Harper and colleagues in the Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports 30 (2020), article 102228, analysed isotopes in the marble and argued that the stone most likely came from a quarry on Kos, not from Judea. If that holds, the object is a Roman imperial edict about tomb violation that has nothing to do with Nazareth or the empty-tomb narratives. Its association with Nazareth would then reflect the shipping history of a Paris collection rather than a findspot. Marble origin alone does not, however, settle where the stone was originally displayed.

What it establishes

Roman imperial interest in punishing tomb robbery, an offence relevant to how burial and sealed tombs were policed.

Open questions

Was the stone ever in Galilee, and does it say anything about Jesus’s tomb? Its label records only where it was sent from, the isotope evidence points to Kos, and no direct link to Christianity has been shown. The dossier presents it as an instructive example of how an object’s origin is tested, set out here for your own discernment.

Where it is now

Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris (Cabinet des Médailles).
