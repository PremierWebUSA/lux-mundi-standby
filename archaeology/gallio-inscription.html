The Gallio inscription (Delphi)

AD 52; published 1905Emperor Claudius (letter)· Published 5 November 2026

Fragments of a letter from the emperor Claudius, carved on stone at Delphi in Greece, naming Gallio as proconsul of Achaia. Dated by Claudius’s titles to AD 52, it fixes Paul’s stay in Corinth, and with it the framework of Paul’s life, to within a year or two.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

The Gallio inscription is a set of inscribed stone fragments from Delphi, the sanctuary of Apollo in central Greece. They carry a letter in Greek from the emperor Claudius about the city of Delphi, which mentions “Junius Gallio, my friend and proconsul”. The first four fragments were published by the French scholar Émile Bourguet in 1905, and more have been joined to them since.

Gallio was the elder brother of the philosopher Seneca, adopted into the family of Lucius Junius Gallio. The letter gives Claudius’s titles, including his twenty-sixth acclamation as imperator, a title he received in the first half of AD 52. Proconsuls of a senatorial province such as Achaia, which covered southern Greece, usually held office for a year from early summer, so Gallio’s year was most probably AD 51–52.

Acts relates that when Gallio was proconsul of Achaia, the Jews of Corinth brought Paul before his judgment seat, and Gallio dismissed the case as a dispute about words and names. Paul had arrived in Corinth after Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome, and he stayed there eighteen months. The inscription allows that stay to be dated to about AD 50–52.

No other event in Paul’s life can be dated so closely, and it serves as the anchor for the chronology of his journeys and letters. Working forward and back from Corinth, historians place his conversion in the early to middle 30s and his first surviving letter, 1 Thessalonians, written from Corinth, around AD 50.

The text

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

Nine fragments of a stone slab from the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi, inscribed in Greek with a letter of the emperor Claudius concerning the city.

The inscription

The key line names the governor of the province. Square brackets mark letters restored by editors:

[Lucius Jun]ius Gallio, my [friend] and [pro]consul

Discovery

The fragments were found in the French excavations at Delphi. Émile Bourguet published four of them in 1905, and André Plassart published the fuller set of nine in 1970.

The date

The letter names Claudius as acclaimed imperator for the twenty-sixth time. Other inscriptions show that he received this acclamation in the first half of AD 52. Gallio was therefore in office in AD 52, most probably for the year beginning in the early summer of 51.

Paul in Corinth

Acts places Paul in Corinth for eighteen months (Acts 18:11), during which he was accused before Gallio:

But when Gallio was proconsul of Achaia, the Jews with one accord rose up against Paul and brought him before the judgment seat,

Gallio refused to judge “questions about words and names” and drove the accusers from the court (Acts 18:14–16). Paul had met Aquila and Priscilla in Corinth, lately come from Italy because Claudius had ordered the Jews to leave Rome (Acts 18:2), an expulsion that Suetonius also records.

What it establishes

That Gallio, brother of Seneca, was proconsul of Achaia around AD 51–52, as Acts says. It gives a fixed point from which the rest of Paul’s career is dated.

Open questions

Some scholars place Gallio’s year in 52–53 rather than 51–52, and the stone does not say when in his term Paul appeared before him. The difference shifts Paul’s chronology by about a year.

Where it is now

Delphi Archaeological Museum, Greece.
