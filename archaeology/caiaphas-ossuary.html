The Caiaphas ossuary

1st century AD; found 1990Unknown (Jerusalem family tomb)· Published 5 November 2026

An ornate limestone bone box from a first-century Jerusalem family tomb, inscribed “Joseph son of Caiaphas”. Many scholars hold that it belonged to the family of the high priest Caiaphas who, in the Gospels, questioned Jesus.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

The Caiaphas ossuary is an ornately carved limestone bone box. Jews in and around Jerusalem in the late Second Temple period gathered the bones of the dead into such boxes about a year after burial. This one was recovered in 1990 from a burial cave in the Peace Forest, south of Jerusalem’s Old City, uncovered during construction work.

It carries two Aramaic inscriptions. One reads Yehosef bar Qayafa, “Joseph son of Caiaphas”; the other, more roughly cut, reads Qafa. The Israel Antiquities Authority published the find, and the archaeological report dates the use of the family tomb to the first century. The ossuary is now in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem.

The name matters because the Gospels call the high priest who questioned Jesus Caiaphas (Matthew 26:3, 57; John 11:49; 18:13-14), and Josephus names a “Joseph, who was called Caiaphas” as high priest from about 18 to AD 36. The box shows a family of that name and of some means using a Jerusalem tomb in the right period.

The inscription names a family rather than a single man, and it does not speak of Jesus’s trial. Even so, many scholars hold that the ossuary belonged to the high priest’s family, and many believe it held the bones of the very man named in the Gospels.

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

An ornate limestone ossuary, a box for the secondary burial of bones, decorated with carved rosettes on its front. It was one of several ossuaries in a burial cave in the Peace Forest, south of Jerusalem’s Old City, found in 1990 during construction work. The cave was a family tomb, and the archaeological report dates its use to the first century.

The inscriptions

The ossuary carries two Aramaic inscriptions. The fuller one reads:

Yehosef bar Qayafa: “Joseph son of Caiaphas”

A second, more roughly incised inscription reads Qafa.

Why it matters

The Gospels name the high priest who interrogated Jesus as Caiaphas (Matthew 26:3, 57; John 11:49; 18:13-14). Josephus independently names a “Joseph, who was called Caiaphas” as high priest from about 18 to AD 36. The ossuary attests a family of that name, wealthy enough for a decorated box, in a Jerusalem tomb of the same period.

What it establishes

A family of high-priestly rank used the name Caiaphas in Jerusalem in roughly the period the Gospels assign to the high priest of Jesus’s trial. The name was already independently attested by the Gospels and Josephus; the box gives it a physical, archaeological context.

Open questions

The inscription names a family of Caiaphas rather than a particular man, and it says nothing of the trial narratives. Whether these are the bones of the high priest himself is a question for your own discernment; the identification is widely reported, and many scholars find it persuasive.

Where it is now

The Israel Antiquities Authority published the find, and the ossuary is displayed in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem.
