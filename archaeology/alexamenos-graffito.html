The Alexamenos graffito (Palatine, Rome)

c. AD 200; found 1857

A crude drawing scratched into plaster on the Palatine Hill in Rome: a man raises his hand to a crucified figure with a donkey’s head, above the Greek words “Alexamenos worships his god”. It is a pagan mockery of a Christian, and one of the earliest surviving pictures of the crucifixion.

What it says of ChristRead the text
About this artifact

The Alexamenos graffito is a drawing scratched into the plaster wall of a room on the Palatine Hill in Rome, the hill of the emperors’ palaces. It was found in 1857 in a building usually called the Paedagogium, on the slope above the Circus Maximus,, which is thought to have housed the young slaves trained for service in the imperial household.

The drawing shows a figure with a man’s body and a donkey’s head, hanging on a cross and seen from behind. To the left a young man stands with one hand raised in a gesture of worship. Beneath runs a line of Greek in uneven capitals: “Alexamenos worships his god”. It is usually dated to around AD 200.

The joke is aimed at a Christian. Pagan critics claimed that Christians worshipped a donkey’s head, a slander that the Christian writers Tertullian of Carthage and Minucius Felix both report and answer around the same time. To the mocker, a god who died on a cross, the death of slaves, was absurd. A few scholars have proposed that the target was some other cult, but most read it as an insult to a Christian named Alexamenos.

The scratch tells the reader two things at once. Within the emperor’s own palace there was a Christian whom others knew well enough to mock by name, and the crucifixion was at the heart of what outsiders knew about his faith. Paul had written as much to the Corinthians a century and a half earlier.

The text

An account of the object written for this site from the archive’s source record and the research dossier.

The object

A section of wall plaster with a scratched drawing: a donkey-headed figure on a cross, a young man raising his hand towards it, and a line of Greek beneath.

The inscription

The Greek reads as follows. The verb is spelled the way it was pronounced, a common feature of informal writing:

ΑΛΕΞΑΜΕΝΟΣ ΣΕΒΕΤΕ ΘΕΟΝ: “Alexamenos worships his god”

Discovery

The plaster was found in 1857 in the Paedagogium on the Palatine Hill. Many other graffiti were scratched on the walls of the same building. One, in Latin, reads “Alexamenos fidelis”, “Alexamenos is faithful”, and may be a reply by Alexamenos or a friend.

The donkey slander

Tertullian, writing in Carthage around AD 197, says that some people imagined the Christian God to have a donkey’s head, and he traces the rumour to an older slander against the Jews. Minucius Felix, a Christian writer in Rome, puts the same charge in the mouth of a pagan critic. The graffito shows that this was not just a literary charge but a joke current among ordinary people in Rome.

The cross

Christian art of the second and third centuries rarely depicted the crucifixion; the Good Shepherd and other symbols were preferred. Here, one of the earliest surviving pictures of Jesus on the cross was made by an opponent, who saw the cross as the most ridiculous thing about Christianity:

“… we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Greeks …”

What it establishes

That by about AD 200 there were Christians among the household staff on the Palatine, that outsiders identified them by the worship of a crucified god, and that the donkey slander reported by Christian writers was in popular circulation.

Open questions

The date rests on the building’s history and the style of the writing and cannot be fixed precisely. Nothing more is known of Alexamenos than these two scratched lines.

Where it is now

Palatine Museum, Rome.
