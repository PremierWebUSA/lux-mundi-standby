The Timeline

Every witness in this archive, placed in the order it was written or made: prophecy and Gospel, historian and Father, stone and scroll.

Before his birth

The prophets, and the scrolls that preserved them

c. 750–725 BCHosea: out of Egypt I called my son

A prophet of the northern kingdom in the eighth century BC. Chapter 11 remembers God calling Israel, his son, out of Egypt, a line Matthew applies to the return of the child Jesus.

The Prophets
c. 735–700 BCMicah: the ruler from Bethlehem

A prophet of Judah in the late eighth century BC. Chapter 5 looks for a ruler of Israel from Bethlehem, David’s town, whose origins are from of old.

The Prophets
c. 1000–400 BCPsalms: the songs read of the Messiah

Eight of Israel’s psalms that the New Testament reads as speaking of Jesus: royal songs of the king God enthrones, and laments of a righteous sufferer, including Psalm 22, whose opening words Jesus cried from the cross.

The Prophets
c. 740–500 BCIsaiah: the chapters read as prophecy

The longest of the prophets, from Jerusalem in the eighth century BC. These chapters, from the sign of Immanuel to the suffering servant of chapter 53, are the ones the New Testament draws on most to explain who Jesus was.

The Prophets
c. 7th–6th century BC2 Samuel: the promise to David

The story of David’s reign, c. 1000 BC. In chapter 7 God promises David, through the prophet Nathan, a son and a throne established forever, the root of Israel’s hope for a messiah from David’s line.

The Prophets
c. 627–580 BCJeremiah: Rachel’s weeping and the new covenant

The prophet of Jerusalem’s last years before the Babylonian conquest. Chapter 31 holds Rachel weeping for her children and the promise of a new covenant written on the heart.

The Prophets
c. 8th–5th century BCJonah: three days in the fish

The story of a prophet who flees God’s call and is swallowed by a great fish for three days and three nights, the sign Jesus gave of his own death and rising.

The Prophets
6th century BC setting; c. 165 BC on the critical viewDaniel: the vision of the Son of Man

Visions of the empires and of God’s coming kingdom. In chapter 7 one like a son of man comes with the clouds to receive an everlasting dominion, the figure Jesus named himself by.

The Prophets
Final form c. 6th–5th century BCGenesis: the call of Abraham

The first book of the Torah. In chapter 12 God calls Abraham and promises that through him all the families of the earth will be blessed, a promise Paul read as the gospel announced in advance.

The Prophets
Final form c. 6th–5th century BCExodus: the first Passover

The second book of the Torah. Chapter 12 institutes the Passover, with its unblemished lamb whose blood marks the doors and whose bones are not to be broken, a pattern the New Testament sees in the death of Jesus.

The Prophets
Final form c. 6th–5th century BCNumbers: the bronze serpent and the star

The fourth book of the Torah, following Israel through the wilderness. Chapter 21 tells of the bronze serpent lifted on a pole, and chapter 24 of Balaam’s oracle of a star rising out of Jacob.

The Prophets
Date disputed, c. 9th–4th century BCJoel: the Spirit poured out

A prophet of the day of Yahweh. Chapter 2 promises that God will pour out his Spirit on all flesh, the passage Peter quoted at Pentecost.

The Prophets
c. 520–450 BCZechariah: the humble king and the stricken shepherd

A prophet of the return from exile. These chapters, of the king riding on a donkey, the thirty pieces of silver, the one who was pierced and the shepherd struck, shape the Gospels’ account of Jesus’s last days.

The Prophets
2nd century BC (manuscript)The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ)

The only complete biblical book among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied in the second century BC. It shows that Isaiah, a book Christians read as foretelling Jesus, existed in substantially its known form well before Christianity.

Stones and Scrolls
Built 1st century BC; found 2004The Pool of Siloam (Jerusalem)

A large stepped pool at the southern end of ancient Jerusalem, discovered by chance in 2004. Built in the first century BC and used until the Roman destruction of AD 70, it is the pool of Siloam where, in John’s Gospel, Jesus sent a man born blind to wash.

The apostolic age

The lifetime of those who knew him

Early 1st century AD (letter forms)The Nazareth inscription

A Greek imperial edict on marble forbidding the disturbance of tombs, once linked to Jesus’s empty tomb because it was “sent from Nazareth” in 1878. Isotope analysis (2020) points to marble from Kos; it remains a vivid witness to Roman law on tomb robbery.

Stones and Scrolls
1st century AD; found 2009The Magdala Stone (Galilee)

A carved stone block found in 2009 at the centre of a first-century synagogue at Magdala, on the Sea of Galilee. Its seven-branched menorah, carved while the Temple still stood, makes it one of the earliest Jewish images of the Temple lampstand, in a synagogue from the town of Mary Magdalene.

Stones and Scrolls
Second Temple period; excavated from the 19th centuryThe Pool of Bethesda (Jerusalem)

Two large rock-cut basins north of the Temple Mount, in the grounds of the Church of St Anne in Jerusalem. Their layout, with a dividing wall between them, matches John’s description of a pool “having five porches”, where Jesus healed a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 26–36; found 1961The Pilate inscription (Caesarea Maritima)

A limestone block from Caesarea Maritima with a Latin dedication naming “Pontius Pilate, Prefect of Judea”. It is the only contemporary inscription naming the governor under whom Jesus was crucified.

Stones and Scrolls
1st century AD; found 1990The Caiaphas ossuary

An ornate limestone bone box from a first-century Jerusalem family tomb, inscribed “Joseph son of Caiaphas”. Many scholars hold that it belonged to the family of the high priest Caiaphas who, in the Gospels, questioned Jesus.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 50–51Paul’s First Letter to the Thessalonians

Probably the oldest surviving Christian writing, c. AD 50–51, sent by Paul to the young church at Thessalonica about twenty years after the crucifixion. He takes it as known that Jesus died and rose again, and looks for his return.

The New Testament
1st century AD; found 1968The heel bone of Yehohanan (Giv’at HaMivtar)

The right heel bone of a young Jewish man named Yehohanan, still pierced by the iron nail that fixed him to a cross. Found in a family tomb in Jerusalem in 1968, it is the only skeletal evidence of crucifixion from Roman Judea.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 48–55Paul’s Letter to the Galatians

Paul’s forceful letter to the churches of Galatia, from the late 40s or 50s AD. In it he tells, in the first person, how he stayed with Peter in Jerusalem and met “James, the Lord’s brother”, among the earliest references to Jesus’s family.

The New Testament
AD 52; published 1905The Gallio inscription (Delphi)

Fragments of a letter from the emperor Claudius, carved on stone at Delphi in Greece, naming Gallio as proconsul of Achaia. Dated by Claudius’s titles to AD 52, it fixes Paul’s stay in Corinth, and with it the framework of Paul’s life, to within a year or two.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 53–55Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians

Paul’s letter to the church he founded in Corinth, c. AD 53–55. It preserves the earliest written account of the Lord’s Supper and, in 15:3–8, an older summary of Jesus’s death, burial, resurrection and appearances that Paul says he had “received”.

The New Testament
c. AD 55–56Paul’s Second Letter to the Corinthians

Paul’s second surviving letter to Corinth, c. AD 55–56, written in a season of strain. It defends his apostleship and recalls his escape from Damascus under King Aretas, an episode that helps anchor Paul’s early chronology.

The New Testament
c. AD 55–62Paul’s Letter to Philemon

Paul’s shortest letter: a personal appeal from prison on behalf of Onesimus, an enslaved man who had become a believer, to be received as “a beloved brother”. It is first-hand evidence of an early house church.

The New Testament
c. 40–70, if it existedQ: The Sayings Source (a Scholarly Reconstruction)

Q is a hypothetical sayings source that, on the two-source theory, Matthew and Luke used alongside Mark. No manuscript exists; this is a modern reconstruction of the shared non-Markan material, translated from the Critical Edition of Q.

The Other Gospels
c. AD 55–58Paul’s Letter to the Romans

Paul’s longest letter, written c. AD 55–58 to a church he had not yet visited. Its opening preserves what may be an older confession: Jesus born of David’s line “according to the flesh” and declared Son of God by the resurrection.

The New Testament
c. AD 54–62Paul’s Letter to the Philippians

Paul’s warm letter from prison to his first church in Europe. Its hymn to Christ (2:6–11), possibly older than Paul, shows that within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion Jesus was being confessed as Lord.

The New Testament
1st century AD (claimed); surfaced 2002The James ossuary (disputed)

A limestone bone box inscribed “James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus”, announced in 2002 without an excavation record. Experts still debate whether the whole inscription is ancient; its owner’s 2012 acquittal left that question to the evidence.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 55–62 if by Paul; otherwise c. AD 70–80The Letter to the Colossians

A letter of Paul and Timothy to the church at Colossae, received as Paul’s by Irenaeus and the early church. Its hymn-like passage (1:15–20) calls Christ “the image of the invisible God”, through whom God made peace “through the blood of his cross”.

The New Testament
c. AD 65–75The Gospel of Mark

The shortest Gospel, which the earliest tradition traces to Mark, the interpreter of Peter. Its fast-moving account of the ministry in Galilee and the passion in Jerusalem, c. AD 65–75, gave Matthew and Luke their framework.

The New Testament
c. AD 50–52 if by Paul; otherwise c. AD 80–100The Second Letter to the Thessalonians

Paul’s second letter to Thessalonica, received as his by Irenaeus and the early church. It answers a claim that “the day of Christ has already come”, evidence of early debate over the timing of Jesus’s return.

The New Testament
Date uncertain (proposals range from after AD 73 to the 4th century)The Letter of Mara bar Serapion to his Son

A Syriac letter of consolation from a prisoner to his son. It compares the killing of Socrates, Pythagoras and the Jews’ ‘wise king’. He is not named and the letter’s date is debated, but many readers identify him with Jesus.

Witnesses Outside the Church
c. AD 60–90The Letter to the Hebrews

An anonymous sermon in letter form, c. AD 60–90, which some early churches ascribed to Paul and Origen said only God knows who wrote. It presents Jesus as the Son and heavenly high priest, who prayed “with strong crying and tears”.

The New Testament
before AD 62 if by James; otherwise c. AD 70–100The Letter of James

A letter of moral teaching from “James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ”, traditionally the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church. Many of its sayings echo the Sermon on the Mount.

The New Testament
c. AD 60 if by Paul; otherwise c. AD 80–100The Letter to the Ephesians

A letter of Paul from prison on the unity of Jews and gentiles in Christ, received as his throughout the early church. It presents Christ as the one who reconciles both “in one body to God through the cross”, with the church built on him.

The New Testament
c. AD 80–95The Gospel of Matthew

The Gospel most read in the early church, attributed from the second century to Matthew the tax collector, one of the Twelve. It presents Jesus as Israel’s Messiah and teacher, with the infancy narrative, the Sermon on the Mount and scripture fulfilled.

The New Testament
c. AD 80–100The Gospel of Luke

The first volume of Luke–Acts, attributed from the second century to Luke, the physician and companion of Paul. Its prologue describes its own method, drawing on eyewitness tradition, and it adds the infancy narratives, many parables and the Emmaus appearance.

The New Testament
c. AD 80–90The Acts of the Apostles

The second volume of Luke–Acts, attributed from the second century to Luke, companion of Paul: the earliest narrative of the first Christian communities, from Jerusalem under Peter and James to Paul’s arrival in Rome.

The New Testament
c. AD 75–95 (before c. 65 if by Peter)The First Letter of Peter

A letter from “Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ” to Christians in Asia Minor facing hostility, used by Polycarp and accepted throughout the early church. Its writer calls himself a witness of Christ’s sufferings and reads the cross through Isaiah 53.

The New Testament
c. AD 50–100The Letter of Jude

A short, urgent letter from “Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James”, traditionally one of the brothers of Jesus. Accepted in the Muratorian fragment, it quotes 1 Enoch and was reused in 2 Peter.

The New Testament
c. AD 93–94Josephus, Jewish Antiquities, Book 18: the Testimonium Flavianum and John the Baptist

The Jewish historian Josephus’s passage on Jesus (Ant. 18.63–64), widely judged to be an authentic notice later expanded by Christian copyists, with his account of John the Baptist and Pilate’s governorship as context.

Witnesses Outside the Church
c. AD 93–94Josephus, Jewish Antiquities, Book 20: the death of James, brother of Jesus

Josephus reports that the high priest Ananus had ‘the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James’ stoned in AD 62. Most scholars accept the passage as authentic, making it a key non-Christian reference to Jesus.

Witnesses Outside the Church
c. AD 90–110The Gospel of John

The Fourth Gospel, which Irenaeus and the early church attributed to John the apostle, the “disciple whom Jesus loved”. Largely independent of the other three, it opens with the Word who became flesh and sets the ministry across several Passovers.

The New Testament
c. AD 95 (some prefer c. 68–70)The Revelation to John

A prophetic vision written by John on Patmos to seven churches in Asia, dated by Irenaeus to the end of Domitian’s reign, c. AD 95. It presents Jesus as “the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead” and the Lamb who was slain.

The New Testament
c. AD 96 (traditional date)The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians

A letter from the church at Rome to the church at Corinth, traditionally dated c. 96. It recalls the deaths of Peter and Paul, quotes sayings of Jesus, and says the apostles were sent by Christ and appointed leaders after them.

The second century

The Fathers, the apologists and the first critics

c. AD 90–110 (60s if by Paul)The First Letter to Timothy

One of the Pastoral Letters, addressed in Paul’s name to his co-worker Timothy and quoted as Paul’s by Irenaeus. It contains a short hymn about Christ and refers to Christ Jesus “who before Pontius Pilate testified the good confession”.

The New Testament
c. AD 90–110 (60s if by Paul)The Second Letter to Timothy

Presented as Paul’s last letter, written from prison as he faced death. Its summary of the gospel, “Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, of the offspring of David”, echoes Romans 1:3–4 and shows what was handed on as essential.

The New Testament
c. AD 90–110 (60s if by Paul)The Letter to Titus

The third Pastoral Letter, addressed in Paul’s name to Titus, left in Crete to appoint elders. Mostly about church leadership, it looks for the appearing of “our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ”, a verse much discussed as evidence of belief.

The New Testament
c. AD 90–110The First Letter of John

A treatise from the circle of the beloved disciple, attributed from the second century to John the apostle. Written to a divided community, it proclaims what “our hands touched” of “the Word of life” and insists that Jesus Christ came in the flesh.

The New Testament
c. AD 90–110The Second Letter of John

A brief letter from “the elder” to a congregation, which Irenaeus quotes as the work of John, the Lord’s disciple. It warns against deceivers who do not confess that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, making his real humanity a test of belief.

The New Testament
c. AD 90–110The Third Letter of John

A private letter from “the elder” to Gaius about hospitality to travelling missionaries who went out “for the sake of the Name”. It does not name Jesus, but it opens a window on the life of the early congregations.

The New Testament
Late 1st or early 2nd century ADThe Didache (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles)

A short early church manual: the ‘Two Ways’ of life and death, then instructions on baptism, fasting, the Lord’s Prayer, eucharistic prayers, travelling prophets, Sunday worship and the Lord’s coming. A window on how early communities lived their devotion to Jesus.

The Early Church
c. AD 100–130The Second Letter of Peter

A testament in the name of Simon Peter, appealing to his presence at the transfiguration: “we were eyewitnesses of his majesty”. Slow to win acceptance and listed by Eusebius among the disputed books, it is usually dated c. AD 100–130.

The New Testament
c. AD 110 (debated)Ignatius of Antioch, Letter to the Smyrnaeans

Written by the bishop of Antioch on his way to execution in Rome. He insists that Jesus was truly born, truly nailed to the cross under Pontius Pilate and Herod, and rose in the flesh. The first text to speak of ‘the Catholic Church.’

The Early Church
c. AD 111–112Pliny the Younger and Trajan, Letters 10.96–97: on the trials of Christians

A Roman governor’s report to the emperor on the Christians of Bithynia-Pontus: their dawn meetings, a hymn ‘to Christ, as though he were a god’, their oath and shared meal, and Trajan’s rule that they are not to be hunted out.

Witnesses Outside the Church
c. AD 115–117Tacitus, Annals 15.38–44: the Great Fire and Nero’s punishment of the Christians

The Roman historian’s account of the fire of AD 64, after which Nero blamed and cruelly executed the Christians. Tacitus explains that their founder Christus was executed under Tiberius by the procurator Pontius Pilate.

Witnesses Outside the Church
c. AD 120Suetonius, Life of Claudius 25: the expulsion over “Chrestus”

In a list of Claudius’s measures, Suetonius says he expelled from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting ‘at the instigation of Chrestus’. Many historians see a garbled reference to Christ; others think of an unknown agitator, a question left to your discernment.

Witnesses Outside the Church
c. AD 110–130Papias of Hierapolis, Fragments

Quotations from a lost early-2nd-century work by the bishop of Hierapolis, who sought out what followers of the apostles remembered. They include the earliest statements about how the Gospels of Mark and Matthew were written.

The Early Church
c. AD 124–125Hadrian’s Rescript to Minucius Fundanus (Eusebius, Church History 4.9)

An imperial ruling to the proconsul of Asia: Christians may be prosecuted only by formal accusation in court and convicted of acting against the laws, and false accusers are to be punished. Many historians accept it as genuine; its exact meaning is weighed here.

Witnesses Outside the Church
1st–2nd century, disputed (Greek fragments c. 200–250; Coptic c. 350)The Gospel of Thomas

A collection of 114 sayings of “the living Jesus”, complete in Coptic from Nag Hammadi with earlier Greek fragments from Oxyrhynchus. Many parallel the Synoptics; its date and independence are among the most debated questions in Gospel study.

The Other Gospels
2nd century (papyrus c. 150–200)The Egerton Gospel (Papyrus Egerton 2)

Fragments of an unknown second-century gospel on papyrus: Jesus tells the rulers to search the scriptures, escapes arrest, heals a leper and answers a question about paying kings. Among the oldest surviving non-canonical Gospel texts.

The Other Gospels
2nd century in early form (Greek manuscript 11th c.)The Infancy Gospel of Thomas

Legends of Jesus between five and twelve: clay sparrows that fly, curses and healings, and lessons that confound his teachers. A second-century story cycle that fills the canonical Gospels’ silence, not a record of his childhood.

The Other Gospels
2nd century (Akhmim copy 8th–9th c.)The Gospel of Peter (Akhmim Fragment)

A second-century Passion and resurrection narrative told in Peter’s name, known from an Egyptian manuscript found in 1886–87. It retells the canonical story with new scenes, including giant figures and a speaking cross at the tomb.

The Other Gospels
2nd century (Greek fragments 3rd c.)The Gospel of Mary

A fragmentary second-century dialogue in which the risen Savior teaches and departs, Mary relates a vision of the soul’s ascent, and Peter and Andrew dispute her authority. Evidence for early debates about revelation and leadership.

The Other Gospels
c. 130–170 (Coptic copy c. 280 ± 60)The Gospel of Judas

A second-century revelation dialogue, known to Irenaeus and recovered in Coptic in Codex Tchacos, in which Jesus mocks the disciples’ worship and gives Judas secret teaching before the betrayal. A witness to later debates, not to the events.

The Other Gospels
2nd century (Coptic copy 4th c.)The Secret Book of James (Apocryphon of James)

A second-century letter in James’s name relating secret teaching of the risen Savior to James and Peter before his ascension: be “filled”, seek suffering, believe in the cross. Preserved only at Nag Hammadi.

The Other Gospels
c. AD 150–155Justin Martyr, First Apology

A Christian philosopher’s defence addressed to Antoninus Pius. It says Jesus was crucified under Pontius Pilate, argues from the Hebrew prophets, and gives the earliest full description of baptism, the Eucharist and Sunday worship, where ‘the memoirs of the apostles’ were read.

The Early Church
c. 140–180 (Coptic copies 4th c.)The Gospel of Truth

A second-century Valentinian meditation on how Jesus, “nailed to a tree”, made known the unknown Father and dispelled ignorance. Probably the work Irenaeus called the Gospel of Truth; preserved at Nag Hammadi.

The Other Gospels
c. AD 165Lucian of Samosata, The Passing of Peregrinus

A Greek satirist’s account of the Cynic Peregrinus, who burned himself at Olympia in 165. In passing it describes Christians who worship ‘the crucified sage’ of Palestine, live by his laws and care for their imprisoned members.

Witnesses Outside the Church
2nd century (c. 150–200)The Infancy Gospel of James (Protevangelium of James)

A second-century legend of Mary’s birth, childhood and virginity, ending with Jesus’s birth in a cave near Bethlehem. Not a historical source, but enormously influential: it gave the Church the names Joachim and Anne.

The Other Gospels
c. 170 (Arabic version 11th c.; tr. 1896)Tatian’s Diatessaron

Tatian’s single harmony of Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, made about 170 and long the gospel of the Syriac churches. It shows the four Gospels already treated as a set; this page gives its opening and its Passion and resurrection.

The Other Gospels
Copy 2nd century (c. 150–200); published 2023Sayings of Jesus (Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 5575)

A small second-century papyrus, published in 2023, preserving sayings of Jesus close to Matthew 6, Luke 12 and the Gospel of Thomas: on anxiety, the birds and the lilies, fasting from the world, and seeking the kingdom.

The Other Gospels
c. AD 180Irenaeus of Lyons, Against Heresies, Book I

Irenaeus sets out the ‘Rule of Faith’ the churches had received from the apostles and describes the Gnostic teachers he opposed, from Valentinus to Basilides, Cerinthus and Marcion, and the sect that produced a Gospel of Judas.

The Early Church
c. AD 180Irenaeus of Lyons, Against Heresies, Book III

The classic second-century defence of the four Gospels: how Matthew, Mark, Luke and John came to be written, why there are four and only four, and the succession of bishops from the apostles in Rome and Smyrna.

Persecution and peace

From the house-churches to the councils

c. AD 200; found 1857The Alexamenos graffito (Palatine, Rome)

A crude drawing scratched into plaster on the Palatine Hill in Rome: a man raises his hand to a crucified figure with a donkey’s head, above the Greek words “Alexamenos worships his god”. It is a pagan mockery of a Christian, and one of the earliest surviving pictures of the crucifixion.

Stones and Scrolls
Disputed: c. 200 if authentic; reported 1958The Secret Gospel of Mark (the Mar Saba Letter)

A letter ascribed to Clement of Alexandria, reported by Morton Smith in 1958, that quotes a “more spiritual” expanded Gospel of Mark. The manuscript cannot now be examined, and whether it is ancient or a modern forgery is an open question for your discernment.

The Other Gospels
c. AD 221 (Africanus); Thallus earlier, date disputedJulius Africanus on Thallus and Phlegon: the darkness at the crucifixion

A fragment of Julius Africanus’s lost Chronography. He says Thallus explained the darkness at the crucifixion as an eclipse, objects that no eclipse is possible at Passover full moon, and cites Phlegon’s report of an eclipse under Tiberius.

Witnesses Outside the Church
2nd–3rd century (Codex Tchacos, c. 280 ± 60)The Stranger’s Book (Allogenes, Codex Tchacos)

A damaged Sethian text from Codex Tchacos whose hero, “Stranger”, is tempted by Satan, transfigured in a bright cloud on Mount Tabor and taught to ascend past hostile powers. It reuses Gospel scenes without naming Jesus.

The Other Gospels
Late 2nd–3rd century (Coptic copy c. 350)The Gospel of Philip

A Valentinian anthology of sayings and reflections on baptism, anointing, the Eucharist and the bridal chamber, preserved at Nag Hammadi. Often sensationalised for its lines on Mary Magdalene, it is evidence for early Christian ritual thought.

The Other Gospels
c. AD 225–235Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX

A Roman presbyter attacks teachers of his own day who said the Father and the Son were one and the same, including Bishop Callistus, and the Elchasaites. It shows how sharply Christians in Rome were arguing about who Christ is in the early 3rd century.

The Early Church
c. AD 230The Megiddo mosaic (“God Jesus Christ”)

A third-century mosaic floor from a Christian prayer hall near Megiddo, found in 2005, with a Greek dedication “to God Jesus Christ”. It is among the earliest archaeological witnesses to Christian worship of Jesus as God.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 235–256The Christian building at Dura-Europos

A private house on the Euphrates remodelled for Christian worship, with a baptistery painted with scenes of Christ healing and the women at the tomb. Sealed by the city’s fall around 256, it is a fixed anchor for early Christian art.

Stones and Scrolls
c. AD 248Origen, Against Celsus, Book I

Origen replies to Celsus, a 2nd-century critic who called Jesus the son of a soldier named Panthera and a magician trained in Egypt. Along the way he cites Josephus on ‘James the Just, the brother of Jesus (called Christ).’

The Early Church
c. AD 230–270The Frankfurt silver amulet (Nida)

A rolled silver amulet from a third-century grave at Roman Nida (Frankfurt), read by CT scanning in 2024. Its text invokes Jesus, making it among the earliest Christian objects found north of the Alps.

Stones and Scrolls
3rd century (Askew Codex, 4th c.); tr. 1924Pistis Sophia

A Coptic Gnostic work in which the risen Jesus, eleven years after the resurrection, ascends in light, returns and teaches the disciples, Mary above all, about the heavens and the saving mysteries. Narrated revelation, not history.

The Other Gospels
3rd century text (Lamplugh’s version 1918)The Gnôsis of the Light (Untitled Text of the Bruce Codex)

A 1918 English version of the Untitled Text of the Bruce Codex, a Sethian Gnostic hymn of the Pleroma and its emanations, in which Jesus and Christ appear only as heavenly beings. Lamplugh’s framing is an early-20th-century mystical reading.

The Other Gospels
Early 4th century AD (completed c. 325)Eusebius, Church History, Book I

The opening book of the first history of the Church. It sets Jesus in the time of Augustus, Herod, Pilate and Caiaphas, quotes Josephus on John the Baptist and Jesus, and gives the Edessa story of King Abgar’s letter to Jesus.

The Early Church
Early 4th century AD (completed c. 325)Eusebius, Church History, Book III

Eusebius on the generation after the apostles: the origins of the four Gospels, his list of accepted and disputed books, Papias on Mark and Matthew, Jesus’s relatives, the martyrdom of Symeon, and Ignatius and Polycarp.

The Early Church
Early 4th century AD (completed c. 325)Eusebius, Church History, Book IV

The second century, from Trajan to Marcus Aurelius: Quadratus’s claim that people healed by Jesus lived into his own day, Hadrian’s rescript, Justin Martyr, Hegesippus, Tatian, and the eyewitness letter on the martyrdom of Polycarp.

The Early Church
Early 4th century AD (completed c. 325)Eusebius, Church History, Book V

The late second century: the martyrs of Lyon and Vienne, Irenaeus on how the four Gospels were written, his memory of Polycarp and John, the Easter controversy, and a protest that earlier writers had always called Christ God.

The Early Church
AD 367Athanasius of Alexandria, Festal Letter 39

The Easter letter of 367 in which the bishop of Alexandria lists the books of Scripture. It is the earliest surviving list of exactly the 27 books of today’s New Testament, and it names other books, including the Didache, as useful but outside the canon.

Late antiquity, Islam and the Middle Ages

4th–5th century in present form (tr. 1870)The Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate) and the Pilate Letters

Late-antique Christian legends of Jesus’s trial before Pilate, his descent into Hades, and letters in Pilate’s name, from the Ante-Nicene Fathers. Not Roman records, but hugely influential on medieval art and devotion.

The Other Gospels

A Talmudic tradition that Yeshu (rendered ‘Jesus the Nazarene’) was hanged on Passover eve for sorcery and leading Israel astray after a herald sought defenders for forty days. Whether it preserves an independent memory of Jesus is an open question for your discernment.

Witnesses Outside the Church

From William Hone’s 1820 Apocryphal New Testament: the Arabic Infancy Gospel, with its legends of the Holy Family in Egypt, and the letters of Jesus and King Abgar of Edessa that Eusebius reported. Legends, not history.

The Other Gospels
Meccan period (c. AD 610–622)Qur’an, Surah 19 (Maryam): Mary

The chapter named after Mary: Zachariah and John, the annunciation, the birth of Jesus beneath a palm tree, and the infant Jesus speaking from the cradle as ‘a servant of God’ and prophet. A 7th-century text, not an independent source for the nativity.

Other Voices
Medinan period (c. AD 622–632)Qur’an, Surah 3 (Al ʿImran): The Family of Imran

The Qur’an’s longest account of Mary’s birth, Zachariah and John, and the annunciation of ‘the Messiah Jesus the son of Mary’, with his miracles ‘by God’s permission’. A 7th-century witness to the Islamic portrait of Jesus, not an independent source for his life.

Other Voices
Medinan period (c. AD 622–632)Qur’an, Surah 4 (An-Nisa): Women

Contains the Qur’an’s only explicit statement on the crucifixion (4:157–158): ‘they did not kill him, and they did not crucify him’, but God raised him. Also 4:171, calling Jesus ‘the apostle of God and His Word’ and rejecting ‘Three’.

Other Voices
Medinan period (c. AD 622–632)Qur’an, Surah 5 (Al-Ma’idah): The Table

Late Medinan surah named for the table Jesus’s disciples ask him to call down from heaven. It calls Jesus a prophet who brought the Gospel, rejects ‘God is the third of three’, and ends with Jesus denying that he told people to worship him and his mother.

Other Voices
Radiocarbon date AD 1260–1390 (tested 1988)The Shroud of Turin: radiocarbon and later dating claims

A linen cloth bearing the image of a crucified man, venerated as Jesus’s burial shroud. Three laboratories dated it to about AD 1260–1390 in 1988. Later challenges, including a 2022 X-ray study, have not displaced that result, though the debate continues.

Our own time

The churches, and modern science, speak

28 October 1965Nostra aetate: The Church and Non-Christian Religions

Vatican II’s declaration on other religions. It notes that Muslims revere Jesus as a prophet and honour Mary, and states that Christ’s passion cannot be charged against all Jews then living or Jews today. A modern reception, not historical evidence.

Other Voices
1992 (definitive Latin edition 1997)Catechism of the Catholic Church, sections 464–469: True God and True Man

The Catholic Catechism’s short statement that Jesus Christ is one divine person, ‘true God and true man’, in two natures, as defined by the councils from Nicaea to Chalcedon. A statement of faith, not historical evidence.

Other Voices
11 November 1994Common Christological Declaration of the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East (1994)

A joint statement in which the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East confess together that Christ is truly God and truly man, united in one person, ending a dispute over wording that went back to the Council of Ephesus in 431.

Other Voices
24 October 2024Dilexit nos: Pope Francis on the Heart of Jesus

Pope Francis’s 2024 encyclical on the human and divine love of the Heart of Jesus (the Sacred Heart), drawing on scripture, the Fathers and the visions of Margaret Mary Alacoque. Evidence of present-day teaching, not of first-century events.

Other Voices
17 May 2024Norms for Discerning Alleged Supernatural Phenomena

The Vatican’s 2024 procedure for judging reported apparitions, visions and similar claims, many concerning Jesus or Mary. It sets graded conclusions instead of a yes-or-no verdict, and does not rule on any particular event.
