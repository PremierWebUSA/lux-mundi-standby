The prophecies fulfilled in Christ

2026The Jesus Archive research dossier· 59 min read· Revised 18 November 2026

One hundred connections between the Old Testament and Jesus, each with its historical setting, its fulfillment in Christ and his Church, and the reading of the early Fathers, with the questions raised over Isaiah 7:14, Isaiah 53, Psalm 22 and Daniel 9, and Jesus’s own predictions.

Read the text
About this chapter

This chapter catalogues 100 substantial connections between Israel’s Scriptures and Jesus, arranged roughly in the order of his life, death and resurrection and the Church that followed. Each entry gives the passages involved, the setting in which the older text was written, and how it is fulfilled in Christ, and labels the connection: an explicit New Testament application, an allusion, a typology, or a reading of the Fathers.

The chapter reads these passages as the apostles and the early Church Fathers read them. It explains why a list can be stretched to 300 items or reduced to a handful, takes up four questions the Fathers answered (among them Isaiah 7:14 and the servant of Isaiah 53), gives a table of Jesus’s own predictions, and sets out how the promises of peace, ingathering and a lasting sanctuary are fulfilled in Christ and his Church and completed at his return.

What this catalogue counts

This is a catalogue of 100 substantial scriptural connections, arranged approximately in the order of Jesus’s life, death and resurrection and the Church that followed. It includes direct prophecies, promises, figures and narrative patterns. It is not a claim that the Old Testament contains exactly 100 separate forecasts of Jesus. No ancient canon supplies a numbered master list, and a list can reach 300 or more by dividing a single passage into many details, counting parallel Gospel accounts separately and including loose similarities. The early Fathers never counted them. They read the whole of the Old Testament as a preparation for Christ, as he taught: everything written about him in the Law of Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms had to be fulfilled (Luke 24:27, 44; John 5:39, 46).

Fulfillment takes more than one form. Some passages are direct prophecy: Micah names Bethlehem, and Zechariah a king who comes riding on a donkey. Others are types: persons, events and institutions of Israel’s history that God ordered as figures of what he would accomplish in Christ (1 Corinthians 10:6, 11; Hebrews 10:1). Matthew says that Hosea 11:1 was fulfilled in Jesus’s return from Egypt, although Hosea is recalling Israel’s exodus. That is typology: the exodus is real history, and it is completed in the Son. The apostles and the early Fathers read both kinds as true fulfillment, and the catalogue says which is which, so that a type is not presented as a dated forecast. Matthew also cites a saying about a Nazorean for which no single Old Testament verse survives; it is explained at entry 18. P08

Four labels are used below:

  • Explicit NT application: a New Testament author quotes or unmistakably applies the earlier passage to Jesus or his Church. The apostolic reading is the one the early Fathers received and handed on. Whether the event itself is historically attested is a separate question, treated in the other chapters.
  • NT allusion: the wording or narrative clearly echoes an earlier passage, without a formal statement of fulfillment.
  • Typology: a person, institution, rite or event of the Old Testament prefigures Christ and is completed in him.
  • Reading of the Fathers: a connection that the New Testament does not state outright but that the early Christian writers make and hand on.

References normally follow common English Bible numbering. Hebrew, Catholic, Orthodox, and Septuagint editions sometimes number verses differently, especially in Psalms, Malachi, Micah, and Jonah. The reference is therefore more important than an isolated translated phrase. Biblical chapter-and-verse references are primary-source citations; the numbered references identify additional retrieved editions or contextual scholarship.

1. Ancestry, covenant, and royal identity

1. The woman’s offspring defeats the serpent. Genesis 3:15; compare Romans 16:20, Galatians 4:4, Revelation 12:1-17. Reading of the Fathers, with NT thematic parallels. After the fall, God tells the serpent that the woman’s offspring will strike at its head. This is the protoevangelium, the first announcement of the gospel: a Redeemer born of a woman will overthrow the one who brought sin and death into the world. Irenaeus reads the offspring as Christ, born of a woman, who tramples the serpent’s head and so undoes Adam’s defeat (Against Heresies 5.21.1). The victory is Christ’s, and Paul extends it to his members: God will crush Satan under the feet of the faithful (Romans 16:20). P01

2. Blessing reaches the nations through Abraham’s offspring. Genesis 12:1-3; 18:18; 22:18; Galatians 3:8, 16, 29; Acts 3:25-26. Explicit NT application. God promises Abraham that all nations will be blessed in his offspring. Paul teaches that the offspring is Christ, and that all who belong to Christ, Jew and Gentile alike, are Abraham’s descendants and heirs of the promise (Galatians 3:16, 29). The Fathers read it the same way. Justin tells Trypho that those who believe in Christ are the nation promised to Abraham (Dialogue with Trypho 119), and Irenaeus that Abraham is the father of all who believe and that his seed is the Church (Against Heresies 4.21.1; 5.32.2). P02

3. The chosen line runs through Isaac. Genesis 17:19; 21:12; Matthew 1:2; Luke 3:34; Romans 9:7. Promise and genealogy. Genesis sets Isaac’s line apart from Abraham’s other descendants as the bearer of the covenant, and the Gospels place Jesus within it. Paul draws out the meaning: it is the children of the promise, not physical descent alone, who are counted as Abraham’s offspring (Romans 9:6-8), and Christians are, like Isaac, children of the promise (Galatians 4:28). The line itself rests on the Gospel genealogies; no separate first-century family register survives.

4. The line continues through Jacob. Genesis 28:13-14; Numbers 24:17; Matthew 1:2; Luke 3:34. Promise and messianic application. The promise passes to Jacob: in him and in his offspring all the families of the earth will be blessed. The Gospels place Jesus within Israel, and the blessing of the nations promised to Jacob is given in him. Descent from Jacob does not by itself identify the Messiah; it is one strand of a line that narrows to Judah and to David (entries 5 and 6).

5. A ruler comes from Judah. Genesis 49:8-12; Matthew 1:2-3; Hebrews 7:14; Revelation 5:5. Explicit NT identity claim; reading of the Fathers. Jacob’s blessing gives Judah the sceptre until the one comes to whom it belongs, whom the peoples will obey. The word often transliterated Shiloh is difficult and translations differ. Hebrews states that the Lord sprang from Judah, and Revelation calls him the Lion of the tribe of Judah. Justin reads the blessing as a prophecy of Christ, the expectation of the nations, and observes that Judah had its own rulers until he came and lost them afterwards (First Apology 32; Dialogue with Trypho 52).

6. David’s dynasty endures. 2 Samuel 7:12-16; Psalm 89:3-4, 19-37; Psalm 132:11-12; Luke 1:32-33; Romans 1:3; Acts 2:30. Explicit NT application. Nathan promises David a son whose throne God will establish for ever and who will build a house for God’s name. Solomon built the first Temple, but his kingdom was divided and his line lost the throne. Gabriel announces the fulfillment: Jesus receives the throne of David and reigns without end (Luke 1:32-33), and Peter says that David foresaw the resurrection of the Christ (Acts 2:30-31). Augustine shows that the promise was not fulfilled in Solomon but in Christ, and that the house he builds is made of believers, not of wood and stone (City of God 17.8). The lines about a son who is disciplined when he does wrong apply to David’s ordinary heirs; the everlasting throne belongs to Christ alone.

7. A shoot grows from Jesse’s line. Isaiah 11:1-10; Romans 15:12; Revelation 22:16. Explicit NT application. Isaiah promises a shoot from the stump of Jesse on whom the Spirit of the Lord rests, who judges the poor with justice and to whom the nations turn. Paul quotes the passage for the hope of the Gentiles in Christ (Romans 15:12). Justin and Irenaeus read the Spirit’s resting on the shoot as fulfilled in Jesus (Dialogue with Trypho 87; Against Heresies 3.9.3).

The peace of Isaiah 11 has begun in the Church, where peoples once hostile are reconciled in one body (Ephesians 2:14-16), and it will be complete when Christ returns and creation is renewed (section 10). P03

8. The righteous Branch restores just rule. Jeremiah 23:5-6; 33:14-18; Luke 1:32-33; 1 Corinthians 1:30. Reading of the Fathers, with NT thematic parallels. Jeremiah answers the failure of Judah’s kings with the promise of a righteous Branch of David, a king named “The Lord is our righteousness”, under whom Judah is saved and Israel dwells secure. The New Testament does not quote the passage with a fulfillment formula, but it proclaims its substance: Jesus is the Son of David whose kingdom has no end (Luke 1:32-33), and he has become our righteousness (1 Corinthians 1:30). The early Fathers read the Branch as Christ, and the safety of Judah and Israel as the salvation he gives to his people, gathered from Israel and from the nations. The lasting priesthood promised beside the throne (Jeremiah 33:17-18) is fulfilled in his own eternal priesthood (Hebrews 7:24).

9. The king is God’s son. Psalm 2:7-9; 2 Samuel 7:14; Acts 13:33; Hebrews 1:5; 5:5. Explicit NT application. Psalm 2 was sung of the Davidic king, whom God calls his son on the day of his enthronement and to whom he promises the nations as an inheritance. No king of Judah received them. Acts and Hebrews apply the words to Jesus, declared Son of God in power by his resurrection (Acts 13:33; Romans 1:4). The Fathers hear in them the Father addressing his Son; Justin connects the verse with the voice at the Jordan (Dialogue with Trypho 88; First Apology 40). P04

10. The anointed king has an enduring throne. Psalm 45:6-7; Hebrews 1:8-9. Explicit NT application. A psalm for a royal wedding addresses the king in words that go beyond any earthly monarch: “Your throne, O God, is for ever and ever.” Hebrews gives them as words spoken to the Son. Justin quotes the psalm to show that Christ is to be worshipped as God (Dialogue with Trypho 38, 63), and Irenaeus cites the same verses to show that Scripture gives the name God to the Son as well as to the Father (Against Heresies 3.6.1). P04

2. Conception, birth, and childhood

11. The virgin and Immanuel. Isaiah 7:14-16; Matthew 1:22-23. Explicit NT application. Isaiah offers the sign to King Ahaz during the crisis of about 734 BC, and the verses around it are tied to the events of that reign. The sign itself, a child born of a virgin and called God-with-us, reaches beyond Ahaz’s day. Matthew records its fulfillment in the virginal conception of Jesus, quoting the Greek version, which reads parthenos, virgin. Justin and Irenaeus defend this reading at length (Dialogue with Trypho 43, 66-67; Against Heresies 3.21). The Hebrew and Greek wording is treated in section 7. [P05, P06]

12. Bethlehem as the ruler’s origin. Micah 5:2-5; Matthew 2:5-6; John 7:41-42. Explicit NT application. Micah, in the eighth century BC, names Bethlehem, David’s town, as the place from which a ruler of Israel will come, one “whose origin is from of old, from ancient days”. Matthew reports that the chief priests and scribes gave Herod this passage as the birthplace of the Messiah, and he joins to it the shepherd language of 2 Samuel 5:2. Jesus was born in Bethlehem. Justin cites the prophecy twice (First Apology 34; Dialogue with Trypho 78). Cyril of Jerusalem reads “from ancient days” of the eternal Son, born in time at Bethlehem (Catechetical Lectures 11.20). P07

13. A royal child brings justice and peace. Isaiah 9:6-7; Luke 1:32-33; 2:11-14. Reading of the Fathers, with NT echoes. Isaiah announces a child born for David’s throne, bearing names such as Wonderful Counsellor, Mighty God and Prince of Peace, whose government and peace will have no end. No king of Judah bore such names or reigned without end. The New Testament does not quote the verse with a fulfillment formula, but Gabriel’s words to Mary repeat its substance (Luke 1:32-33) and the angels announce peace at the birth (Luke 2:14). Justin applies the verse to Christ and sees the government on his shoulders in the cross (First Apology 35). The peace he gives is real now in those reconciled to God, and will be complete at his return (section 10).

14. A star rises from Jacob. Numbers 24:17; Matthew 2:1-12; Revelation 22:16. NT allusion; reading of the Fathers. Balaam, a foreign seer, foretells a star rising from Jacob and a sceptre from Israel. Matthew does not quote Numbers, but he tells of foreign magi led by a star to the newborn king of the Jews, and Revelation calls Jesus the bright morning star. Justin and Irenaeus read Balaam’s star as a prophecy of Christ (Dialogue with Trypho 106; Against Heresies 3.9.2). The prophecy does not depend on identifying the star of the magi with a particular astronomical event.

15. Foreigners bring tribute. Psalm 72:10-11; Isaiah 60:1-6; Matthew 2:1-12. NT allusion; reading of the Fathers. Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60 picture kings and nations bringing gold and frankincense to Israel’s king and to Zion. Matthew’s magi, the first Gentiles to worship Christ, bring those gifts. Justin argues that Psalm 72 was not fulfilled in Solomon but in Christ (Dialogue with Trypho 34), and Tertullian sees the prophecy accomplished in the magi (Against Marcion 3.13). Leo the Great calls their coming “the day of our first-fruits and the commencement of the nations’ calling” (Sermon 33.5). Matthew calls the visitors magi, not kings, and does not say there were three; the later tradition of three kings draws on these passages and on the three gifts.

16. God’s son comes out of Egypt. Hosea 11:1; Matthew 2:14-15. Explicit NT typology. Hosea recalls the exodus: God called Israel, his son, out of Egypt, and Israel then turned to idols. Matthew sees the pattern completed in Jesus, the Son in the full sense, who comes out of Egypt and remains faithful where Israel did not. This is fulfillment by type, not by forecast: the exodus was itself a figure of what God would do in his Son, and Christ takes up Israel’s history in his own person. P42

17. Rachel weeps for her children. Jeremiah 31:15-17; Matthew 2:16-18. Explicit NT application. Jeremiah pictures Rachel, mother of the northern tribes, weeping at Ramah as her children go into exile. The verses that follow promise their return, and the chapter goes on to the new covenant. Matthew hears the lament again in the mothers of Bethlehem when Herod kills the children, and Justin cites the same prophecy (Dialogue with Trypho 78). The hope Jeremiah attaches to the lament is kept in the child who escapes and who brings the new covenant. The historical question of the massacre is separate from the scriptural connection.

18. He will be called a Nazorean. Matthew 2:23; proposed connections include Isaiah 11:1 and Judges 13:5. Explicit NT fulfillment claim without a single source verse. Matthew says that the prophets, in the plural, foretold that he would be called a Nazorean. No one verse says this. Jerome points to Isaiah 11:1, where the Hebrew word for the branch from Jesse’s root is netser (Letter 57.7). Matthew may also have in mind the prophets’ witness that the Messiah would be despised (Psalm 22:6; Isaiah 53:3), as a man from obscure Nazareth was (John 1:46). Nazorean should not be confused with Nazirite, a person under a special vow. P08

19. The endangered child repeats Moses’s story. Exodus 1:15-2:10; 4:19; Matthew 2:13-21. Narrative typology. Pharaoh orders the death of Hebrew boys, the child Moses is saved, and he returns when those who sought his life are dead. Matthew’s account of Herod, the flight and the return follows the same pattern and echoes the wording of Exodus 4:19. Jesus is the new Moses, the deliverer of his people and the giver of the new law (compare entry 31). The pattern is a type, not a separate oracle.

3. The forerunner, baptism, and public ministry

20. Elijah returns before the decisive day. Malachi 4:5-6, or 3:23-24 in some editions; Luke 1:17; Matthew 11:14; 17:10-13; John 1:21. Explicit NT application to John the Baptist. Malachi promises Elijah before the day of the Lord. Gabriel says that John will go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah, and Jesus says that John is the Elijah who was to come. When John says he is not Elijah (John 1:21), he denies being Elijah in person, not the office. Justin explains to Trypho that the spirit of Elijah was in John as the forerunner of Christ’s first coming (Dialogue with Trypho 49). P09

21. A wilderness voice prepares the Lord’s road. Isaiah 40:3-5; Mark 1:3; Matthew 3:3; Luke 3:4-6; John 1:23. Explicit NT application. Isaiah 40 opens the message of comfort to the exiles: a voice cries out to prepare the way of the Lord, the God of Israel, who is coming to his people. All four Gospels identify the voice as John the Baptist. The one whose way John prepares is Jesus, so the Gospels present the coming of Jesus as the coming of Israel’s God. Justin cites the passage of John (Dialogue with Trypho 50).

22. A messenger goes ahead. Malachi 3:1; Exodus 23:20; Mark 1:2; Matthew 11:10; Luke 7:27. Explicit NT application. Malachi announces a messenger who prepares the way before the Lord comes to his temple. The Gospels apply the words to John, joining them to Exodus 23:20; Mark places them with Isaiah 40:3 under Isaiah’s name, a composite quotation of a kind common at the time. Jesus himself applies the verse to John (Matthew 11:10). The Lord whose way is prepared is Jesus. P09

23. The chosen son receives the Spirit. Psalm 2:7; Isaiah 42:1; Mark 1:9-11 and parallels. NT allusion. The voice at the Jordan joins the royal Son of Psalm 2:7 with the servant of Isaiah 42:1, in whom God delights and on whom he puts his Spirit; “beloved Son” also recalls Isaac in Genesis 22. At his baptism Jesus is shown to be the anointed king and the servant together, and the Spirit descends on him. Justin quotes Psalm 2:7 as the Father’s voice at the Jordan (Dialogue with Trypho 88).

24. Faithful Israel succeeds in the wilderness. Deuteronomy 6:13, 16; 8:2-3; Matthew 4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13. Explicit quotation within typology. Israel, God’s son, was tested for forty years in the wilderness and failed. Jesus is tested for forty days and answers each temptation from Deuteronomy’s account of that testing. He is the faithful Israel and the new Adam, who conquers where they fell. Irenaeus dwells on the point: Christ overcame the tempter by the words of the Law, and so reversed Adam’s defeat (Against Heresies 5.21.2). P10

25. Light reaches Galilee. Isaiah 9:1-2, or 8:23-9:1; Matthew 4:13-16. Explicit NT application. Isaiah promises light to Zebulun and Naphtali, the northern lands first overrun by Assyria, and goes straight on to the child born for David’s throne (Isaiah 9:6-7). Matthew sees the promise kept when Jesus makes Capernaum his home and begins to preach in Galilee. The light is Christ himself (John 8:12), and the deliverance Isaiah joins to it is the kingdom he proclaims.

26. An anointed herald announces good news and release. Isaiah 61:1-2; 58:6; Luke 4:16-21. Explicit NT application spoken by Jesus in Luke. Isaiah’s herald, anointed by the Spirit, brings good news to the poor and release to captives. In the synagogue at Nazareth Jesus reads the passage, joined to a line from Isaiah 58:6, and says that it is fulfilled that day in the hearing of those present. He stops before “the day of vengeance of our God”: his first coming is the year of the Lord’s favour, and judgment belongs to his return. The title Christ, the Anointed, has its meaning here. [P10, P11]

27. The blind see, the lame walk, and the poor receive news. Isaiah 35:5-6; 61:1; compare Isaiah 26:19; Matthew 11:2-6; Luke 7:18-23. NT allusive answer to a messianic question. Isaiah says that when God comes to save, the blind will see, the deaf hear and the lame leap. Asked by John’s disciples whether he is the one who is to come, Jesus points to those very works. The Dead Sea Scroll 4Q521 shows that Jews before Jesus expected such signs in the time of the Messiah. Justin cites Isaiah 35 as fulfilled in Christ’s healings (First Apology 48; Dialogue with Trypho 69). P12

28. The servant bears illnesses. Isaiah 53:4; Matthew 8:16-17. Explicit NT application. Matthew applies the servant’s bearing of infirmities to Jesus’s healings. The same servant bears sins in his death (1 Peter 2:24). The healings are the first signs of one work, in which Christ takes on himself the whole weight of human misery, sickness and sin together. The verse is fulfilled both in the ministry and on the cross.

29. The servant acts gently and brings justice. Isaiah 42:1-4; Matthew 12:15-21. Explicit NT application. Isaiah’s servant is gentle, does not break the bruised reed, and brings justice to the nations. Matthew quotes the passage in full of Jesus. In this part of Isaiah the name servant is also given to Israel; the Fathers answer that Christ is the servant in person, who carries out Israel’s calling. Justin reads the servant named Jacob and Israel as Christ, in whom the nations hope (Dialogue with Trypho 123, 135). P13

30. A light for the Gentiles. Isaiah 42:6; 49:6; Luke 2:29-32; Acts 13:47. Explicit NT application. God tells the servant that restoring Israel is too small a task: he will be a light to the nations, so that salvation reaches the ends of the earth. Simeon greets the child Jesus in those words, and Paul and Barnabas apply the same commission to their own preaching. Christ is the light, and the Church carries it: the servant’s mission is fulfilled in the head and continued in his body. Justin insists that the light of the nations is Christ (Dialogue with Trypho 121-122). P14

31. A prophet like Moses. Deuteronomy 18:15-19; John 6:14; Acts 3:22-23; 7:37. Explicit NT application. Moses promises that God will raise up a prophet like him, to whom the people must listen. Israel had many prophets, but Deuteronomy itself closes by saying that none had arisen like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face (Deuteronomy 34:10). Peter and Stephen declare that Jesus is that prophet. At the transfiguration the Father’s voice repeats Moses’s command: “Listen to him” (Mark 9:7).

32. Teaching through parables. Psalm 78:2; Matthew 13:34-35. Explicit NT application. Psalm 78 opens with the psalmist saying he will open his mouth in a parable, and goes on to draw out the hidden lessons of Israel’s history. Matthew says this is fulfilled when Jesus teaches the crowds in parables and utters what has been hidden since the foundation of the world. The psalmist’s way of teaching finds its full form in Christ, who reveals what Israel’s story was pointing to. P15

33. Hearers fail to understand. Isaiah 6:9-10; Mark 4:10-12; Matthew 13:14-15; John 12:39-41; Acts 28:25-27. Explicit NT application. At his call Isaiah is told that the people will hear without understanding. Jesus applies the words to those who hear his parables and do not respond, John to the unbelief that met his signs, and Paul to his hearers in Rome. John adds that Isaiah said this because he saw Christ’s glory (John 12:41): the Lord enthroned in Isaiah’s vision is the Son. The hardening is not final; Paul teaches that it is partial and will end (Romans 11:25-26). P15

34. Lips honour God while hearts remain distant. Isaiah 29:13; Mark 7:6-7; Matthew 15:7-9. Explicit NT application. Isaiah rebukes worship offered with the lips while the heart is far away. Jesus applies the words to particular opponents who set human tradition above God’s command. It is a prophetic rebuke renewed, and it speaks to every worshipper; it is not a judgment on the Jewish people.

35. The servant’s message encounters disbelief. Isaiah 53:1; John 12:37-38; Romans 10:16. Explicit NT application. The servant poem begins by asking who has believed the report. John sees this fulfilled in those who did not believe in spite of Jesus’s signs, and Paul in the mixed reception of the gospel. The servant’s rejection is part of the prophecy: that many did not believe the Messiah is itself what Isaiah foretold.

36. Hatred without cause. Psalm 35:19; 69:4; John 15:24-25. Explicit NT application. In Psalms 35 and 69 an innocent man is hated without reason. Jesus says the words are fulfilled in the hatred shown to him (John 15:25). David, persecuted though innocent, is a figure of Christ, the one truly innocent sufferer. Augustine teaches that in such psalms Christ himself speaks, sometimes in his own person as head and sometimes in the person of his body, the Church (Expositions of the Psalms 41 (40).1; 86 (85).1).

37. Estrangement from his family. Psalm 69:8; Mark 3:20-21, 31-35; John 7:5. Reading of the Fathers. The psalmist has become a stranger to his brothers; the Gospels record that Jesus’s relatives did not at first believe in him. No Gospel quotes the verse, but the New Testament applies Psalm 69 to Christ repeatedly (John 2:17; 15:25; Romans 15:3). The psalm’s confession of sin in verse 5 is, as Augustine says of this verse, spoken “out of the mouth of the Body”: it is the voice of Christ speaking for his members, whose sins he made his own (Expositions of the Psalms 69 (68).9).

38. The true shepherd gathers the flock. Ezekiel 34:11-24; Psalm 23; John 10:1-18; Matthew 9:36. NT allusion. Ezekiel condemns Israel’s failed shepherds. God promises to shepherd his flock himself, and in the same passage to set over them one shepherd, his servant David. Jesus says: “I am the good shepherd” (John 10:11). Both promises are kept in one person, who is God shepherding his people and the Son of David. The gathering of the scattered flock is fulfilled in the Church, one flock under one shepherd, drawn from Israel and from the sheep that are “not of this fold” (John 10:16).

39. Bread from heaven. Exodus 16; Psalm 78:24; John 6:30-58. Explicit NT typology. God fed Israel in the wilderness with bread from heaven. Jesus says that the manna was a figure: his Father gives the true bread from heaven, and “I am the bread of life” (John 6:32-35). The fathers ate the manna and died; whoever eats this bread will live for ever. The early Fathers read the manna as a figure of the Eucharist: Ambrose sets the two side by side (On the Mysteries 8).

40. Water from the wilderness rock. Exodus 17:1-7; Numbers 20:2-13; 1 Corinthians 10:1-4. Explicit NT typology. Twice in the wilderness water flows from a rock for Israel. Paul writes that “the rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4): the one who sustained Israel then is the one who gives living water now. Paul treats the wilderness events as figures written down for the Church’s instruction (1 Corinthians 10:6, 11), and Tertullian reads the water from the rock as a figure of baptism, water blessed in Christ (On Baptism 9).

41. The lifted bronze serpent. Numbers 21:4-9; John 3:14-15. Explicit NT typology. Moses sets a bronze serpent on a pole, and those who have been bitten look at it and live. Jesus says that the Son of Man must be lifted up in the same way (John 3:14). The lifting up is the cross, and the looking is faith. The Epistle of Barnabas and Justin both read the serpent as a figure of the crucified Christ (Barnabas 12; Dialogue with Trypho 94; First Apology 60).

42. Jacob’s ladder and the meeting of heaven and earth. Genesis 28:10-17; John 1:51. Explicit NT allusion. Jacob dreams of a ladder between earth and heaven, with angels ascending and descending. Jesus tells Nathanael that he will see the angels ascending and descending on the Son of Man. Christ is himself the meeting place of heaven and earth, true God and true man, and what Jacob called the house of God and the gate of heaven is found in him. Justin holds that it was Christ who appeared to Jacob above the ladder, and reads the stone that Jacob anointed there as a figure of him (Dialogue with Trypho 86).

43. Jesus as the temple. 2 Samuel 7:13; Zechariah 6:12-13; John 2:19-22; compare Revelation 21:22. Typology and prophetic application. Nathan promises that David’s son will build a house for God’s name, and Zechariah that the Branch will build the temple of the Lord. Jesus says: “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up”, and John explains that he spoke of the temple of his body. The true Temple is the risen body of Christ, and the Church built on him as living stones (1 Corinthians 3:16; Ephesians 2:19-22; 1 Peter 2:5). Augustine reads the house of 2 Samuel 7 in this way (City of God 17.8). In the new Jerusalem there is no temple building, because its temple is the Lord God and the Lamb (Revelation 21:22).

44. Living water flows outward. Isaiah 44:3; Ezekiel 47:1-12; Zechariah 14:8; John 7:37-39. Scriptural allusion without a single source verse. At the feast of Tabernacles Jesus cries out that rivers of living water will flow, as the Scripture has said. No one verse matches. The prophets’ promises of water and Spirit stand behind the saying, above all the river that flows from the temple in Ezekiel 47. John explains that Jesus spoke of the Spirit, to be given after his glorification. Christ is the temple from which the river flows, and the promise is kept at Pentecost and in baptism. P16

45. Elijah and Elisha as miracle patterns. 1 Kings 17:8-24; 2 Kings 4:18-44; Luke 4:25-27; 7:11-17; John 6:1-14. Explicit comparison and narrative typology. Elijah and Elisha each raise a mother’s dead son, and Elisha feeds a hundred men with a few loaves. Jesus raises the widow’s son at Nain and feeds five thousand, and the crowd says that a great prophet has arisen (Luke 7:16). At Nazareth he recalls both prophets himself. Their works were figures of his: they prayed and God acted, while he commands in his own name.

46. An unimpressive appearance. Isaiah 53:2-3; compare Philippians 2:6-8. Reading of the Fathers. The servant has “no form or majesty that we should look at him” and is despised. The Gospels give no description of Jesus’s appearance, and the verse should not be used as one. The Fathers read it of his humiliation: Justin says the prophets speak of two comings, the first without glory and in suffering, the second in majesty (Dialogue with Trypho 14, 32). Paul describes the same descent: he emptied himself and took the form of a servant (Philippians 2:7).

4. Jerusalem, betrayal, trial, and crucifixion

47. The king enters on a donkey. Zechariah 9:9-10; Matthew 21:4-5; John 12:14-16; compare Mark 11:1-10. Explicit NT application. Zechariah calls Jerusalem to rejoice: her king comes, humble and riding on a donkey, and his rule of peace will reach the ends of the earth. Jesus enters Jerusalem in exactly this way, a deliberate claim to be that king. Matthew mentions a donkey and a colt and Mark one colt; Zechariah’s Hebrew parallelism names the same animal twice. Justin cites the prophecy as fulfilled (First Apology 35; Dialogue with Trypho 53). The entry is the coming of the king who conquers by humility and not by force. The peace “from sea to sea” is his kingdom, present in the Church among all nations and complete at his return. P17

48. Acclamation of the one who comes. Psalm 118:25-26; Mark 11:9-10 and parallels. Explicit scriptural acclamation. Psalm 118, sung at the great feasts, blesses the one who comes in the name of the Lord. The crowds greet Jesus with it as he enters Jerusalem, and he says that the city will not see him again until it says the same words (Matthew 23:39). Christians still sing them in the Sanctus at the Eucharist, greeting the Lord who comes.

49. Praise from children. Psalm 8:2; Matthew 21:15-16. Explicit NT application. Psalm 8 says that God has drawn praise from the mouths of infants; Matthew follows the Greek wording, praise, where the Hebrew is commonly translated strength. When the chief priests object to the children crying “Hosanna to the Son of David”, Jesus answers with this verse. A psalm about praise given to God is applied by Jesus to praise given to himself.

50. The temple should be a house of prayer. Isaiah 56:6-8; Mark 11:17; Matthew 21:13; Luke 19:46. Explicit quotation. Isaiah promises that foreigners who join themselves to the Lord will be welcomed in his house, which will be called a house of prayer for all peoples. Jesus quotes the verse as he clears the Temple; Mark preserves the phrase about all nations. The promise is fulfilled in him and in the Church, where all nations worship the Father (John 4:21-23; Ephesians 2:13-18).

51. A den of robbers. Jeremiah 7:1-15; Mark 11:17 and parallels. Explicit quotation and prophetic action. Jeremiah, standing in the Temple gate, warns those who do wrong and then treat the sanctuary as a safe refuge, and reminds them that God destroyed the earlier sanctuary at Shiloh. Jesus takes up Jeremiah’s words and his warning with them. The Temple fell forty years later (see section 9).

52. Zeal for God’s house. Psalm 69:9; John 2:13-17; compare Romans 15:3. Explicit NT application. “Zeal for your house will consume me”, says the psalmist. John records that the disciples remembered the line when Jesus cleared the Temple, and Paul applies the second half of the verse to Christ’s bearing of insults (Romans 15:3). The New Testament thus reads the whole verse, and the psalm, as Christ’s own voice. John places the Temple action early in the ministry and the other Gospels in the last week.

53. The Lord comes to his temple. Malachi 3:1-4; compare Luke 2:22-38; Mark 11:11-17. Reading of the Fathers. Malachi says that the Lord will come suddenly to his temple and will purify the sons of Levi until they present right offerings. The Lord comes to his Temple when Jesus is presented there as a child and when he cleanses it as a man. The pure offering that Malachi foresees among the nations (Malachi 1:11) is, for the earliest Christian writers, the Eucharist: so the Didache (14), Justin (Dialogue with Trypho 41, 117) and Irenaeus (Against Heresies 4.17.5). P09

54. The rejected stone becomes foundational. Psalm 118:22-23; Mark 12:10-11; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7. Explicit NT application. “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone.” Jesus applies the verse to himself at the end of the parable of the tenants; Peter repeats it before the Sanhedrin and in his first letter. The rejection is the cross and the exaltation is the resurrection. The stone texts were among the first that early Christians gathered as testimonies to Christ (Epistle of Barnabas 6; Cyprian, Testimonies 2.16).

55. The stone causes stumbling and offers security. Isaiah 8:14-15; 28:16; Romans 9:32-33; 1 Peter 2:6-8. Explicit NT application. Isaiah speaks of the Lord himself as a stone of stumbling for both houses of Israel, and of a precious cornerstone laid in Zion on which the believer can rest. Paul and Peter join the two texts and apply them to Christ: he is the sure foundation for those who believe, and a stumbling block for those who do not. What Isaiah says of the Lord is said of Jesus.

56. Betrayal by a table companion. Psalm 41:9; John 13:18. Explicit NT application. David laments that the friend who ate his bread has turned against him; the nearest parallel in his life is the treachery of his counsellor Ahithophel (2 Samuel 15-17). At the Last Supper Jesus says that the verse is to be fulfilled in Judas. The betrayal of David is the figure, and the betrayal of the Son of David at table is the fulfillment. The psalm’s plea for forgiveness is the voice of Christ speaking for his members, as Augustine explains in his exposition of this psalm (Expositions of the Psalms 41 (40).1, 5).

57. Betrayal by an intimate friend. Psalm 55:12-14, 20-21; compare Matthew 26:47-50. Reading of the Fathers. The psalmist grieves that his betrayer is no enemy but a companion and close friend. The Gospels do not quote the passage, but the early Fathers read it beside Judas’s kiss. It belongs with Psalm 41 as one witness to the betrayal, not as a second and separate prediction.

58. Thirty pieces of silver. Zechariah 11:12-13; Matthew 26:14-16; 27:3-10. Explicit NT application. In Zechariah the shepherd of the flock asks for his wages and is paid thirty pieces of silver, the price of a slave (Exodus 21:32). The Lord calls it, with irony, the handsome price at which he was valued, and the money is thrown into the house of the Lord. Matthew records that Judas was paid thirty pieces of silver and threw them into the Temple. The shepherd so valued is the Lord himself. Tertullian cites the prophecy in his account of the betrayal (Against Marcion 4.40). The sum is given by Matthew alone. P18

59. Money in the temple and the potter’s field. Zechariah 11:13; Jeremiah 18:1-3; 19:1-13; 32:6-9; Matthew 27:5-10. Explicit fulfillment claim with a composite source. Matthew attributes the prophecy to Jeremiah, although the closest wording is Zechariah 11:13. Jeremiah supplies the potter, the purchase of a field and the judgment on Jerusalem (Jeremiah 19; 32:6-9), and Matthew joins the two prophets under one name, as Mark does with Isaiah (entry 22). The Fathers knew the difficulty: Augustine discusses it at length and concludes that the prophets speak by one Spirit (Harmony of the Gospels 3.7). No single verse of Jeremiah contains the whole quotation. P18

60. The shepherd is struck and the sheep scatter. Zechariah 13:7; Mark 14:27; Matthew 26:31. Explicit NT application. “Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered.” In Zechariah the shepherd is the man who stands next to the Lord, and his death is followed by the refining of a remnant who call on God’s name. On the way to Gethsemane Jesus quotes the verse as about to be fulfilled in his arrest and the flight of the disciples, and adds that after he is raised he will go before them to Galilee. The Epistle of Barnabas and Justin cite the same prophecy (Barnabas 5; Dialogue with Trypho 53).

61. False witnesses testify. Psalm 35:11; compare Mark 14:55-59; Matthew 26:59-61. Reading of the Fathers; general allusion. “False witnesses rise up”, says the psalmist. The Gospels record false testimony at the hearing before the high priest without quoting the verse. It is one more feature of the innocent sufferer of the Psalms that is found in Christ’s Passion, and it is best read as part of that whole figure, not as a separate prediction.

62. Striking, humiliation, and spitting. Isaiah 50:6; Mark 14:65; 15:19; Matthew 26:67; 27:30. NT allusion. Isaiah’s servant gives his back to those who strike him and his cheeks to those who pull out the beard, and does not hide his face from insult and spitting. The Gospels record the scourging, the blows and the spitting. They do not mention the beard, and that detail should not be listed as separately attested. The Epistle of Barnabas and Justin quote the verse of the Passion (Barnabas 5; First Apology 38).

63. Silence before accusers. Isaiah 53:7; Mark 14:61; 15:5; Matthew 27:12-14; Acts 8:32-35. NT allusion and explicit application. The servant is led like a lamb to the slaughter and does not open his mouth. Philip, asked by the Ethiopian official whom the prophet means, begins from this verse and tells him the good news of Jesus (Acts 8:32-35). The Gospels record that Jesus gave no answer to the accusations before the high priest and before Pilate, to Pilate’s amazement. He did speak at other points; the fulfillment lies in his refusal to defend himself. Clement of Rome copies out the whole chapter as a portrait of Christ before the end of the first century (1 Clement 16).

64. Rulers gather against the anointed one. Psalm 2:1-2; Acts 4:25-28. Explicit NT application. Psalm 2 opens with nations and rulers conspiring against the Lord and his Anointed. The Jerusalem church prays the psalm and names those who gathered against Jesus: Herod, Pontius Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, who did what God’s plan had determined beforehand (Acts 4:25-28). Justin quotes the psalm in the same sense (First Apology 40). Gentile and Jew are named together, and the Passion cannot be charged against the Jewish people as a whole, then or now, as the Second Vatican Council (Nostra Aetate 4) and the Catholic Catechism (597) state plainly.

65. The righteous son is tested by a shameful death. Wisdom of Solomon 2:12-20; Matthew 27:43. NT allusion; reading of the Fathers. In the Wisdom of Solomon the wicked plot against the righteous man who calls God his father: if he is God’s son, God will help him, so they will condemn him to a shameful death. The mockery at the cross in Matthew 27:43 follows the passage closely. Augustine says that in this book the Passion of Christ is most openly prophesied (City of God 17.20). Wisdom belongs to the Catholic and Orthodox Old Testament; it is not in the Jewish canon or in most Protestant Bibles. P19

66. Mockery and challenges to divine rescue. Psalm 22:7-8; Mark 15:29-32; Matthew 27:39-43. NT allusion. The sufferer of Psalm 22 is mocked by all who see him: he trusted in the Lord, so let the Lord rescue him. The passers-by and the chief priests mock Jesus on the cross in these terms, and Matthew gives the taunt almost in the psalm’s words. Justin goes through the psalm line by line as a prophecy of the Passion (Dialogue with Trypho 97-106). P43

67. The cry of abandonment. Psalm 22:1; Mark 15:34; Matthew 27:46. Explicit quotation by Jesus. On the cross Jesus prays the first line of Psalm 22 in his own language. The psalm he begins moves from abandonment to rescue and to the praise of God among all nations, and in praying it he makes the whole psalm his own. Justin says that the opening line announced beforehand what Christ would say (Dialogue with Trypho 99). Augustine hears in the cry Christ speaking in the person of the humanity he bore, the head for his body (Expositions of the Psalms 22 (21).1-2). P44

68. Hands and feet pierced. Psalm 22:16, Hebrew verse 17; compare Luke 24:39-40; John 20:25-27. Reading of the Fathers; not quoted in the New Testament. The Greek version, made before Christ, has a verb meaning dug or pierced; the received Hebrew text reads “like a lion”, in a clause that is hard to construe. The textual question is treated in section 7. The Gospels record the wounds in the risen Jesus’s hands and feet. Justin and Tertullian quote the verse of the crucifixion (Dialogue with Trypho 97; First Apology 35; Against Marcion 3.19). P21

69. Clothes divided and lots cast. Psalm 22:18; Mark 15:24; John 19:23-24. Explicit NT application in John. The psalmist says that his garments are divided and lots are cast for his clothing. All four Gospels record that the soldiers divided Jesus’s clothes; John quotes the psalm and adds the seamless tunic for which they cast lots. Justin cites the verse (Dialogue with Trypho 97; First Apology 35). The four Gospels are not four independent witnesses to this detail, but all of them report it. P22

70. Extreme thirst. Psalm 22:15; John 19:28. NT allusion. John says that Jesus, knowing that all was now finished, said “I thirst” to fulfill the Scripture, without naming the passage. Psalm 22:15 speaks of the sufferer’s parched mouth, and Psalm 69:21 of vinegar given for his thirst, which is what follows in John. Both psalms are in view.

71. Gall or sour wine. Psalm 69:21; Matthew 27:34, 48; Mark 15:23, 36; John 19:28-30. NT allusion and fulfillment context. The psalmist is given gall for food and vinegar for his thirst. Matthew mentions wine mixed with gall before the crucifixion, Mark wine mixed with myrrh, and all four Gospels sour wine at the end. The accounts differ in detail and should be reported as they stand. Irenaeus lists the vinegar and gall among the things the prophets foretold of the Passion (Against Heresies 4.33.12).

72. Entrusting his spirit to God. Psalm 31:5; Luke 23:46. Explicit quotation. “Into your hands I commit my spirit” is the psalmist’s prayer of trust in danger. Luke records it as Jesus’s last word, with “Father” added. The prayer of David becomes the prayer of the Son, and Stephen echoes it at his own death (Acts 7:59). Justin cites the saying from the memoirs of the apostles (Dialogue with Trypho 105).

73. No bone is broken. Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12; Psalm 34:20; John 19:33-36. Explicit NT fulfillment claim. The law forbids breaking a bone of the Passover lamb, and Psalm 34 says that God guards the bones of the righteous man. John records that the soldiers broke the legs of the two men crucified with Jesus but not his, and says that this fulfilled the Scripture. Jesus dies as the true Passover lamb (entry 79). P22

74. The pierced figure is seen and mourned. Zechariah 12:10; John 19:34-37; Revelation 1:7. Explicit NT application. Zechariah foretells that God will pour out a spirit of grace on the house of David and on Jerusalem, and that they will look on the one they have pierced and mourn as for an only son. In the received Hebrew the Lord says “they shall look on me”. John sees the prophecy begin to be fulfilled when the soldier pierces Jesus’s side; Revelation applies it to his coming in glory, when every eye will see him. Justin reads it of the second coming (First Apology 52; Dialogue with Trypho 14). The mourning that comes with the spirit of grace is repentance, begun at Pentecost (Acts 2:37) and awaited in full before his return (Romans 11:25-27). P23

75. Darkness at noon. Amos 8:9-10; Mark 15:33; Matthew 27:45; Luke 23:44-45. NT allusion; reading of the Fathers. Amos says that on the day of judgment the sun will go down at noon and there will be mourning as for an only son. The Gospels report darkness over the land from noon until three. They do not quote Amos, but Irenaeus cites the verse as a plain announcement of it (Against Heresies 4.33.12). The report of the darkness and any proposed natural explanation are separate questions.

76. Counted among transgressors. Isaiah 53:12; Luke 22:37; 23:32-33. Explicit NT application. At the Last Supper Jesus quotes Isaiah 53:12 and says that this Scripture must be fulfilled in him. He is arrested as a criminal and crucified between two others. Mark 15:28, which repeats the citation in some Bibles, is absent from the earliest manuscripts and is a later addition; the fulfillment statement rests on Luke. P43

77. Burial associated with a rich man. Isaiah 53:9; Matthew 27:57-60. NT allusion; reading of the Fathers. The servant’s grave is assigned with the wicked, and he is with a rich man in his death. The Hebrew of the line is difficult and translations vary. Jesus died as a criminal and was buried in the new tomb of Joseph of Arimathea, whom Matthew calls rich. Cyril of Jerusalem reads the verse as fulfilled in that burial (Catechetical Lectures 13.34; 14.3). P24

5. The theological meaning of the death

78. The servant suffers for others. Isaiah 52:13-53:12; Mark 10:45; Acts 8:30-35; 1 Peter 2:22-25; Hebrews 9:28. Explicit NT application and allusion. The servant is pierced for the transgressions of others, bears the sin of many, and is then exalted. Jesus describes his death as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45), Philip preaches Jesus from this chapter, and Peter quotes it of the cross. Clement of Rome copies out the chapter as a portrait of Christ (1 Clement 16), and Justin sets it beside the Passion (First Apology 50-51). From the apostles onward, the early Fathers read Isaiah 53 as the prophecy of Christ’s redeeming death. The history of the passage’s interpretation is given in section 7. P25

79. The Passover lamb. Exodus 12; 1 Corinthians 5:7; John 19:14, 36; compare John 1:29. Explicit NT typology. At the first Passover the blood of an unblemished lamb protected Israel’s households, and the people went out from slavery. Paul writes that Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed. John the Baptist calls Jesus the Lamb of God, and John’s Gospel places his death at the time of the Passover sacrifice. Justin and Melito of Sardis expound the lamb as a figure of Christ (Dialogue with Trypho 40, 111; On Pascha 32-33). The exodus was a real deliverance and also the figure of a greater one, from sin and death.

80. Isaac, the beloved son offered and restored. Genesis 22:1-19; Romans 8:32; Hebrews 11:17-19. Typology, with NT verbal and conceptual parallels. Abraham does not withhold his only son, Isaac carries the wood, and Isaac is given back alive. Paul echoes the scene when he says that God did not spare his own Son (Romans 8:32), and Hebrews says that Abraham received Isaac back as a figure. In Genesis the sacrifice is stopped and a ram is provided; on Calvary the Son is given. The Epistle of Barnabas calls Isaac a type fulfilled in Christ (Barnabas 7), and Irenaeus says that Abraham offered his only son so that God might offer his (Against Heresies 4.5.4).

81. The Day of Atonement and sacrificial access. Leviticus 16; Hebrews 9:6-14, 24-28; 10:1-18; later Epistle of Barnabas 7. Explicit NT and early Christian typology. Once a year the high priest entered the Holy of Holies with blood to atone for the people. Hebrews teaches that Christ, the true high priest, entered heaven itself once for all with his own blood, and that the yearly rite was a shadow of this. The Epistle of Barnabas and Justin read the two goats as figures of Christ as well (Barnabas 7; Dialogue with Trypho 40). The details of the rite are many and should not be forced into a single picture; Hebrews gives the reading to follow.

82. The curse of hanging on a tree. Deuteronomy 21:22-23; Galatians 3:13; Acts 5:30. Explicit NT application. Deuteronomy says that a hanged man is under God’s curse. Trypho raises this against a crucified Messiah, and Paul had already given the answer: Christ became a curse for us to redeem us from the curse of the law, so that the blessing of Abraham might come to the nations (Galatians 3:13-14). Justin answers Trypho in the same way (Dialogue with Trypho 94-96). The law is a general rule, not a prediction of the cross; the cross is what gives it its deepest meaning. P02

83. An anointed one is cut off. Daniel 9:24-27; compare Matthew 24:15; Mark 13:14. Reading of the Fathers; the Gospels cite the chapter. Gabriel tells Daniel that seventy weeks of years are decreed to end sin, atone for iniquity and bring in everlasting righteousness; an anointed one will be cut off, and the city and the sanctuary destroyed. Jesus speaks of Daniel’s desolating abomination as still to come in his own day (Matthew 24:15). Most of the Fathers read the weeks as completed in Christ’s coming and death and in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70: so Tertullian (Against the Jews 8), Eusebius (Demonstration of the Gospel 8.2) and Athanasius (On the Incarnation 39-40). Irenaeus and Hippolytus place the last week at the end of the age (Against Heresies 5.25.4; On Christ and Antichrist 43). Their calculations differ; all of them read the anointed one as Christ. See section 7. P26

84. A new covenant. Jeremiah 31:31-34; Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25; Hebrews 8:8-12; 10:16-17. Explicit NT application. Jeremiah promises a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah: the law written on hearts and sins forgiven. At the Last Supper Jesus calls the cup the new covenant in his blood. Hebrews quotes the prophecy in full and declares it fulfilled in Christ. The covenant was made with Israel, in the persons of the apostles and the first Jewish believers, and opened to the nations; its people is the Church. Justin tells Trypho that Christ is the new covenant and that those who come to God through him are “the true spiritual Israel” (Dialogue with Trypho 11). P27

85. Covenant ratified through blood. Exodus 24:3-8; Mark 14:24; Matthew 26:28; Hebrews 9:18-22. Explicit NT typology and verbal echo. At Sinai Moses sprinkles the people with blood and says: “This is the blood of the covenant.” Jesus takes up those words over the cup, and Hebrews sets the two scenes side by side. The Sinai rite is the figure; the death of Christ and the Eucharist are the reality, and they found the people of the new covenant.

86. Obedience and a prepared body. Psalm 40:6-8; Hebrews 10:5-10. Explicit NT application. The psalmist says that God does not want sacrifice but a servant who does his will. Hebrews quotes the Greek version, which speaks of a body prepared, where the received Hebrew speaks of ears opened, and gives the words to Christ as he comes into the world. Both forms speak of obedience; the Greek brings out how it was given, in the body offered once for all. The Septuagint was the Bible of the early Church, and Hebrews argues from it with full confidence. P28

6. Resurrection, exaltation, and the continuing mission

87. Revival on the third day. Hosea 6:1-3; compare 1 Corinthians 15:4; Luke 24:46. Reading of the Fathers; not quoted in the New Testament. In Hosea the people say that after two days God will revive them and on the third day raise them up. Paul says that Christ was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, without naming a verse. Tertullian and Cyprian give this one (Against Marcion 4.43; Testimonies 2.25). The words are spoken of the people, and they are fulfilled first in Christ, in whom his people rise (Ephesians 2:5-6). Jonah is the other third-day text (entry 88). P29

88. Jonah’s three days. Jonah 1:17; Matthew 12:39-40; compare Luke 11:29-32. Explicit NT typology. Jesus gives the sign of Jonah: as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the fish, so the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth. Luke stresses a second side of the sign, Jonah’s preaching to Nineveh. The reckoning is inclusive, as was usual: Friday, Saturday and Sunday are three days, not seventy-two hours. Justin and Cyril of Jerusalem expound the type (Dialogue with Trypho 107; Catechetical Lectures 14).

89. The holy one does not undergo corruption. Psalm 16:8-11; Acts 2:25-32; 13:35-37. Explicit NT application. David says that God will not abandon him to the realm of the dead or let his holy one see corruption. Peter argues at Pentecost that David died and his tomb is known, so that he spoke as a prophet of the resurrection of the Christ; Paul makes the same argument at Antioch. The early Fathers follow the apostles: the psalm is fulfilled in Jesus, whose body did not see decay. P31

90. The suffering servant sees life and vindication. Isaiah 53:10-12; compare the Christological reading of the servant in Acts 8:35 and 1 Peter 2:21-25. Reading of the Fathers. After his death the servant sees his offspring and prolongs his days, and in the Greek version and the Dead Sea Scrolls he sees light. One who has been cut off from the land of the living and then lives has been raised. The early Fathers read these verses of the resurrection, and the offspring as those reborn in Christ. P24

91. David’s enduring promises become effective. Isaiah 55:3; Acts 13:34. Explicit NT application. Isaiah promises an everlasting covenant, the sure mercies shown to David. Paul quotes it at Antioch as fulfilled in the resurrection: the promise of an everlasting kingdom needs a king who lives for ever, and Jesus, raised to die no more, is that king.

92. Death is finally defeated. Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:14; 1 Corinthians 15:54-57. Explicit NT application to the resurrection of the dead. Isaiah says that God will swallow up death for ever, and Hosea taunts death and its sting. Paul quotes both: the victory is won in Christ’s resurrection and will be complete when the dead are raised at his coming. This is fulfillment begun and awaiting completion, as the apostle himself presents it. P30

93. The vindicated one joins his brothers in praise. Psalm 22:22; Hebrews 2:11-12. Explicit NT application. Psalm 22 turns from suffering to praise: the sufferer will tell of God’s name to his brothers and praise him in the assembly. Hebrews gives the words to Christ, who is not ashamed to call those he sanctifies his brothers. Justin sees them fulfilled when the risen Jesus stood among the apostles (Dialogue with Trypho 106). The psalm goes on to say that all the ends of the earth will turn to the Lord, which is fulfilled in the Church among the nations.

94. Enthronement at God’s right hand. Psalm 110:1; Mark 12:35-37; 14:62; Acts 2:34-36; Hebrews 1:13. Explicit NT application. “The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand.” Jesus asks how the Messiah can be David’s son if David calls him Lord, and before the high priest he claims the seat at God’s right hand. Peter proclaims it fulfilled in his exaltation. It is the Old Testament verse most often cited in the New. Justin argues that it cannot refer to Hezekiah or to any earthly king (Dialogue with Trypho 33, 83; First Apology 45). The Church confesses the enthronement in the Creed: he is seated at the right hand of the Father. P32

95. The Son of Man receives dominion. Daniel 7:13-14, 18, 27; Mark 14:62; Matthew 24:30; Revelation 1:7, 13. Explicit NT application and allusion. Daniel sees four beasts, the empires, and then one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven, who receives from the Ancient of Days an everlasting dominion over all peoples. Jesus takes Son of Man as his own title and tells the high priest that he will be seen coming with the clouds. The kingdom is also given to the people of the holy ones (Daniel 7:27): the king and his people share one kingdom, Christ and his Church. Justin and Hippolytus read the vision of Christ (Dialogue with Trypho 31; Commentary on Daniel 4.10-11). P33

96. A permanent priest in Melchizedek’s order. Genesis 14:18-20; Psalm 110:4; Hebrews 5:5-10; 7:1-28. Explicit NT typology and application. Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, brings out bread and wine and blesses Abraham; Psalm 110 swears to David’s lord a priesthood for ever in the order of Melchizedek. Hebrews teaches that this is Christ’s priesthood: it does not come from Levi and it does not end. Melchizedek is a figure of Christ, not Christ himself. Cyprian sees in his bread and wine a figure of the Lord’s sacrifice (Letter 63.4, numbered 62 in some editions). P34

97. Ascension and gifts. Psalm 68:18; Ephesians 4:7-13. Explicit NT application. The psalm celebrates God ascending in triumph with captives in his train. Paul applies it to Christ’s ascension, reading that he gave gifts to men where the Hebrew says that he received them: the victor receives the spoils in order to share them out, and the gifts are the ministries that build up the Church. Justin quotes the verse in Paul’s form (Dialogue with Trypho 39). P35

98. The renewed Adam and restored human vocation. Genesis 1:26-28; 2:7; Psalm 8:4-6; Romans 5:12-21; 1 Corinthians 15:21-28, 45-49; Hebrews 2:5-9. Explicit NT typology. Adam was made in God’s image and given dominion, and by his disobedience brought death. Paul calls Adam “a type of the one who was to come” (Romans 5:14): Christ is the last Adam, by whose obedience many are made righteous and in whom all will be made alive. Hebrews reads the son of man of Psalm 8, crowned with glory, as Jesus. Irenaeus made this the centre of his teaching: Christ sums up Adam in himself and restores what was lost (Against Heresies 3.21.10; 5.21.1). P30

99. The Spirit is poured out. Joel 2:28-32, or 3:1-5; Acts 2:16-21. Explicit NT application to Pentecost. Joel promises that God will pour out his Spirit on all flesh. At Pentecost Peter says that this is what was spoken through the prophet Joel. Irenaeus and Cyril of Jerusalem repeat him (Against Heresies 3.12.1; Catechetical Lectures 17). Peter quotes the signs in heaven and the day of the Lord as well: the last days began at Pentecost, and they end with Christ’s return.

100. David’s fallen tent is restored and Gentiles are included. Amos 9:11-12; Acts 15:13-18. Explicit NT application to the Church. Amos promises that God will raise up the fallen tent of David. At the council of Jerusalem James quotes the passage in its Greek form, which speaks of the rest of mankind seeking the Lord, where the received Hebrew speaks of possessing the remnant of Edom. He uses it to show that the Gentiles who turn to God belong to the promised restoration. The apostles thus teach that the kingdom of David is restored in the risen Christ and that its people is the Church, Jews and Gentiles together. Irenaeus cites James’s words (Against Heresies 3.12.14). P36

7. Four questions the Fathers answered

Isaiah 7:14: whose child, and what does the word mean? The sign is given to King Ahaz when he fears two hostile kings, and the verses that follow say that their lands will be deserted before the child is grown. Hebrew almah means a young woman of marriageable age. It is used of unmarried girls (Genesis 24:43; Exodus 2:8), though it is not the technical word for a virgin. The Jewish translators of the Septuagint, working before Christ, rendered it parthenos, virgin, and Matthew quotes that wording of the virginal conception of Jesus. [P05, P06]

The objection is as old as the second century. Trypho tells Justin that the Hebrew means young woman and that the child is Hezekiah. Justin replies that a young woman bearing a child in the ordinary way would be no sign at all (Dialogue with Trypho 43, 66-67, 84), and Tertullian adds that the child’s name, God-with-us, fits no one but Christ (Against Marcion 3.12). Irenaeus adds that the Greek translation was made by Jews, long before Christians had any stake in the matter (Against Heresies 3.21). The Fathers knew that the oracle was spoken to Ahaz. They hold that the sign reaches past his day: the names given to the child here and in Isaiah 9:6 fit no son of Ahaz, and the prophecy has its full and intended sense in Christ, born of the Virgin Mary. This is the reading of the Fathers.

Isaiah 53: who is the servant? The servant poem begins at Isaiah 52:13. Elsewhere in this part of Isaiah, Israel is called God’s servant, as in 41:8 and 49:3. Yet the servant also has a mission to Israel (49:5-6), suffers although he is innocent, and is struck for the sin of the prophet’s own people (53:8). He is therefore distinguished from the people for whom he suffers. The New Testament says who he is: when the Ethiopian official asks of whom the prophet speaks, Philip begins from this passage and tells him the good news of Jesus (Acts 8:34-35). [P14, P24]

The history of interpretation is worth knowing. In the third century Origen reports that a Jewish opponent in debate referred the servant to the whole people in dispersion, and he answers from the text: the servant is led to death for the sins of God’s people, so he cannot be that people (Against Celsus 1.55). The collective reading is therefore ancient, and was not invented by the medieval commentator Rashi. Ancient Jewish tradition also knew a messianic reading: the Talmud applies Isaiah 53:4 to the Messiah (Sanhedrin 98b). Both are facts of history. [P37, P38]

The Christian reading is that of the apostles and of the Fathers who followed them: Clement of Rome (1 Clement 16), Justin (First Apology 50-51; Dialogue with Trypho 13) and those after them. The servant is Christ, who suffered for the sins of all, was buried, and lives. The poem does not give his name or his dates. It gives the meaning of his death, centuries beforehand, in a text whose date before Christ the Great Isaiah Scroll puts beyond doubt.

Psalm 22: did the Hebrew say pierced? The received Hebrew wording in verse 17, usually English verse 16, contains ka’ari, like a lion, in a syntactically difficult clause about hands and feet. The ancient Greek version has a verb meaning dug, often rendered pierced in this context. A fragment from Nahal Hever is important to the debate, but its lettering, spelling, and the proposed underlying Hebrew form do not remove every difficulty. A scholarly defence of pierced exists; other scholars defend alternative readings or reconstruct corruption in the transmitted text. P21

Two things are clear whatever the original wording of that clause. First, the application of the psalm to the Passion does not rest on one word: the New Testament quotes or echoes its opening cry, the mockery and the division of the garments, and Jesus prayed it on the cross. Second, the New Testament does not quote the hands-and-feet clause. Justin is the first to do so, in the Greek form, and he reads the whole psalm as a prophecy of the crucifixion (Dialogue with Trypho 97-106). The psalm moves from abandonment to rescue and to the worship of God by all nations, and Justin and the Fathers after him read that whole movement as Christ’s death, his resurrection and the conversion of the Gentiles. The textual history is more complicated than either the claim that Christians invented the wording or the claim that archaeology has settled it. P20

Daniel 9: the seventy weeks. Daniel is told that Jeremiah’s seventy years are to be understood as seventy weeks of years, 490 years, decreed to finish transgression, put an end to sin, atone for iniquity and bring in everlasting righteousness; that an anointed one will be cut off; and that the city and the sanctuary will be destroyed. Many modern commentators refer the last week to the persecution under Antiochus IV, the killing of the high priest Onias III in 171 BC and the desecration of the Temple in 167-164 BC. Those events did not end sin or bring in everlasting righteousness, and Jesus speaks of Daniel’s desolating abomination as still to come in his own day (Matthew 24:15). P26

The Fathers read the prophecy of Christ, and most of them as already fulfilled in him. Tertullian counts the weeks down to his birth and Passion and to the destruction of Jerusalem under Vespasian (Against the Jews 8). Eusebius identifies the Holy of Holies who is anointed as Christ, places the confirming of the covenant in his ministry and the end of sacrifice at his death, when the veil of the Temple was torn, and sees the desolation in the Roman destruction that followed (Demonstration of the Gospel 8.2). Athanasius argues that prophecy and kingship ceased in Israel once the Holy of Holies had come (On the Incarnation 39-40). They begin the count at different points (Tertullian from the first year of Darius, when Daniel received the vision; Julius Africanus, whom Eusebius quotes, from the twentieth year of Artaxerxes, when Nehemiah was sent to rebuild Jerusalem) and end it at different points, and no single calculation was agreed among them. What these three hold in common is the substance: the weeks ran out in the first century, the prophecy is fulfilled in Jesus, his death made the atonement it promised, and the sanctuary fell within a generation.

The prophecy should not be presented as a calculation exact to the day. Anyone who offers such a figure should show the starting decree, the calendar and the rules of counting that were used. Its force lies elsewhere: a text older than Christ set the coming of the Anointed, the atonement for sin and the end of the Temple within one bounded period, and all three came within it.

8. Other connections, and claims that should not be made

  • The creator language applied to the Son: Psalm 102:25-27 in Hebrews 1:10-12, and angelic worship in Hebrews 1:6 using scriptural Greek traditions related to Deuteronomy 32:43 and Psalm 97:7. These are major Christological applications. They state who Jesus is, the eternal Son through whom all things were made; they are not predictions of particular events in his earthly life. P04
  • Wisdom before creation: Proverbs 8:22-31, Wisdom 7:22-8:1, John 1:1-18, and 1 Corinthians 1:24 inform the Christian teaching that the Son exists before creation: Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God. These texts are not a birth announcement. Both sides of the Arian controversy argued from Proverbs 8:22, and the Council of Nicaea (325) settled the question: the Son is begotten, not made.
  • Job’s redeemer: Job 19:25-27. The Hebrew is difficult and the New Testament does not cite the passage, but Clement of Rome already cites Job for the resurrection of the body (1 Clement 26), and later Fathers read the redeemer as Christ.
  • Wounds in a friend’s house: Zechariah 13:6 is sometimes applied to Jesus’s wounds. In the immediate context, the speaker belongs to a discussion of discredited prophets. It cannot responsibly be quoted while omitting verses 2-5.
  • The desired one of the nations: Haggai 2:6-9 has been read as a title of Christ, chiefly through the Latin Bible. The Hebrew is commonly understood as the treasures of the nations coming to adorn the temple, and the New Testament does not quote the phrase of Jesus, though Hebrews 12:26-27 uses the shaking of heaven and earth. The promise that the glory of the later house would surpass the former (Haggai 2:9) is fulfilled in Christ, who entered that Temple and is himself greater than the Temple (Matthew 12:6).
  • Joseph betrayed and exalted: Joseph in Genesis 37-50 is sold by his brothers, counted as dead, raised to the king’s right hand, and becomes the saviour of his family and of the nations. He is a type of Christ, and Stephen retells his story in that light (Acts 7:9-14). It is one figure, and should not be counted as a list of separate predictions.
  • Intercession for transgressors: Isaiah 53:12 contributes to Christian accounts of Jesus’s intercession, including Hebrews 7:25. Luke 23:34 supplies the prayer for executioners in many manuscripts, but its omission in other early witnesses means that its textual status must be acknowledged.
  • Judas’s vacant office: Acts 1:20 applies Psalm 69:25 and Psalm 109:8 to Judas and his replacement. Peter says that the Holy Spirit spoke these words beforehand through David concerning Judas (Acts 1:16). David’s laments over his betrayers are fulfilled in the betrayal of David’s Son.
  • The new people of God: Hosea 1:10 and 2:23 are applied to the Church in Romans 9:25-26 and 1 Peter 2:10; Isaiah 65:1-2 in Romans 10:20-21; Isaiah 54:13 in John 6:45. Hosea’s words were first spoken of Israel’s restoration after judgment. Paul and Peter teach that they are fulfilled in the Church, called from Jews and Gentiles alike (Romans 9:24): those who were not God’s people are now his people. Peter gives the Church Israel’s own titles: a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation (1 Peter 2:9-10).
  • Numbers, names, codes, and calendar combinations: acrostics, Bible-code searches, and numerical associations can produce patterns after the target is known. A genuine evidential test would need a predeclared rule, independently fixed data, and comparison with how often similar patterns appear elsewhere.

9. Jesus’s own predictions, arranged by the events they concern

These are Jesus’s own predictions as the Gospels record them, arranged by the events they concern. In particular, a Gospel written after an event is evidence that its author reports an earlier prediction; it is not automatically an independently dated pre-event document. That is a limitation of the record, not proof that Jesus could not have anticipated an event.

These are narrated episodes of knowledge or instruction, not independent forecasts documented before their reported fulfillments. Predictions framed in parables, such as the rejected son and the destroyed tenants in Mark 12:1-12, likewise need to be distinguished from datable public oracles.

10. Promises fulfilled in Christ and completed at his return

The prophets join great promises to the coming of the Messiah: an end to war (Isaiah 2:2-4; Micah 4:1-4), the knowledge of God filling the earth (Isaiah 11), a righteous king under whom Judah and Israel dwell secure (Jeremiah 23), and the gathering of God’s people under one shepherd, with his sanctuary among them for ever (Ezekiel 37:21-28). Jesus did not establish these as a political order during his ministry. The objection is an old one. Trypho puts it to Justin, and Justin answers that the prophets foretold two comings of the Christ, the first in humility and suffering, the second in glory (Dialogue with Trypho 14, 32, 110).

The answer is the one the Fathers gave. The kingdom has come in Christ, is present now in his Church, and will be complete when he returns. The promises are kept in that order. The peace of the Messiah is first of all peace with God, and peace between the peoples he reconciles in one body (Ephesians 2:14-18). Justin and Irenaeus point to Christians of every nation who have turned from war as the beginning of what Isaiah and Micah foretold (Dialogue with Trypho 110; Against Heresies 4.34.4). The ingathering is the gathering of the Church from Israel and the nations under the one shepherd (John 10:16; 11:52). The sanctuary set among God’s people for ever is the risen body of Christ and the Church built on him (John 2:19-21; 1 Corinthians 3:16); it is not a building to be raised again in Jerusalem. All of this awaits its completion in the new heaven and the new earth, where death is no more and God dwells with his people (Revelation 21:1-4, 22). The same is true of the resurrection of the dead (1 Corinthians 15) and of the day when every tongue confesses that Jesus Christ is Lord (Philippians 2:10-11).

Two things follow. First, this reading does not take modern political events as the fulfillment of prophecy, and that includes the founding of the modern State of Israel in 1948. The messianic hope is not realised within history as an earthly kingdom. Some early Fathers, Justin and Irenaeus among them, expected a thousand-year reign of Christ on earth (Dialogue with Trypho 80-81; Against Heresies 5.32-36); most later Fathers, Augustine among them, did not follow them (City of God 20.7). The idea of a separate prophetic programme for a national Israel alongside the Church is not found in the Fathers; it dates from the nineteenth century. Second, God has not rejected the Jewish people (Romans 11:1-2): the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable (Romans 11:29). Paul teaches that a hardening has come upon part of Israel until the full number of the Gentiles has come in, and that so all Israel will be saved (Romans 11:25-26). The Catholic Catechism puts it plainly: the glorious coming of the Messiah awaits his recognition by all Israel (Catechism of the Catholic Church 674). Christians are bound to the Jewish people, the first to hear the word of God (Romans 3:1-2; 9:4-5), and the Second Vatican Council rejects all hatred and contempt for them (Nostra Aetate 4).

11. What the prophecies establish

The Dead Sea Scrolls decisively prevent the entire Christian prophecy argument from being dismissed as Christians inventing Isaiah after Jesus. The Israel Museum dates the Great Isaiah Scroll to approximately 125 BC; it contains the book’s full sequence of chapters. Earlier scriptural texts genuinely existed before the Christian movement. That settles the question of date: the prophecies were written before the events. P41

A historian testing any one entry asks several questions. Was the earlier text fixed before the event? Is the event well attested? Is the connection a direct prophecy or a type? Could those involved, knowing the text, have acted on it deliberately, as Jesus did when he rode into Jerusalem on a donkey? The answers differ from entry to entry, and the catalogue marks them.

This also explains why popular probability demonstrations fail to provide a rigorous proof. One cannot responsibly multiply invented odds for ancestry, birthplace, betrayal, payment, and execution as though all were independent random experiments. Several claims derive from the same Gospel; several details from one earlier poem; some are actions a participant could intentionally perform; and some are selected after the outcome is known. A large denominator does not repair ungrounded input probabilities.

The Christian claim is larger than any single match. It is that the whole of Israel’s Scripture, its promises, its kings, its sacrifices, its sufferers and its hopes, converges on one person and is fulfilled in him and in his Church. Jesus taught his disciples to read the Scriptures in this way (Luke 24:27, 44-47), the apostles preached it, and the Fathers from Clement and Justin onward expounded it. This is the reading the catalogue follows. Historical method on its own can establish part of the case: that the texts are older than Jesus, that his followers read them of him from the earliest sources that survive, and that the reading was shared across the early Church from Rome to Syria. That God spoke through the prophets and brought their words to fulfillment in Christ is held by faith, and the Church confesses it in the Creed: he rose again on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures.
