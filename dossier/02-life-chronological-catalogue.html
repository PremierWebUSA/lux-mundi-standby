Jesus’s life: a chronological catalogue of the canonical narratives

2026The Jesus Archive research dossier· 40 min read

A numbered catalogue of the narrative units and teaching blocks in the four Gospels, from birth to ascension, with the New Testament’s accounts of the risen Jesus, chronological anchors, and a comparison of the resurrection sources.

Read the text
About this chapter

This chapter indexes the major episodes and teaching blocks of the four canonical Gospels in a broad life order, followed by the New Testament passages that present Jesus acting from heaven. Each numbered entry marks a distinct item in the Gospel record, with its references, for the reader to weigh on its own evidence.

It opens with the chronological anchors (birth around 6-4 BC, the ministry dated by Luke 3:1, crucifixion under Pilate in about 30 or AD 33) and sets out the questions still open. The sequence ordinarily follows Mark, keeps John’s festival itinerary as a separate strand, and ends with a table comparing what each resurrection source actually reports.

How to read this chronology

This catalogue indexes the major narrative units and teaching blocks in the four canonical Gospels, followed by the New Testament’s accounts of the risen or exalted Jesus. It does not claim that every Gospel incident can be dated, that every repeated saying was spoken only once, or that the surviving books record everything Jesus did. John 20:30 and 21:25 expressly say their account is selective. A numbered entry means a distinguishable item in the literary record, not an independently verified historical event.

The sequence follows a broad life outline. Within the ministry it ordinarily follows Mark’s sequence, supplements Matthew and Luke, and keeps John’s festival itinerary visible as a separate strand. This avoids silently turning four theological biographies into one modern diary. Parallel passages may preserve versions of one tradition; listing four references does not automatically create four independent witnesses.

The terms Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John below identify the Gospel texts without deciding their authorship. Reference abbreviations are Mt, Mk, Lk, Jn, Acts, and 1 Cor. Scripture references are primary citations. The supplementary sources L01-L26 provide checked translations, textual notes, and academic discussion.

Chronological anchors and what remains uncertain

Birth: approximately 6-4 BC is a common working estimate, based chiefly on placing the birth before Herod the Great’s conventionally dated death in 4 BC. It is an estimate, not an independently preserved birth registration. Matthew 2 and Luke 1 place their infancy settings under Herod. Luke 2 also connects the birth to Quirinius’s census, independently dated to AD 6. The roughly ten-year discrepancy is real; proposed earlier governorships or alternative translations have not produced an accepted historical reconciliation. There is also no corroborated empire-wide order requiring everyone to return to a remote ancestral town. L01L02

Beginning of the public ministry: Luke 3:1 dates John’s preaching to Tiberius’s fifteenth regnal year, commonly calculated as 28/AD 29, with some variation depending on regnal reckoning. Luke calls Jesus approximately thirty, which is an approximate age, not an exact birthday. Pontius Pilate governed Judea in AD 26-36; Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea in 4 BC-AD 39. These rulers constrain the broad setting. L03

Duration: the Synoptic Gospels narrate only the final Passover explicitly during Jesus’s ministry, whereas John names Passovers at 2:13, 6:4, and 11:55. John’s sequence therefore spans at least about two years between its first and last named Passovers. The familiar three-year ministry is a reconstruction, not a number supplied by any Gospel. Counting an unnamed feast in John 5:1 as another Passover is an additional assumption.

Death: a Friday around Passover, during Pilate’s governorship, is the central canonical framework. 30 and AD 33 are leading proposals; the exact year and calendar date remain disputed. Astronomical compatibility establishes possible dates only after assumptions about the ancient calendar and Gospel chronology. In the Synoptics the final meal is presented as Passover; John places the authorities’ Passover meal ahead of them during the trial and calls the sentencing day Passover preparation. Mark 15:25 places crucifixion at the third hour, while John 19:14 places the sentencing scene around the sixth. Alternate calendars and timekeeping schemes are proposed harmonisations, not facts independently established about this particular execution. L04L05L06L24L26

Language and social setting: Jesus was a Galilean Jew, identified with Nazareth and an artisan’s household. Aramaic was probably his main spoken language; Hebrew knowledge is plausible, and some Greek is possible, but precise fluency cannot be recovered. The Gospels are Greek literary accounts, not recordings in his everyday speech. Aramaic expressions such as talitha koum, ephphatha, and abba survive within them. L07

A. Before the public ministry: theological origins, birth, and childhood

1. Pre-existence and incarnation. John identifies Jesus with the divine Word through whom creation came into being, now embodied in human life (Jn 1:1-18). Philippians 2:6-11, Colossians 1:15-20, and Hebrews 1:1-4 offer related but distinct accounts of his heavenly status. These are theological claims about origins, not datable earthly episodes. L08

2. The genealogies. Matthew traces Jesus through David and Abraham, using three schematic groups of fourteen; Luke traces him through David to Adam and God (Mt 1:1-17; Lk 3:23-38). Their post-David lines differ, including Solomon versus Nathan and Jacob versus Heli. Calling one Mary’s genealogy is a traditional reconciliation, not an explicit statement in either text. L03L09

3. John’s birth announced. Gabriel tells the priest Zechariah that Elizabeth will bear John, who will prepare a people for the Lord. Zechariah becomes unable to speak after questioning the announcement (Lk 1:5-25). This establishes John’s precursor role within Luke’s infancy narrative; no contemporary external record confirms the scene.

4. Mary’s annunciation. Gabriel visits Mary in Nazareth, promises a Davidic ruler, and explains conception through the Holy Spirit (Lk 1:26-38). Luke and Matthew affirm virginal conception, but narrate different announcement scenes. Mark, John, and Paul’s undisputed letters do not narrate such a conception.

5. Mary visits Elizabeth. Elizabeth blesses Mary; the unborn John moves in her womb; Mary responds with the Magnificat (Lk 1:39-56). The hymn concerns God’s reversal of power and wealth as well as Israel’s covenant. These speeches are part of Luke’s literary record, not recoverable transcripts.

6. John’s birth and Zechariah’s hymn. John is born and circumcised; Zechariah recovers speech and pronounces the Benedictus (Lk 1:57-80). John later lives in the wilderness. Luke interweaves the births to establish continuity between John’s prophetic work and Jesus’s mission. L10

7. Joseph’s dream and naming instruction. In Matthew, Joseph considers ending his betrothal after Mary’s pregnancy; an angel tells him to receive her and name the child Jesus (Mt 1:18-25). Matthew connects the conception to Isaiah 7:14 and calls Jesus Emmanuel. The dream is Matthew’s account, not a second version of Gabriel’s visit to Mary.

8. The birth at Bethlehem. Luke brings Mary and Joseph from Nazareth to Bethlehem through the census and places the child in a manger (Lk 2:1-7). Matthew begins with Jesus already born at Bethlehem (Mt 2:1). Neither supplies a day or month. December 25 is a later liturgical date, not a Gospel birth date.

9. Shepherds and angels. Luke describes shepherds receiving an angelic announcement, visiting the manger, and publicising what they heard (Lk 2:8-20). No magi appear in this scene. The familiar combined Christmas tableau joins material from two different narratives.

10. Circumcision and naming. On the eighth day, Jesus is circumcised and named (Lk 2:21). The act situates him within Jewish covenantal practice. Jesus/Yeshua is a personal name; Christ/Messiah is a title meaning anointed one, not a surname.

11. Presentation, Simeon, and Anna. The parents bring Jesus to Jerusalem; Simeon praises God’s salvation and predicts opposition and Mary’s grief; Anna recognises the child’s significance (Lk 2:22-38). Luke then says the family returns to Nazareth after completing the required observances (2:39-40). L11

12. The magi, star, and Herod. Matthew describes eastern magi locating the child through a star and scriptural consultation, offering gold, incense, and myrrh (Mt 2:1-12). He does not say they were kings, number them as three, or give their names. Astronomical proposals do not independently identify Matthew’s star. L25

13. Flight to Egypt and killings at Bethlehem. Joseph escapes with the family after a warning; Herod orders the killing of boys aged two and under (Mt 2:13-18). These incidents are absent from Luke and other surviving contemporary sources. Matthew structures the story through Exodus imagery and scriptural fulfillment.

14. Return and settlement at Nazareth. After Herod’s death, Joseph avoids Judea under Archelaus and settles in Nazareth (Mt 2:19-23). In Luke, Nazareth was already the family’s home. Reading the Egyptian journey into Luke’s return is possible as a harmonisation but is not narrated by Luke.

15. Jesus at twelve. Luke alone places the twelve-year-old Jesus among teachers during a Jerusalem Passover visit. His parents find him after searching; he returns home obediently and continues to mature (Lk 2:41-52). This is the sole canonical childhood episode after infancy. L11

16. The largely unrecorded years. Mark calls Jesus an artisan and names his mother, brothers James, Joses, Judas, and Simon, with unnamed sisters (Mk 6:3; compare Mt 13:55-56). Traditions disagree about whether these relatives are siblings, step-siblings, or cousins. The canonical record supplies no marriage, children, formal rabbinic apprenticeship, or travels to India or Britain.

B. John the Baptist and the beginning of the ministry

17. John’s preaching and baptism. John calls for repentance, baptizes, warns of judgment, and announces a stronger coming figure (Mt 3:1-12; Mk 1:1-8; Lk 3:1-18; Jn 1:19-28). John’s historical existence has separate support in Josephus; that does not authenticate every speech assigned to him.

18. Jesus’s baptism. Jesus receives baptism; a heavenly voice and Spirit identify him as Son (Mt 3:13-17; Mk 1:9-11; Lk 3:21-22). John 1:29-34 gives the Baptist’s retrospective testimony about the descending Spirit without narrating the baptism itself. Historians commonly regard Jesus’s connection with John’s movement as especially well grounded.

19. Wilderness temptation. The Synoptics describe a wilderness period and testing by Satan (Mt 4:1-11; Mk 1:12-13; Lk 4:1-13). Matthew and Luke expand the episode with scriptural dialogue but arrange the last two temptations differently. The forty-day pattern evokes Israel’s wilderness and Moses/Elijah traditions.

20. John’s identification of Jesus. John calls Jesus God’s lamb and bears witness to his priority and the Spirit (Jn 1:29-34). The Fourth Gospel develops this identification into its Passion symbolism. It should not be read as a transcript independently corroborating every detail of the Synoptic baptism narratives.

21. First followers in John’s account. Andrew and another disciple follow Jesus; Andrew brings Simon, renamed Cephas; Philip brings Nathanael (Jn 1:35-51). This differs from the lakeside call stories. Nathanael is not explicitly identified as Bartholomew in the New Testament.

22. Water becomes wine at Cana. At a wedding Jesus supplies wine from water jars after his mother’s intervention (Jn 2:1-11). John calls it the first sign and links it to revealed glory and disciples’ belief. The Gospel supplies neither bridegroom’s identity nor a historical wedding date.

23. A brief stay at Capernaum. Jesus goes there with his mother, brothers, and disciples (Jn 2:12). The other Gospels also associate Capernaum with his activity. Calling a particular excavated house Jesus’s residence goes beyond what this literary notice establishes.

24. The Temple action in John’s early chronology. Jesus expels commercial activity and speaks of destroying and raising the Temple; the narrator interprets this as his body (Jn 2:13-25). The Synoptics place a comparable action in the final Jerusalem visit. Two separate clearings are possible, but the texts do not require that historical solution.

25. Nicodemus visits. Jesus speaks about birth from above, Spirit, heavenly testimony, the lifted-up Son, and God’s love (Jn 3:1-21). The Greek permits a misunderstanding about birth both again and from above. The transition between Jesus’s speech and the evangelist’s exposition is not marked by ancient quotation marks.

26. Baptizing activity in Judea. Jesus’s movement operates alongside John’s before John’s imprisonment (Jn 3:22-24). John 4:1-2 clarifies that the disciples, rather than Jesus personally, perform the baptisms. This is an explicit correction to an overly literal reading of the earlier wording.

27. John’s final witness. John’s disciples raise a rivalry concern; John uses bridegroom imagery and affirms Jesus’s increasing role (Jn 3:25-36). The section emphasises hierarchy between the two missions. As elsewhere in John, precisely where direct speech ends is interpretively uncertain.

28. John’s imprisonment and Jesus’s departure. The Synoptics connect the beginning of Galilean proclamation with John’s arrest (Mt 4:12; Mk 1:14; Lk 3:19-20; 4:14). John supplies another departure context, competition and attention over baptizing (Jn 4:1-3). These are different narrative explanations, not a secure day-by-day itinerary.

29. The Samaritan woman. At Jacob’s well Jesus speaks with a Samaritan woman about living water, her relationships, worship, and his identity; her testimony brings townspeople (Jn 4:4-42). John does not name her or call her a prostitute. The harvest discourse to the disciples forms part of this episode.

30. The royal official’s son. Jesus heals at a distance after an official requests help for his dying son in Capernaum (Jn 4:43-54). Similarities to the centurion’s servant do not prove they are the same episode; John calls this a second sign associated with the return from Judea.

C. Galilean proclamation, discipleship, and works of power

31. The kingdom proclamation. Jesus announces God’s approaching kingdom and calls for repentance and belief (Mt 4:17; Mk 1:14-15; Lk 4:14-15). Matthew places his move to Capernaum within Isaiah’s imagery of light in Galilee (Mt 4:13-16). This introduces the ministry’s central theme.

32. Nazareth rejection in Luke’s introductory position. Jesus reads Isaiah in the synagogue, applies the promise to the present, invokes Elijah and Elisha, and faces hostility (Lk 4:16-30). Mark and Matthew locate the hometown rejection later (Mk 6:1-6; Mt 13:53-58). An exact chronology cannot simply be extracted from both placements.

33. Calling the fishermen. Simon, Andrew, James, and John leave their work to follow Jesus (Mt 4:18-22; Mk 1:16-20). Luke places Simon’s call around an extraordinary catch after Jesus teaches from a boat (Lk 5:1-11). John’s first-followers tradition has a different setting and should remain visible alongside these versions.

34. Exorcism in Capernaum. An unclean spirit identifies Jesus; Jesus silences and expels it during synagogue teaching (Mk 1:21-28; Lk 4:31-37). The episode portrays authority over teaching and spirits. It does not permit a modern medical diagnosis of the afflicted man.

35. Simon’s mother-in-law and evening healings. Jesus removes her fever and subsequently treats numerous sick or possessed people (Mt 8:14-17; Mk 1:29-34; Lk 4:38-41). Simon’s marriage is implicit in the relationship. Matthew connects these healings with Isaiah 53:4.

36. Solitary prayer and a preaching circuit. Jesus withdraws to pray, resists remaining in one locality, and teaches through Galilean synagogues (Mk 1:35-39; Lk 4:42-44; Mt 4:23-25). General summaries report many unnamed healings. Such notices make any numerical total of every miracle impossible even within the Gospels.

37. Cleansing a man with a skin disease. Jesus touches and restores him and directs him to priestly verification (Mt 8:1-4; Mk 1:40-45; Lk 5:12-16). The biblical disease category traditionally translated leprosy is broader than modern Hansen’s disease. Mark emphasises the publicity that follows despite the instruction to silence.

38. The paralytic and forgiveness. Companions bring a paralysed man; Jesus pronounces forgiveness and restores mobility amid accusations about divine authority (Mt 9:1-8; Mk 2:1-12; Lk 5:17-26). Mark and Luke include access through the roof. The story links a visible healing claim to an invisible claim about forgiveness.

39. Levi/Matthew and table fellowship. Jesus calls a tax collector and eats with socially stigmatised people, explaining his mission through the physician image (Mt 9:9-13; Mk 2:13-17; Lk 5:27-32). Matthew names the called man Matthew; Mark and Luke name him Levi. Equating them is traditional, not an explicit cross-identification.

40. Fasting, the bridegroom, and new wine. Challenged about fasting, Jesus invokes a present bridegroom and a future loss, then offers patch and wineskin comparisons (Mt 9:14-17; Mk 2:18-22; Lk 5:33-39). These mini-parables articulate the tension between his mission and existing practices.

41. Grain on the Sabbath. The disciples pluck grain; Jesus cites David and argues about lawful provision and the Son of Man’s Sabbath authority (Mt 12:1-8; Mk 2:23-28; Lk 6:1-5). This is a Jewish dispute over interpretation, not evidence that all Jews shared one monolithic position.

42. The withered hand. Jesus heals in a synagogue on the Sabbath, provoking hostile planning (Mt 12:9-14; Mk 3:1-6; Lk 6:6-11). Mark names Pharisees and Herodians cooperating. The narrator’s account of a plot is not equivalent to possessing minutes of an actual meeting.

43. Crowds and withdrawal. Jesus draws followers from several regions, heals, and restrains spirits from public identification (Mt 12:15-21; Mk 3:7-12). Matthew applies the servant passage of Isaiah 42. The geographical list describes the narrative’s widening reach; it cannot establish precise crowd sizes.

44. Appointment of the Twelve. Jesus selects a group to accompany him and share preaching and exorcism (Mt 10:1-4; Mk 3:13-19; Lk 6:12-16). Lists differ in some names, especially Thaddaeus/Judas of James. The Twelve symbolise Israel; their existence is earlier than the written Gospel narratives (1 Cor 15:5).

45. The major ethical sermon. Matthew 5-7 gathers beatitudes, salt/light, Torah interpretation, anger, sexual fidelity, oaths, nonretaliation, love of enemies, almsgiving, prayer, fasting, wealth, anxiety, judgment, discernment, and doing God’s will. Luke 6:20-49 preserves a related sermon with blessings and woes, generosity, enemy-love, and foundations. Their lengths, wording, and settings differ. These are organised literary discourses, not demonstrably verbatim transcripts of one occasion.

46. The centurion’s servant. Jesus responds to a Gentile officer’s appeal and praises his faith (Mt 8:5-13; Lk 7:1-10). Matthew has direct dialogue; Luke uses Jewish elders and friends as intermediaries. Treating the versions as narratively identical conceals a real difference in presentation.

47. The widow’s son at Nain. Jesus interrupts a funeral and restores the son to his mother (Lk 7:11-17). The scene evokes Elijah and Elisha and leads the crowd to acclaim a prophet. Only Luke records this particular raising.

48. John’s question from prison. Messengers ask whether Jesus is the expected one; Jesus points to healings and good news for the poor (Mt 11:2-6; Lk 7:18-23). The response recalls Isaiah’s restoration language rather than offering a simple title. It also preserves uncertainty within John’s circle.

49. Jesus evaluates John and his generation. John is praised, compared with the expected messenger, and contrasted with an unresponsive generation (Mt 11:7-19; Lk 7:24-35). Matthew also records woes on towns and Jesus’s invitation to the burdened (Mt 11:20-30; compare Lk 10:13-16, 21-22).

50. An unnamed woman anoints Jesus. At a Pharisee’s meal a woman weeps over and anoints his feet; Jesus tells the two-debtors parable and pronounces forgiveness (Lk 7:36-50). Luke does not identify her as Mary Magdalene or as Mary of Bethany. Conflating the women imports later tradition into the text.

51. Women sustain the mission. Luke names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Susanna, and others who support the group materially (Lk 8:1-3). Mary’s seven expelled demons are mentioned without an episode narrative. Possession is not a statement that she was sexually immoral.

52. Beelzebul controversy. Opponents attribute exorcisms to demonic power; Jesus argues from a divided kingdom and the binding of a strong man (Mt 12:22-37; Mk 3:20-30; Lk 11:14-23). Warnings about blasphemy and harmful speech accompany the controversy. Its different placements do not yield a single secure date.

53. Family and discipleship. His mother and brothers seek him; Jesus identifies those who do God’s will as his family (Mt 12:46-50; Mk 3:31-35; Lk 8:19-21). Mark 3:20-21 also reports an attempt by those close to him to restrain him, although the precise scope of that group is debated.

54. Signs, Jonah, and genuine hearing. Jesus answers a demand for a sign with Jonah and the queen of the South; related sayings concern returning spirits, lamps, and hearing God’s word (Mt 12:38-45; Lk 11:24-36). Matthew’s three-days analogy is more explicit than Luke’s formulation.

55. The sower and interpretations. Seed falls on different soils, explained as varied responses to the word (Mt 13:1-23; Mk 4:1-20; Lk 8:4-15). The surrounding discussion asks why Jesus speaks in parables and invokes Isaiah 6. Scholars dispute how much of each interpretation derives from Jesus or subsequent teaching.

56. Lamps, measures, and growing seed. Jesus speaks of disclosure, attentive hearing, and the measure used toward others (Mk 4:21-25; Lk 8:16-18). Mark alone supplies the seed that grows while the farmer sleeps (Mk 4:26-29), a picture of God’s kingdom progressing beyond human control.

57. Wheat and weeds. An enemy plants weeds among wheat; separation waits for harvest, followed by an explicit interpretation (Mt 13:24-30, 36-43). The explanation associates harvest with judgment. Whether parable and explanation have the same compositional history is a historical question, not settled by their shared placement.

58. Mustard seed and leaven. Small beginnings become extensive growth (Mt 13:31-33; Mk 4:30-34; Lk 13:18-21). Luke places the pair elsewhere in his travel narrative. A harmonised timeline should not pretend that their location is independently known.

59. Treasure, pearl, net, and the instructed scribe. Matthew’s parable collection closes with costly discovery, final separation, and old/new treasure (Mt 13:44-52). The tiny stories vary in emphasis; they are not interchangeable proofs of a single abstract doctrine.

60. Calming the storm. Jesus stills wind and water after frightened disciples wake him (Mt 8:23-27; Mk 4:35-41; Lk 8:22-25). The story asks who can exercise such authority. Matthew rearranges it relative to other Galilean material.

61. The Gerasene/Gadarene demoniac tradition. Demons leave afflicted people for a herd of pigs (Mt 8:28-34; Mk 5:1-20; Lk 8:26-39). Mark and Luke focus on one man; Matthew has two. Place-name manuscript variants and geography complicate precise site identification.

62. Jairus’s daughter and the woman with bleeding. One story interrupts another: a chronically bleeding woman touches Jesus; he then raises a synagogue leader’s daughter (Mt 9:18-26; Mk 5:21-43; Lk 8:40-56). Matthew compresses the sequence and has the father announce death initially; Mark and Luke report death later.

63. Two blind men and a man unable to speak. Matthew records restoration of sight to two men and an exorcism associated with speech (Mt 9:27-34). These are additional units within Matthew’s healing sequence, although some motifs resemble other healing traditions. They cannot simply be counted as externally corroborated separate cases.

64. The hometown rejection in Mark and Matthew. Familiarity with Jesus’s family contributes to disbelief; Jesus performs limited healing activity and comments on a prophet’s reception (Mk 6:1-6; Mt 13:53-58). This cross-refers to entry 32 rather than asserting a second independently established visit.

65. Sending the Twelve. Jesus commissions itinerant preaching, healing, and dependence on hospitality (Mt 10:5-42; Mk 6:7-13; Lk 9:1-6). Matthew expands the charge with persecution, family division, fearless testimony, and reception sayings. The immediate mission there is explicitly directed to Israel.

66. John the Baptist is executed. The Gospels recount Herod’s banquet, a dance, and a requested beheading (Mt 14:1-12; Mk 6:14-29; Lk 9:7-9). The dancer is unnamed in those passages. Josephus independently describes John’s execution but gives political suspicion as the motive; the two accounts should be compared, not equated.

67. The disciples return and Jesus withdraws. The apostles report; Jesus seeks rest, but crowds follow (Mk 6:30-34; Lk 9:10-11; Mt 14:13-14). The scene introduces compassionate teaching and feeding. Its location is described differently across the narratives.

68. Feeding the five thousand. Bread and fish supply a large crowd with leftovers (Mt 14:15-21; Mk 6:35-44; Lk 9:12-17; Jn 6:1-15). This is the only feeding miracle present in all four Gospels. John adds an attempted royal acclamation from which Jesus withdraws.

69. Walking on the sea. Jesus approaches a boat across the water (Mt 14:22-33; Mk 6:45-52; Jn 6:16-21). Matthew alone adds Peter’s attempt and rescue. John’s account ends with the boat at its destination; Luke does not include this episode.

70. Gennesaret healings. People recognise Jesus, bring patients, and seek contact with his clothing (Mt 14:34-36; Mk 6:53-56). This is a collective summary, not an itemised patient register. It should remain separate from enumerations of individually narrated miracles.

71. The bread-of-life discourse. John follows the feeding with teaching about heavenly bread, flesh and blood, life, and belief; many disciples withdraw, while Peter affirms allegiance (Jn 6:22-71). The discourse connects nourishment to Jesus’s identity. Its relation to Eucharistic practice is debated; it is not a Synoptic sermon transcript.

72. Purity and human tradition. A handwashing dispute develops into teaching about what defiles a person (Mt 15:1-20; Mk 7:1-23). Mark’s parenthetical conclusion about foods and Matthew’s formulation differ. Reading every later Christian dietary rule directly back into one historical conversation oversimplifies this dispute.

73. The Syrophoenician/Canaanite woman’s daughter. A Gentile woman persists through a difficult exchange and her daughter is delivered (Mt 15:21-28; Mk 7:24-30). Matthew and Mark describe her identity differently. The household-table metaphor exposes a question about the priority of Israel and extension beyond it.

74. A deaf man with impaired speech. Jesus uses touch and an Aramaic command, then urges silence (Mk 7:31-37). Mark alone narrates this individual healing; Matthew provides a broader healing summary before the next feeding (Mt 15:29-31).

75. Feeding the four thousand. Seven loaves supply another crowd (Mt 15:32-39; Mk 8:1-10). Mark explicitly distinguishes two feedings later (8:19-20). Historical analysis can still ask whether similar traditions developed into two narratives; neither automatic conflation nor automatic independence is warranted.

76. Demands for a sign and dangerous leaven. Jesus refuses opponents’ demands and warns disciples about corrupting influence (Mt 16:1-12; Mk 8:11-21; compare Lk 12:1). Matthew names Pharisees and Sadducees; Mark names Pharisees and Herod. Disciples initially mistake the warning for a bread problem.

77. The two-stage healing at Bethsaida. A blind man first sees indistinctly, then clearly (Mk 8:22-26). Mark alone preserves the gradual restoration. Its position before the disciples’ partial understanding of Jesus supports a literary reading, while not deciding whether the event occurred.

78. Peter’s confession and first Passion prediction. Peter identifies Jesus as Messiah; Jesus predicts rejection, death, and resurrection and rebukes Peter’s resistance (Mt 16:13-23; Mk 8:27-33; Lk 9:18-22). Matthew adds the rock, church, and keys sayings. The Gospels vary in how directly and early Jesus’s identity is recognised.

79. Taking up the cross. Jesus teaches self-denial, losing life to save it, judgment, and the coming kingdom (Mt 16:24-28; Mk 8:34-9:1; Lk 9:23-27). The saying about some hearers living to see the kingdom has several interpretations; its chronological difficulty should not be hidden.

80. The transfiguration. Peter, James, and John see Jesus with Moses and Elijah; a voice identifies the Son (Mt 17:1-13; Mk 9:2-13; Lk 9:28-36). Luke includes discussion of Jesus’s coming departure. Neither the Gospels nor this catalogue establish the mountain’s modern identification.

81. A boy with a destructive spirit. The disciples fail to help; Jesus restores the child and addresses belief or prayer (Mt 17:14-20; Mk 9:14-29; Lk 9:37-43). Some later manuscripts add fasting to Mark 9:29. Symptoms described by an ancient narrator should not be converted into a confident modern diagnosis.

82. Another death prediction and the Temple tax. Jesus again speaks of betrayal, death, and rising (Mt 17:22-23; Mk 9:30-32; Lk 9:43-45). Matthew alone adds payment through a coin in a fish’s mouth (Mt 17:24-27); the text gives Jesus’s instruction but does not narrate Peter actually catching the fish.

83. Greatness and a child. Jesus answers the disciples’ competition by placing a child before them and defining greatness through service or humility (Mt 18:1-5; Mk 9:33-37; Lk 9:46-48). The action belongs to the record of his teaching through enacted examples.

84. The outsider exorcist and stumbling blocks. Jesus discourages suppression of someone acting in his name and warns against causing vulnerable believers to stumble (Mk 9:38-50; Lk 9:49-50; 17:1-2; Mt 18:6-9). Salt, fire, and severe bodily metaphors express urgency, not a literal program of self-injury.

85. Restoring and forgiving community members. Matthew gathers the lost sheep, reconciliation procedures, agreement in prayer, repeated forgiveness, and the unforgiving servant (Mt 18:10-35). The sheep appears again in Luke 15 with another audience and emphasis. This is one major discourse block, containing several distinguishable teachings.

D. The journey toward Jerusalem and Luke’s extensive travel material

Luke 9:51-19:27 gives the journey a theological direction but little information sufficient for a precise travel map. Much of the following material cannot be assigned to individual calendar dates. Some sayings parallel episodes already located elsewhere by Matthew or Mark.

86. Departure and a Samaritan refusal. Jesus turns toward Jerusalem; a Samaritan village refuses hospitality; he rejects the disciples’ desire for destructive retaliation (Lk 9:51-56). Some manuscripts expand the rebuke. The scene concerns a village in this story, not a verdict on all Samaritans.

87. Would-be followers. Jesus answers aspirants with sayings about homelessness, burying the dead, and not turning back (Lk 9:57-62; compare Mt 8:18-22). Their demanding language subordinates ordinary obligations to the kingdom’s urgency. Exact biographical details about the aspirants are unavailable.

88. Mission of the seventy/seventy-two. Luke narrates a further mission, its instructions, woes over resistant towns, the disciples’ return, Jesus’s vision of Satan’s fall, and thanksgiving (Lk 10:1-24). The number varies in manuscripts. Matthew distributes parallels through other contexts.

89. The good Samaritan. A lawyer’s question leads to the wounded traveller’s story and an outsider’s practical mercy (Lk 10:25-37). The command to become a neighbor matters more than identifying a narrowly bounded eligible group. It is a parable, not a documented roadside case.

90. Martha and Mary. Jesus visits two sisters and defends Mary’s attention to his teaching amid Martha’s concern about hospitality (Lk 10:38-42). Luke does not name the village here. Identifying it as Bethany draws on John’s separate information about sisters with these names.

91. Teaching prayer. Jesus gives the Lord’s Prayer, tells of a midnight request, and encourages asking, seeking, and confidence in God’s giving (Lk 11:1-13; compare Mt 6:9-13; 7:7-11). The prayer exists in different Gospel forms; later liturgical wording incorporates additional transmission history.

92. Woes over distorted religion. At a meal Jesus criticises external cleansing without justice, ostentation, burdens, and the suppression of knowledge (Lk 11:37-54). Matthew gathers related material into chapter 23. These texts participate in internal Jewish and later community disputes; they are not neutral ethnography.

93. Hypocrisy, fear, and the rich fool. Jesus warns against concealment and greed, promises divine care, and tells of a prosperous man who dies after planning larger barns (Lk 12:1-34). Much anxiety/wealth teaching parallels Matthew 6 and 10. The inheritance dispute is only briefly described.

94. Readiness, division, and interpreting the moment. Servants must remain ready; an entrusted manager faces accountability; the mission brings conflict; listeners must read the times and settle disputes (Lk 12:35-59). Some parables reappear in Matthew’s final discourse. These are overlapping traditions, not automatically repeated dated sermons.

95. Disasters, repentance, and the barren fig tree. Jesus rejects the idea that particular victims were uniquely sinful and urges repentance; a gardener requests time for an unproductive tree (Lk 13:1-9). The passage mentions Pilate’s violence and a fallen tower without supplying independent incident records.

96. The bent woman. Jesus restores a woman described as afflicted for eighteen years, answers a Sabbath objection, and invokes her identity as Abraham’s daughter (Lk 13:10-17). Luke then places the mustard seed and leaven here, illustrating how parable order differs from Matthew and Mark.

97. The narrow door and lament over Jerusalem. Jesus warns about exclusion, imagines an international banquet, dismisses a threat from Herod, and laments Jerusalem’s resistance (Lk 13:22-35; compare Mt 7:13-14, 21-23; 8:11-12; 23:37-39). These sayings anticipate the approaching city without fixing a route.

98. A Sabbath meal and invitations. Jesus heals a man with swelling, teaches humility in seating, urges hospitality to those unable to repay, and tells the great-banquet parable (Lk 14:1-24). Matthew’s royal wedding banquet is related but substantially different (Mt 22:1-14).

99. The cost of following. Jesus uses stark family-language, a builder’s cost calculation, a king considering war, and salt imagery (Lk 14:25-35). Matthew 10:37 provides a related formulation about loving family more than Jesus. These comparisons help interpret rhetoric without erasing its severity.

100. The lost sheep, coin, and two sons. Jesus answers criticism of his company through three stories of loss and recovery (Lk 15:1-32). The elder son’s resistance is essential to the final story. Calling it only the prodigal son can obscure its dispute about joy, mercy, and resentment.

101. The steward and wealth. An accused manager acts decisively before dismissal; sayings concern reliability, money, and incompatible masters (Lk 16:1-18). Interpretations of the manager’s conduct differ. The passage does not simply endorse fraud; it generates a difficult comparison about prudence and use of resources.

102. The rich man and Lazarus. A reversal after death exposes neglect of a poor man and refuses the idea that a spectacular sign automatically overcomes resistance to Scripture (Lk 16:19-31). This Lazarus is a character in a story, not explicitly Lazarus of Bethany.

103. Forgiveness, faith, and service. Jesus commands repeated forgiveness, uses mustard-seed faith imagery, and describes servants who perform their duty (Lk 17:1-10). These short units cover obligations within the disciple community. Their placement gives no precise date of first delivery.

104. The ten men with skin disease. Ten are cleansed while going to the priests; one Samaritan returns with thanks (Lk 17:11-19). Luke locates the journey between Samaria and Galilee in a geographically loose way. The narrative highlights gratitude and an outsider’s recognition.

105. The kingdom’s presence and the Son of Man’s day. Jesus answers questions with teaching about God’s kingdom and sudden judgment, using Noah, Lot, and ordinary work (Lk 17:20-37). The phrase often translated within you can also mean among you; the audience and context matter.

106. Persistent prayer and the Pharisee/tax collector. A widow persists before an unjust judge; a tax collector’s humble appeal is contrasted with self-congratulation (Lk 18:1-14). Both are parables about approaches to God. Neither establishes the behaviour of every member of the social groups used in the stories.

107. Marriage and divorce. Jesus answers a test about divorce through creation texts (Mt 19:1-12; Mk 10:1-12; compare Lk 16:18). Matthew includes exception language absent from Mark’s formulation and adds eunuch sayings. Christian traditions consequently disagree about the implications for remarriage.

108. Receiving children. Jesus rejects attempts to prevent children being brought and associates them with the kingdom (Mt 19:13-15; Mk 10:13-16; Lk 18:15-17). The texts report blessing or reception, not an explicit baptism of these children.

109. The wealthy questioner. Jesus discusses commandments and dispossession, warns about wealth, and promises recompense to followers (Mt 19:16-30; Mk 10:17-31; Lk 18:18-30). The composite label rich young ruler combines details across accounts: Matthew emphasises youth, Luke leadership, all wealth.

110. The vineyard workers. Matthew’s householder pays workers hired at different hours equally (Mt 20:1-16). The story investigates generosity and resentment through a provocative payment scenario. It does not supply an economic policy directly traceable to an independently documented event.

111. Final journey prediction and the request for rank. Jesus predicts arrest, abuse, death, and rising; James and John, or their mother in Matthew, seek privileged places (Mt 20:17-28; Mk 10:32-45; Lk 18:31-34). Jesus defines leadership through service and giving his life for others.

112. Blindness near Jericho. Mark names Bartimaeus; Matthew has two blind men; Luke narrates the healing as Jesus approaches the city, unlike Mark/Matthew’s departure setting (Mt 20:29-34; Mk 10:46-52; Lk 18:35-43). These differences require acknowledgement even if a historical reconciliation is attempted.

113. Zacchaeus and the minas. Jesus receives a wealthy chief tax collector in Jericho; a nearby parable of entrusted money addresses expectations of the kingdom’s immediate arrival (Lk 19:1-27). Luke’s minas story differs from Matthew’s talents; identical subject matter does not make them one unchanged text.

E. John’s parallel festival itinerary before the final Passover

These entries overlap the time covered by sections C-D. They are not events occurring after Zacchaeus simply because the catalogue lists them here. John 5 belongs before John’s feeding narrative in chapter 6; chapters 7-11 follow it. We cannot responsibly assign all these visits to days within Luke’s long journey.

114. Healing at the pool. At an unnamed Jerusalem festival, Jesus restores a man disabled for thirty-eight years at the pool named Bethesda/Bethzatha in manuscript traditions (Jn 5:1-18). Carrying the mat on the Sabbath provokes dispute. The angel stirring the water in 5:3b-4 is absent from important early witnesses.

115. Authority, testimony, and life. Jesus’s response develops the Father’s and Son’s work, judgment, resurrection, and testimony from John, deeds, Scripture, and Moses (Jn 5:19-47). The discourse is a major Johannine presentation of identity, with no close Synoptic transcript counterpart.

116. The festival of Booths. Jesus’s brothers challenge him; he travels to Jerusalem, debates teaching and origin, evokes living water, and becomes the object of divided judgments and an unsuccessful arrest attempt (Jn 7:1-52). The narrative says even his brothers did not believe at this stage.

117. The woman accused of adultery. In the traditional placement at Jn 7:53-8:11, Jesus challenges the accusers and dismisses the woman with an injunction against sin. The episode is absent from the earliest major Johannine witnesses and appears in different places in later manuscripts. Its later textual status does not by itself prove its underlying story false, but prevents treating it as securely original to John. L12

118. Light, freedom, Abraham, and pre-existence. Jesus debates witness, origin, liberation from sin, and Abraham; opponents attempt to stone him (Jn 8:12-59). The climactic pre-Abraham claim belongs to John’s distinctive theology. Its historical relationship to the wording Jesus used during his life remains debated.

119. The man born blind. Jesus makes clay, sends the man to wash, and a series of interrogations follows; the restored man increasingly recognises him (Jn 9:1-41). Jesus rejects a simple parental-sin explanation of the disability. The narrative’s social conflict is also integral to its theological argument.

120. Shepherd, gate, and dedication. Jesus develops shepherd/gate imagery, then at the winter Dedication festival discusses his works and unity with the Father, faces an attempt at arrest, and withdraws beyond the Jordan (Jn 10:1-42). The winter setting is a distinctive chronological marker within John.

121. Lazarus of Bethany. Jesus delays after hearing of illness, returns to Judea, meets Martha and Mary, weeps, and calls Lazarus from the tomb (Jn 11:1-44). This is John’s climactic pre-Passion sign. Its absence from the Synoptics is a significant source difference, not disproof by itself.

122. The decision against Jesus and retreat. John links Lazarus’s raising to a council’s decision, Caiaphas’s statement about one dying for the people, and Jesus’s withdrawal to Ephraim (Jn 11:45-57). This supplies a different immediate narrative cause for the fatal conflict from Mark’s emphasis on the Temple action.

F. The final Jerusalem visit: the Passion-week framework

Traditional weekday labels help navigate Christian worship, but the Gospels do not supply a fully synchronised calendar. John dates the Bethany arrival six days before Passover; Mark introduces an anointing after a notice of two days before Passover. Whether a flashback resolves the difference is an interpretation.

123. Anointing at Bethany. A woman anoints Jesus at Simon the leper’s house in Mark/Matthew; John names Mary and includes Lazarus, Martha, and Judas (Mt 26:6-13; Mk 14:3-9; Jn 12:1-11). Mark/Matthew describe the head, John the feet. Jesus interprets the act in relation to burial. L04

124. Entry into Jerusalem. Jesus enters with a colt and acclamations (Mt 21:1-11; Mk 11:1-11; Lk 19:28-40; Jn 12:12-19). Matthew mentions a donkey and colt when applying Zechariah; the others emphasise one animal. The texts differ about preparation and public recognition while presenting a symbolic royal arrival.

125. Jesus weeps over the city. Luke alone narrates his lament and a prediction of siege as he approaches Jerusalem (Lk 19:41-44). The forecast is transmitted in a Gospel written after, or at least close to, the destruction of Jerusalem; that timing matters for historical assessment.

126. Temple action and the fig tree. The Synoptics place Jesus’s disruption of Temple commerce in this final visit (Mt 21:12-17; Mk 11:15-19; Lk 19:45-48). Mark frames it between cursing and discovering the withered tree (Mk 11:12-14, 20-25); Matthew compresses the tree episode (Mt 21:18-22).

127. Authority challenged. Leaders ask who authorises him; Jesus counters with John’s baptism and refuses their attempted trap (Mt 21:23-27; Mk 11:27-33; Lk 20:1-8). The scene continues the confrontation in the Temple, without offering an independently recorded official hearing.

128. Two sons and murderous tenants. Matthew includes the two sons’ contrasting responses (Mt 21:28-32); all Synoptics narrate tenants killing a vineyard owner’s son (Mt 21:33-46; Mk 12:1-12; Lk 20:9-19). Psalm 118’s rejected stone is attached to the latter. Interpretations must avoid turning its polemic into collective guilt for Jews.

129. The royal wedding banquet. Matthew depicts rejected invitations, violence, replacement guests, and a guest without proper clothing (Mt 22:1-14). Luke’s great supper lacks several of these details. The differences matter when reconstructing an earlier form of the parable.

130. Tax to Caesar. Jesus requests a coin and answers a trap about tribute (Mt 22:15-22; Mk 12:13-17; Lk 20:20-26). The aphorism distinguishes Caesar’s claim from God’s without supplying a complete theory of church-state relations. Its interpretation remains contested.

131. Resurrection and the greatest commandment. Sadducees pose a marital puzzle; Jesus answers through Scripture and God’s life-giving power (Mt 22:23-33; Mk 12:18-27; Lk 20:27-40). A separate exchange joins love of God and neighbor (Mt 22:34-40; Mk 12:28-34). These are debates within Jewish scriptural interpretation.

132. David’s lord, scribes, and the widow. Jesus raises Psalm 110’s puzzle (Mt 22:41-46; Mk 12:35-37; Lk 20:41-44), criticises religious display, and observes a widow’s small gift (Mk 12:38-44; Lk 20:45-21:4). Whether the widow primarily models devotion or exposes exploitation is debated.

133. Extended denunciation and lament. Matthew 23 gathers warnings against status-seeking, hypocrisy, distorted priorities, and persecuting God’s messengers, ending in lament. It overlaps Luke’s earlier material. The evangelist’s polemical rhetoric should neither be treated as a survey of Judaism nor used to blame later Jewish people.

134. Greeks seek Jesus; the grain of wheat. John narrates a request through Philip, Jesus’s teaching on death and fruitfulness, a heavenly voice, and reflections on unbelief (Jn 12:20-50). This marks his public ministry’s conclusion. The Greeks’ requested interview is not separately narrated as occurring.

135. The Temple’s destruction and the coming crisis. Jesus predicts the sanctuary’s fall and teaches about persecution, deception, tribulation, the Son of Man, and vigilance (Mt 24; Mk 13; Lk 21:5-38). References to the generation, impending events, and unknown timing generate major interpretive debates; chronological certainty should not be manufactured.

136. Readiness and final judgment. Matthew adds ten bridesmaids, talents, and the sheep/goats judgment scene (Mt 25:1-46). The final passage’s identification of the least of Jesus’s brothers is disputed: disciples specifically or needy people broadly. It should not be silently resolved in a neutral catalogue.

137. Judas’s arrangement and preparation for the meal. Judas negotiates betrayal; Matthew specifies thirty silver pieces (Mt 26:1-5, 14-16; Mk 14:1-2, 10-11; Lk 22:1-6).

Disciples prepare the meal (Mt 26:17-19; Mk 14:12-16; Lk 22:7-13). These are reported transactions, not surviving financial documents.

138. Footwashing. John places Jesus washing the disciples’ feet at the last meal, interpreting it through cleansing and exemplary service (Jn 13:1-20). John does not narrate the bread-and-wine institution in this scene. The Synoptic institution and Johannine footwashing are distinct surviving presentations of the farewell meal.

139. Betrayal identified and departure predicted. Jesus announces betrayal; John specifies the morsel and Judas’s departure (Mt 26:20-25; Mk 14:17-21; Lk 22:21-23; Jn 13:21-30). All four predict Peter’s denial, but in differing positions and wording (Mt 26:30-35; Mk 14:26-31; Lk 22:31-34; Jn 13:36-38).

140. Bread, wine, and covenant. Jesus interprets bread and cup through his body, blood, and future kingdom (Mt 26:26-29; Mk 14:22-25; Lk 22:14-20). Paul transmits an earlier written version in 1 Cor 11:23-26. The accounts differ in wording, cup sequence, and emphasis; later Eucharistic theologies build on these texts.

141. Service, testing, and swords. Luke places a dispute about greatness and teaching on service at the meal, then speaks of coming trials and swords (Lk 22:24-38). Interpreting the swords requires reading the subsequent arrest and rebuke; the passage is not a straightforward program for armed expansion.

142. John’s farewell teaching. Jesus gives a new love command and speaks of the Father, his departure and return, the Spirit/Advocate, abiding as branches, friendship, persecution, joy, and peace (Jn 13:31-35; 14-16). These extended discourses are distinctive to John and are not independently preserved as a continuous speech elsewhere.

143. The farewell prayer. Jesus prays about glory, protection, sanctification, and unity for present and future believers (Jn 17). The chapter looks back on his completed work while anticipating the Passion. It is a major source for Christian theology rather than an independently transcribed private prayer.

G. Arrest, execution, and burial

144. Gethsemane or the Mount of Olives. Jesus prays under distress while disciples sleep (Mt 26:36-46; Mk 14:32-42; Lk 22:39-46). Luke 22:43-44, the strengthening angel and sweat like blood, has a significant manuscript dispute. John narrates entry into a garden but no equivalent prayer of anguish there. L23

145. Arrest and the severed ear. Judas brings the arresting group; a disciple attacks the high priest’s servant (Mt 26:47-56; Mk 14:43-52; Lk 22:47-53; Jn 18:1-12). John names Peter and Malchus; Luke alone narrates healing the ear. Mark alone adds the fleeing young man. John emphasises Jesus’s voluntary self-identification and the group’s recoil.

146. Questioning by Jewish authorities. Matthew/Mark narrate a nighttime hearing with testimony and a messianic question; Luke emphasises a morning council; John gives questioning before Annas and transfer to Caiaphas (Mt 26:57-68; Mk 14:53-65; Lk 22:54, 63-71; Jn 18:13-24). Reconstructing one formal trial from these accounts requires choices. Later rabbinic procedure cannot automatically be projected backward onto this event.

147. Peter denies association. Peter denies Jesus three times, with variations in questioners, wording, and rooster-crowing (Mt 26:69-75; Mk 14:66-72; Lk 22:55-62; Jn 18:15-18, 25-27). Luke alone describes Jesus turning to look at him. The story’s multiple forms should not be flattened into a verbatim deposition.

148. Judas’s end. Matthew has Judas return money and hang himself, followed by priests buying a burial field (Mt 27:3-10). Acts describes a field acquired through the reward and a fatal fall/bursting (Acts 1:18-19). Combining hanging, rope failure, and bodily rupture is a later harmonisation, not a sequence stated by either text. L13

149. Pilate and, in Luke, Herod. Jesus is brought before Rome’s prefect and questioned about kingship (Mt 27:1-2, 11-14; Mk 15:1-5; Lk 23:1-5; Jn 18:28-38). Luke alone sends him to Herod Antipas and back (23:6-12). John’s kingdom-and-truth dialogue is distinctive. Pilate’s participation anchors the execution under Roman authority. L05

150. Barabbas and sentencing. The narratives contrast Jesus with a released prisoner and depict pressure on Pilate (Mt 27:15-26; Mk 15:6-15; Lk 23:13-25; Jn 18:39-19:16). Matthew uniquely adds Pilate’s wife’s dream and handwashing. A regular Passover-release custom lacks clear independent corroboration. Nothing here justifies assigning collective responsibility to Jews across time.

151. Flogging and royal mockery. Soldiers whip or beat Jesus, dress him in royal parody, and use a thorn crown (Mt 27:26-31; Mk 15:15-20; Jn 19:1-3). The order relative to formal sentencing differs. Luke has different mockery scenes and records Pilate proposing punishment before release.

152. The route to execution. The Synoptics name Simon of Cyrene as compelled to carry the cross; John describes Jesus carrying it himself (Mt 27:32; Mk 15:21; Lk 23:26-32; Jn 19:17). Luke adds mourning women and a warning. Veronica, the numbered falls, and several familiar Stations are later devotional additions.

153. Crucifixion at Golgotha. Jesus is crucified between two others under a royal charge; clothing is divided, drink offered, and mockery narrated (Mt 27:33-44; Mk 15:22-32; Lk 23:33-43; Jn 19:18-27). Luke distinguishes the criminals’ responses; Matthew/Mark initially describe both reviling. John alone places Jesus’s mother and beloved disciple in a direct exchange at the cross.

154. The sayings from the cross. The traditional seven sayings are a later combination: forgiveness for the executioners (Lk 23:34, textually disputed); promise to the criminal (23:43); mother and beloved disciple (Jn 19:26-27); abandonment (Mk 15:34; Mt 27:46); thirst (Jn 19:28); completion (19:30); and entrusting the spirit (Lk 23:46). No Gospel gives all seven or supplies their combined sequence.

155. Death and extraordinary signs. Jesus dies; the Synoptics narrate darkness and a torn Temple curtain, with differing accompanying declarations (Mt 27:45-56; Mk 15:33-41; Lk 23:44-49). Matthew alone adds an earthquake, opened tombs, and holy people appearing after the resurrection. John 19:28-30 narrates the death differently. A normal solar eclipse cannot occur at Passover’s full-moon phase; neither darkness nor the raised saints has secure independent contemporary confirmation.

156. The pierced side and unbroken legs. John alone describes soldiers breaking the other victims’ legs but finding Jesus dead, then a spear wound producing blood and water (Jn 19:31-37). It connects these details with Scripture. Proposed medical explanations remain retrospective possibilities, not autopsy findings or an identification of surviving blood samples. L06

157. Joseph of Arimathea’s burial. Joseph obtains the body and places it in a tomb (Mt 27:57-61; Mk 15:42-47; Lk 23:50-56; Jn 19:38-42). Matthew calls him wealthy and the tomb his own; John adds Nicodemus, spices, and a garden. Details vary. Historians debate how securely the individual and precise burial location can be recovered.

158. Descent or proclamation to the dead: theological placement. Later creeds place descent between death and resurrection. Relevant texts include 1 Pet 3:18-20; 4:6 and Eph 4:8-10, whose referents and meaning are disputed. They do not supply a Gospel-style itinerary of hell. Elaborate rescue scenes emerge in later literature and belong to reception history.

159. The tomb guard. Matthew alone narrates a sealed tomb, a guard requested by opponents, and later payment to circulate a theft explanation (Mt 27:62-66; 28:11-15). This proves that Matthew knows and counters a theft allegation; it does not give us an independently preserved report from the guards. L13L14

H. Easter narratives, appearances, and ascension

160. Women find the tomb empty. The four accounts differ in named visitors, timing language, angelic figures, and reactions (Mt 28:1-8; Mk 16:1-8; Lk 24:1-12; Jn 20:1-2). Mary Magdalene appears in each. Mark’s earliest recoverable ending reports terrified silence; Matthew/Luke report communication. John’s Mary initially explains absence as removal. These differences matter, while an empty-tomb tradition is common to the Gospel narratives. L14L15

161. Peter and the beloved disciple inspect. John describes their run, gravecloths, and developing belief (Jn 20:3-10). Luke 24:12 mentions Peter’s visit, with a textual-history question in some witnesses. John does not explicitly identify the beloved disciple as John son of Zebedee within this scene.

162. Appearances to Mary and the women. John narrates Mary Magdalene recognising Jesus after mistaking him for the gardener (Jn 20:11-18). Matthew has Jesus meet the women as they leave (Mt 28:9-10). Neither account can be reduced without qualification to the other. Paul’s appearance list does not mention these women.

163. Emmaus and the report about Simon. Luke narrates two travellers, one named Cleopas, recognising Jesus at a meal after scriptural exposition; on return they hear he appeared to Simon (Lk 24:13-35). The Simon appearance is reported rather than narrated. The other traveller and the exact Emmaus site remain unidentified. L16

164. An assembled group in Jerusalem. Luke describes Jesus showing wounds and eating fish (Lk 24:36-49); John describes an evening appearance, commissioning, and bestowal of the Spirit, without Thomas present (Jn 20:19-23). Whether these narrate one reconstructed occasion is a harmonising judgment. They plainly develop the resurrection’s embodied and communal significance.

165. Thomas’s encounter. John places another appearance eight days later, with Thomas invited to examine the wounds and responding with an explicit confession (Jn 20:24-29). The text does not state that Thomas actually touched them. The Gospel then explains its selective writing purpose (20:30-31). L21

166. Fishing and breakfast in Galilee. John 21:1-23 describes an extraordinary catch, Jesus providing food, Peter’s threefold exchange and commission, a prediction about Peter’s death, and clarification about the beloved disciple. The 153 fish invite symbolic proposals but no interpretation is stated. An editorial witness claim and selectivity statement follow (21:24-25). L22

167. The Galilean mountain commission. Matthew ends with the Eleven receiving authority to make disciples of all nations, baptize, and teach (Mt 28:16-20). Some doubt is explicitly acknowledged. Unlike Luke-Acts, Matthew narrates no ascension and emphasises continuing presence. L14

168. Five hundred, James, and other apostles. Paul’s earlier list names Cephas, the Twelve, more than five hundred together, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul (1 Cor 15:3-8). Paul says many of the five hundred were alive when he wrote, but supplies no names, location, or separate surviving testimonies for that group. Their appearance cannot simply be identified with Matthew’s mountain scene as a fact. L17

169. Ascension. Luke 24:50-53 describes departure near Bethany with a compressed narrative impression; Acts 1:1-12 explicitly gives forty days of appearances before ascent from the Mount of Olives and a promise of return. The forty days belong to Acts’s account, not all Gospel narratives. L16L18

170. Mark’s later endings. The longer ending, Mk 16:9-20, summarises appearances, mission, signs, and ascension; a shorter ending also circulated. Important early manuscripts lack 16:9-20, and scholarly editions distinguish it from 16:1-8. It is evidence for later reception and canonical transmission, not an additional independent first-generation eyewitness record. L15

I. Jesus acting from heaven in the remaining New Testament records

171. Pentecost and Stephen. Acts 2 interprets the Spirit’s arrival as the exalted Jesus’s action (especially 2:32-36). At his death Stephen sees Jesus standing at God’s right hand and addresses him (Acts 7:54-60). These are accounts within Acts; they should not be described as appearances from Jesus’s earlier public ministry.

172. Paul’s encounter. Paul himself says Jesus appeared to him and that God revealed the Son (1 Cor 9:1; 15:8; Gal 1:11-17). Acts gives three later narrative versions involving a heavenly encounter near Damascus (Acts 9:1-19; 22:3-21; 26:9-23). Details of companions’ perception differ. Paul does not supply Acts’s full scene in his own letters. L17L19

173. Further guidance to Paul in Acts. Jesus addresses Ananias about Paul (Acts 9:10-16), encourages Paul at Corinth (18:9-10), appears in his Jerusalem Temple trance (22:17-21), and assures him of testimony in Rome (23:11). The shipboard angel in Acts 27 is not explicitly Jesus; it should not be added to a Jesus-appearance list without qualification.

174. Revelation’s visions. Revelation presents the exalted Jesus among lampstands, messages to seven churches, the slain-standing Lamb, heavenly worship, judgment, victory, and promised return (Rev 1-3; 5; 19-22). Its visionary sequence is not a calendar of verified post-resurrection earthly travels. Date, symbolism, authorship, and future application require separate discussion.

175. Continuing heavenly work in Christian theology. Romans 8:34 and Hebrews 7:25 describe intercession; 1 Corinthians 15:20-28 describes resurrection, reign, defeat of death, and the end. These are claims about continuing or future activity. They belong to the complete record of what Christians say Jesus does, while remaining different in evidential kind from a dated public event.

The appearances are a central and early fact about the movement’s proclaimed experience. Whether God raised Jesus is the theological and historical explanatory question, not something demonstrated merely by counting passages. Conversely, differing narratives do not demonstrate that no follower had an experience interpreted as seeing Jesus. The texts preserve both early conviction and substantial narrative diversity.

What this catalogue establishes, and what it cannot establish

The most stable life outline is a Jewish man associated with Nazareth, linked to John the Baptist, active as a kingdom preacher and reputed healer/exorcist, gathering followers, confronting opposition, and executed by Roman crucifixion under Pilate. His followers subsequently proclaimed him raised and exalted. Beyond that outline, confidence varies by incident, source independence, distance from events, and theological shaping. Scholarly portraits differ about his precise self-understanding and how to relate apocalyptic expectation, ethical teaching, healing, wisdom, and messianic action. L20

A sufficiently detailed inventory must also preserve what is missing: no surviving document written by Jesus; no securely identified contemporary portrait, childhood diary, official trial transcript, autopsy, or original disciple notebook; no reliable day-by-day record of his twenties; and no single ancient text that reconciles all four Gospels’ chronology. Absence is not proof that an event never occurred, but it limits claims of knowledge.

For everything he did, the crucial distinction is between an event catalogue and a certainty catalogue. All four Gospels, general healing summaries, and explicitly selective endings make a literal complete biography unrecoverable. The entries above aim at a broad canonical inventory with checkable passage references; the separate dossier sections on noncanonical writings and later reception extend the record of claims without quietly upgrading late claims into eyewitness evidence.
