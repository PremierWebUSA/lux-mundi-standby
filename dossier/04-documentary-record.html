The documentary record of Jesus and the movements that interpreted him

A chronological register of the written evidence: Paul, the Gospels and Acts, the manuscripts, early Christian writers, Josephus and the Roman authors, Jewish counter-traditions, the apocrypha, rival movements, and Jesus in Islam.

About this chapter

This chapter is the dossier’s source register. It separates four different dates for every piece of evidence: when the events are said to have happened, when a text was composed, when the surviving copies were made, and when they were rediscovered. It then works through Paul’s letters, the Gospels and Acts, the earliest manuscripts, and the other early Christian writers.

Later sections assess the non-Christian witnesses (Josephus, Pliny, Tacitus, Suetonius, Lucian, Celsus and others), the Talmud and Toledot Yeshu, the apocryphal gospels and acts, the movements that offered competing portraits of Jesus from the first to the fifteenth century, and the Jesus of the Qur’an and later claims.

1. What a chronological archive can establish

The evidence has several different chronologies. Jesus’s lifetime belongs approximately to the final years before the Common Era and the first third of the first century AD. The surviving writings were composed later; the physical copies we possess are usually later again; their modern rediscovery has another date. A fourth-century manuscript can preserve a second-century work containing a tradition that claims to concern the first century. Those are three different historical claims, each requiring its own argument.

This catalogue therefore distinguishes composition, surviving copies, reported events, and modern discovery. Dates below are working scholarly ranges, not dates written on lost originals. “Independent” means independent transmission of information, not simply a different author’s name. A Roman author repeating Christian information is an external witness to Christian belief, but is not automatically an independent witness to the original event. Conversely, a Christian author’s religious commitments do not make everything in the work fictitious. Each claim has to be assessed individually.

No authenticated autograph written by Jesus, contemporary birth certificate, stenographic sermon transcript, or original Roman execution file survives. The records that do survive are substantial enough to investigate his historical setting, the earliest movement, its claims, and later reinterpretations. They do not allow a complete diary, and they cannot convert every ancient assertion about miracles into an independently demonstrated occurrence. This distinction matters particularly when later texts expand precisely the periods about which the earliest narratives say least. R03R07R62R63

2. Approximately AD 30-100: the earliest recoverable Christian evidence

Oral transmission and the letters of Paul

Traditions circulated before the surviving books: sayings, accounts of meals and healings, scriptural interpretations, baptismal teaching, worship, and proclamations of death and resurrection. Their existence can sometimes be inferred because a writer identifies something as received tradition. Reconstructing its exact original wording or assigning it a precise year is harder. In particular, an early tradition embedded in a letter is not an independently surviving document from the year of Jesus’s death.

The earliest substantial extant Christian writings are Paul’s letters. Most critical scholars accept seven as substantially his: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon. They belong chiefly to the 50s AD, with possible dates in the late 40s or early 60s for individual letters. Galatians depends particularly on competing reconstructions of Paul’s travels; the imprisonment letters also have disputed settings. Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, and the Pastoral Epistles are debated in different degrees. Hebrews does not name Paul and should not be silently counted as his testimony. R01R02

Paul is especially valuable because his letters address immediate community problems rather than attempting a complete life of Jesus. This also limits arguments from what he omits. He does not recount Bethlehem, the magi, a virginal conception, the baptism by John, most healing stories, or a detailed empty-tomb narrative. Silence in correspondence about a different problem is weaker evidence than an explicit denial.

Paul’s claim to an appearance is direct testimony to his own reported experience. His reports about earlier appearances are received testimony. Neither should be mislabeled: he was not an eyewitness to Jesus’s Galilean ministry, but he was not simply a writer living centuries later. His disagreements with Cephas and others also show that the early movement was not a single undifferentiated organisation. Acts, written later, is an additional narrative source with its own aims; its chronology should not automatically overwrite Paul’s first-person account. R02 (Galatians 1-2; Acts 9, 15.)

The canonical Gospels and Acts

The four canonical Gospels are Greek literary accounts. They preserve traditions, interpret Jewish scripture, shape characters and episodes, and address audiences. Calling them theological biographies does not settle whether a given event happened. It identifies the kind of evidence being examined. Their traditional author names matter as early reception evidence, but authorship, access to witnesses, editing, and the historical reliability of individual passages are separate questions. R03R07

The date ranges are orientation points, not a consensus on every boundary. The usual sequence is Mark followed by Matthew and Luke, with John substantially distinct. The considerable verbal agreement among the Synoptics requires a literary explanation. Most scholars think Matthew and Luke used Mark. Many also posit Q, a hypothetical sayings source, to explain material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark. Others, including proponents of the Farrer hypothesis, think Luke used Matthew and dispense with Q. No Q manuscript has been found. “M” and “L” often mean material peculiar to Matthew or Luke, not securely identified lost books. John’s relationship to Synoptic traditions remains disputed; neither complete independence nor simple copying should be assumed. R04R05R06

Other New Testament writings add theological and reception evidence. Hebrews develops Jesus as heavenly high priest; James preserves ethical teaching with Gospel-like parallels; 1 Peter interprets suffering through Jesus; Revelation portrays the slain and victorious Lamb and anticipates judgment. Authorship and dates vary by work. These are further records of how Jesus was understood, rather than dozens of additional biographies of his public ministry. The individual passages should be dated and evaluated within their literary settings rather than combined as if all were written by the Twelve immediately after Easter.

3. The physical manuscripts: what survives and what changed

There are no surviving first-century originals of the New Testament. The earliest physical witnesses are fragments, followed by larger papyrus books and late-antique parchment codices. Many manuscript dates are based primarily on handwriting comparisons. These often justify a range measured in decades or more, not a precise year.

P52, the John Rylands fragment, preserves portions of John 18. Its first editor proposed approximately AD 100-150. The holding institution now notes research favouring a date nearer 200. It remains a very early witness, but the often repeated “AD 125” should not be treated as an experimentally established date. Nor can the place where a copy surfaced identify where the Gospel was composed. R08

P46 is an early collection of Pauline letters, conventionally placed around AD 200, with disagreement about the exact range. P66 preserves much of John; P75 preserves substantial Luke and John. Familiar dates around the late second or early third century for these papyri have been challenged with wider or later ranges. Brent Nongbri’s studies illustrate why a manuscript’s format, archaeological context, and comparison samples matter alongside handwriting. A revision to the age of a surviving copy does not automatically redetermine the composition date of its text. R09R10R97

The fourth-century Codex Sinaiticus contains the earliest complete surviving New Testament. It also includes the Epistle of Barnabas and the Shepherd of Hermas, demonstrating that the contents and arrangement of major Christian books were still not everywhere identical. The roughly contemporary Codex Vaticanus is another major witness. The large number of later manuscripts helps reconstruct the history of copying; it does not turn copies descended from the same earlier text into separate eyewitness reports. R11

Two especially consequential Gospel passages require explicit marking:

  • Mark 16:9-20: the familiar longer ending is missing from important early witnesses and is generally considered a later addition to the Gospel’s earliest recoverable form. It supplies appearances, a commission, extraordinary signs, and an ascension. Other endings also circulated. Its antiquity and later ecclesiastical use are different questions from whether Mark originally wrote it. R12R13
  • John 7:53-8:11: the woman accused of adultery is absent from the earliest major witnesses to John and appears at different locations in later manuscripts, including Luke. Most textual scholars judge that it was not originally part of John’s Gospel. That conclusion concerns the passage’s transmission; whether an older incident lies behind the story is a separate, less recoverable question. R96

These are openly documented textual problems, not newly exposed secrets. Most variants are smaller; a smaller number affect wording, narrative details, or interpretation. A serious dossier identifies relevant variants where they matter instead of pretending every manuscript is identical or claiming that every central belief depends on disputed verses.

The broader Apostolic Fathers collection also includes Polycarp’s letter to the Philippians, the account of his martyrdom, the sermon conventionally called 2 Clement, the Epistle of Barnabas, the Shepherd of Hermas, and the Epistle to Diognetus. These chiefly late-first- and second-century traditions, with individual dating disputes, preserve sayings, worship, ethics, Christological language, and arguments over Israel’s scriptures. The Shepherd is a revelatory work concerned with repentance, while Barnabas interprets scripture in explicitly Christian and often anti-Jewish ways. The collection’s modern title does not mean every item was written by an apostle’s personal acquaintance. These texts expand the evidence for early reception; they do not each supply a separate full life of Jesus. R108

Papias’s reputation for preserving early testimony should not lead to indiscriminate acceptance of everything attributed to him. His fragments also include elaborate millennial imagery and an alternative account of Judas’s death. The historian must assess individual traditions, not award an entire author a blanket certificate of reliability or unreliability. R17

The same method applies to later writers such as Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Origen, and Eusebius. They preserve earlier materials that would otherwise be lost, but quotation, paraphrase, selection, and controversy can intervene. Eusebius’s early-fourth-century Church History is indispensable for the archive’s history; it is not a first-century observation of Jesus. His own list distinguishes accepted, disputed, and rejected writings, rather than describing a universally settled collection from the beginning. R21

5. Approximately AD 93-250: non-Christian and hostile testimony

Josephus, approximately AD 93-94

Josephus’s Jewish Antiquities contains two passages directly relevant to Jesus. They must not be collapsed into one.

Antiquities 18.63-64, conventionally called the Testimonium Flavianum, describes Jesus, his teaching, execution under Pilate, and continuing followers. The transmitted Greek also explicitly affirms messiahship and a third-day appearance. A widespread scholarly position is that Josephus wrote a shorter notice subsequently altered by Christian transmission. Some defend more extensive authenticity; others regard the whole passage as interpolation. The exact original wording is not recoverable by simply crossing out whichever lines sound Christian. T. C. Schmidt’s 2025 study is a recent substantive defence of stronger authenticity, illustrating that the discussion remains active. Even an authentic core would not make Josephus an eyewitness to Jesus’s ministry. R22R24R100

Antiquities 20.200 describes the execution of James, identified as the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, during the interval between the Roman governors Festus and Albinus, conventionally AD 62. This passage is much more widely accepted as authentic. Its focus is the high priest Ananus’s conduct and James’s death, not proof of a miracle. It provides a significant late-first-century Jewish reference to Jesus’s family and public identification. R23R100

Josephus’s descriptions of John the Baptist and Pilate are also important background, but a passage about John is not automatically an additional Josephus passage about Jesus. Likewise, copied versions of the Testimonium in later languages are textual witnesses to that passage, not wholly separate ancient observers. R22

Mara bar Serapion: date uncertain

A Syriac letter attributed to Mara bar Serapion compares the deaths of Socrates, Pythagoras, and the Jews’ unnamed wise king. Many identify the king as Jesus, but the letter does not use his name. Its date is unusually uncertain: proposals range from the aftermath of first-century events to the second or fourth century, and its rhetorical setting is debated. It should not be presented as a securely dated report from AD 73. Its value is evidence of a tradition about a murdered Jewish sage and subsequent calamity; the author supplies no details of Galilean ministry, Pilate’s trial, or an independently investigated resurrection. R25

Pliny the Younger, approximately AD 112

In Letters 10.96-97, Pliny asks Trajan how to deal with Christians in Bithynia-Pontus. He reports meetings, a hymn addressed to Christ with divine honour, ethical commitments, and a meal. He also describes coercive interrogation, including the torture of two enslaved women called ministers or deaconesses. Trajan’s reply directs him not to seek Christians out indiscriminately or accept anonymous accusations, while maintaining punishment for persistent refusal to conform. These are important administrative records of Christian practice and Roman policy. They provide no independent account of Jesus’s childhood or miracles. R26

Tacitus, approximately AD 115-117

In Annals 15.44, Tacitus explains the name Christian by reference to Christus, executed during Tiberius’s reign under Pontius Pilate, while discussing Nero’s treatment of Christians after the fire of Rome. The passage is hostile, not Christian propaganda in its tone. It is a major non-Christian reference to Jesus’s execution. However, Tacitus does not identify his source or say he consulted Pilate’s original records. Possibilities include administrative knowledge and information about Christian origins circulating in Rome. His use of “procurator” for Pilate, whose contemporary title was prefect, is a terminological issue to assess, not automatic proof either of fraud or archival verification. R27

Suetonius, approximately AD 120

In Claudius 25.4, Suetonius attributes disturbances among Roman Jews to “Chrestus.” This might reflect disputes concerning Christ misunderstood as agitation by someone currently present in Rome; it might concern a different person bearing a common name. The identification is therefore possible rather than certain. Nero 16 refers more clearly to Christians but supplies no biography of their founder. Neither passage establishes that Jesus personally travelled to Rome. R28R101

Lucian, approximately AD 165

The satirist Lucian’s Passing of Peregrinus mocks Christians’ attachment to a crucified figure associated with Palestine, their mutual support, and their susceptibility to exploitation by Peregrinus. It shows a recognizable second-century outsider’s picture of the movement. Satire is not neutral ethnography, and a sketch of what Christians worshiped is not an independent investigation of each Gospel event. R29

Celsus, approximately AD 175-180, preserved by Origen around 248

Celsus’s anti-Christian work survives chiefly through Origen’s extensive response. In the argument Origen presents, Jesus is accused of illegitimate birth and gaining magical powers in Egypt; the soldier Panthera appears in an alternative paternity story. These are valuable evidence of ancient counterclaims and of criticism already aimed at Christian narratives. They are not surviving birth records, and the demonstrable existence of an ancient accusation is not proof of its truth. Celsus’s text is also mediated by an opponent who quotes it to answer it. R30

Thallus and Phlegon: fragments of lost works

Apologetic lists often present Thallus and Phlegon as straightforward contemporary witnesses to the darkness at the crucifixion. Their actual transmission is far less direct. The relevant material survives through later discussion, including Julius Africanus and subsequently George Syncellus; the original works and context are lost. Thallus’s identity, date, and original wording are disputed. Phlegon, a second-century author, wrote about remarkable phenomena, including an eclipse and an earthquake; the relation of these reports to Gospel chronology is contested. R31R32

It is therefore inaccurate to announce that two preserved first-century scientific reports independently confirm a three-hour worldwide darkness. Africanus himself objected to explaining Passover darkness as an ordinary solar eclipse, because Passover is associated with the full-moon part of the lunar cycle. The surviving discussion establishes ancient attempts to correlate chronologies and phenomena. It does not provide an intact eyewitness observation at the cross. R31

Porphyry and Julian: third- and fourth-century criticism

Porphyry’s anti-Christian arguments survive through fragmentary transmission and opponents’ responses, with disputes over the attribution of individual fragments. They address scriptural interpretation, contradictions, and Christian claims within a philosophical controversy. Emperor Julian’s Against the Galileans, composed in 362-363, likewise survives largely because Cyril of Alexandria quoted it to refute it in the fifth century. These are substantial records of ancient criticism of Christianity. Their distance from Jesus’s lifetime and engagement with Christian texts make them evidence for intellectual reception, not independent eyewitness reports of the ministry. Reconstruction should preserve the difference between the critic’s surviving words and the refuter’s summaries. R102R103

6. Late antiquity and the Middle Ages: Jewish countertraditions

The Babylonian Talmud reached its principal redaction centuries after Jesus, although it contains earlier traditions. Passages concerning Yeshu, ben Stada, or ben Pandera cannot all be assumed without argument to refer to one person. Names, chronology, manuscript censorship, narrative setting, and relations to Christian traditions complicate identification.

The frequently cited Sanhedrin 43a describes a figure identified in relevant textual forms as Jesus being executed near Passover after accusations of sorcery and leading Israel astray. It also depicts a lengthy public opportunity to offer a defence and lists five disciples. This is not a contemporaneous Jewish court transcript. Its legal and polemical framing differs from the Gospel trial narratives, and scholars disagree about whether particular elements preserve earlier information or answer Christian claims. Other passages place related figures in chronologically incompatible settings. Respectful scholarship takes the texts seriously as Jewish literary evidence without using them as either automatic validation or automatic disproof of every Christian claim. R33R34

Toledot Yeshu is a family of Jewish counter-narratives, not one book written in Jesus’s lifetime. Different versions circulated in Hebrew, Aramaic, and other Jewish languages. They commonly invert Christian claims: illegitimate origins, unauthorised acquisition of divine power, magic, disputes with sages, humiliating death, and explanations for the missing body. Some motifs have late-antique roots; a ninth-century Christian writer, Agobard, knew related Jewish stories. Surviving versions developed through medieval transmission. Princeton’s project and the critical edition demonstrate substantial variation across more than a hundred Hebrew and Aramaic manuscripts. Their chief historical value is for Jewish-Christian controversy and storytelling, not direct access to an original execution file. R34R35

7. The apocryphal archive: additional lives, sayings, revelations, and adventures

“Apocryphal” identifies a relationship to later scriptural collections; it does not mean all these works have the same theology, date, purpose, or historical value. Some are narrative Gospels, some sayings collections, some dialogues set after resurrection, some apostolic romances, and some devotional expansions. Several circulated widely within churches that did not regard them as New Testament scripture. Some survive only because Christian scribes copied them. The title “Gospel of X” does not authenticate authorship by X.

Fragmentary Gospels and sayings traditions, mainly second century

Gospel of Thomas. This collection contains 114 sayings attributed to Jesus rather than a continuous ministry or Passion narrative. The complete version survives in a fourth-century Coptic Nag Hammadi codex; Greek fragments from Oxyrhynchus are earlier, around the late second or third century. Dating the composition is disputed. A second-century form is commonly proposed, while some sayings may preserve older traditions. Others may reflect knowledge of or developments beyond canonical material. It is methodologically unsound either to date every saying to the historical Jesus or to assume no early tradition can survive in a later collection. Its emphasis on understanding sayings and discovering life differs markedly from a narrative centered on crucifixion and resurrection. R36

Egerton Gospel. The surviving papyrus fragments contain episodes resembling both Synoptic and Johannine traditions: controversies over scripture and authority, an attempted arrest, a healing, a question about rulers, and a heavily fragmentary wonder near the Jordan. Composition and literary dependence remain debated; the manuscript is commonly assigned to the second century. The fragments are too small to establish a complete “fifth Gospel” with a recoverable itinerary. R37

Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 5575. Published in 2023 and assigned by its editors to the second century, this small sayings fragment contains affinities with Matthew, Luke, and Thomas. It demonstrates the continuing importance of unpublished manuscript collections. A newly published fragment is not automatically a newly discovered eyewitness document; its relationships to known traditions still need explanation. R38

Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 840. Despite the conventional papyrus label, the surviving object is parchment, generally fourth or fifth century. Its Temple purification dispute between Jesus and a priestly or Pharisaic opponent raises questions about ritual knowledge, literary imagination, and the use of a miniature manuscript. The age of the physical object does not securely date the lost work as a whole. R39

Gospels of the Hebrews, Nazarenes, and Ebionites. These labels organise scattered quotations and descriptions in church writers, not three complete recovered books. The number of underlying works and the correct assignment of fragments remain contested. Traditions associated with the Gospel of the Hebrews include the Holy Spirit as Jesus’s mother and a resurrection appearance to James. The Gospel of the Ebionites, a modern scholarly title for material quoted by Epiphanius, appears to harmonise Synoptic traditions in Greek and reflects distinctive dietary and baptismal interests. It should not be equated casually with an intact original Hebrew Matthew. R40R41

Expanded Passion, birth, and childhood narratives, mainly second century

Gospel of Peter. A Passion and resurrection narrative survives principally in the Akhmim manuscript, copied centuries after the work’s likely second-century origins. Its identification and reconstruction require care. It redistributes responsibility for Jesus’s death and contains extraordinary resurrection imagery, including immense figures and a speaking cross. Serapion of Antioch’s discussion of a Gospel of Peter around the end of the second century is relevant external evidence, but does not guarantee that every feature of the surviving fragment belonged to precisely the text he read. R42

Protevangelium of James. Usually dated to the later second century, it narrates Mary’s birth to Joachim and Anna, her upbringing, Joseph as an older guardian with existing children, and the miraculous birth of Jesus. The midwife and Salome episodes defend Mary’s virginity. Surviving manuscript witnesses include the Greek Papyrus Bodmer V, generally placed around the fourth century, and numerous later copies and translations. This work deeply influenced art and devotion, but it is not a first-century memoir written by Jesus’s brother James. R43

Infancy Gospel of Thomas. Distinct from the sayings Gospel of Thomas, this evolving collection depicts Jesus between roughly ages five and twelve. Episodes include living birds made from clay, extraordinary knowledge, alarming curses, restorations, and help with Joseph’s carpentry. Different versions soften, expand, or reorder the stories. Its early forms belong to the second-century development of childhood traditions, but manuscripts are later and varied. It shows how communities imagined divine power in a child; it does not fill the missing years with independently verified incidents. R44

Marcion’s Gospel. Around the middle of the second century, Marcion’s communities used a Gospel closely related to Luke and a collection of Pauline letters. No complete manuscript survives; scholars reconstruct its contents from opponents’ quotations and arguments. The prevailing model treats Marcion’s Gospel as an edited Luke, while some recent scholarship argues for the priority of Marcion’s form or a more complex shared history. A reconstruction should identify uncertain readings rather than presenting an entirely recovered book. Its missing infancy material and different theological framing matter for the reception of Jesus. R45

Revelatory dialogues and works often called “Gnostic,” second to fourth centuries

Ascension of Isaiah. This composite or layered work places a vision of heavenly realms and the descent of the beloved Christ within a story about Isaiah. Dating, provenance, and the relationship between its martyrdom and vision sections remain disputed; a second-century setting is an important proposal, not a settled date for every component. Its angelic imagery and variant textual forms make it significant for early Christology. It should neither be mistaken for a genuinely pre-Christian prediction written by the biblical Isaiah nor used without textual argument as proof that all early Christians imagined Jesus’s death in precisely the same cosmic location. R104

Gospel of Mary. Usually assigned to the second century, it survives incompletely in a Coptic Berlin codex and earlier Greek fragments. More than half the work is missing. The remaining material presents post-resurrection teaching, Mary’s account of a revelation, the soul’s ascent, and disputes involving Peter, Andrew, and Levi over her authority. Identification of its Mary with Mary Magdalene is customary but should still be distinguished from explicit naming. The text does not establish that Jesus married her. It is valuable evidence of debates about revelation, authority, and gender in early Christianity. R46

Gospel of Judas. A Greek work of approximately the second century is known through Irenaeus and survives in Coptic in Codex Tchacos. It contrasts the disciples’ religious practices with secret teaching to Judas and invokes an elaborate heavenly world. The sensational label “Judas was the real hero” oversimplifies a difficult text: his privileged knowledge, association with a thirteenth figure, sacrifice, and fate can be evaluated negatively as well as positively. It is evidence for a later reinterpretation of betrayal and worship, not a newly recovered diary by Judas Iscariot. R47

Gospel of Philip. Generally located in the late second or third century, this collection of reflections survives in a fourth-century Coptic codex. It discusses sacraments, anointing, imagery of the bridal chamber, and spiritual relationships. The damaged passage about Jesus kissing Mary and calling her a companion is frequently sensationalised. Its setting is symbolic and sacramental, its exact damaged wording is uncertain, and it does not function as a first-century marriage certificate or prove a bloodline. R48

Gospel of Truth. A second-century composition associated with Valentinian Christianity, this work is better understood as a theological meditation or homily than a narrative Gospel. Jesus reveals the Father and overcomes ignorance and error. The title therefore does not mean it competes episode by episode with Mark as another historical biography. Its significance lies in a sophisticated early interpretation of salvation and knowledge. R49

Apocryphon of John. Surviving in several Coptic copies and forms, it presents a revelation to John concerning the highest divine reality, emanations, Sophia, the deficient creator, the making of humanity, and awakening through revelation. The work has second-century roots and subsequent development; the manuscripts are later. The agreements and differences between recensions give unusually valuable access to textual change within a noncanonical tradition. It is not a concealed chapter once demonstrably removed from the canonical Gospel of John. R50R51

Epistle of the Apostles. Probably composed in the second century, this revelation dialogue presents the risen Jesus instructing the apostles and opposes figures such as Simon and Cerinthus. It strongly affirms embodied salvation. It is a useful corrective to the assumption that every noncanonical work denies Jesus’s flesh or belongs to one “Gnostic church.” Its surviving Coptic, Latin, and Ethiopic witnesses differ in completeness and date. R52

Apocryphon of James. Preserved in a fourth-century Nag Hammadi codex, this likely second-century work presents secret instruction to James and Peter in a letter framed as originally Hebrew. It emphasises understanding, fullness, and the willingness to suffer. Its pseudonymous framing is evidence of a claim to apostolic authority, not independent proof that James wrote it. R53

Sophia of Jesus Christ transforms an earlier philosophical-revelatory discourse into a dialogue with the risen Jesus. The Letter of Peter to Philip gathers apostles for revelation and discusses the deficiency of the cosmic order and the mission. Dialogue of the Savior similarly uses questions and answers about salvation rather than a chronological Galilean ministry. These belong in the archive of early Christian interpretations, while their narrative settings should not be confused with documentary dates. R54R55R51

Two different Apocalypses of Peter must be separated. The Greek/Ethiopic work, usually placed in the second century, describes judgment and the rewards or punishments of the afterlife and had significant early reception. The Coptic Nag Hammadi Apocalypse of Peter presents a revelatory interpretation of crucifixion, distinguishing the living revealer from the suffering visible figure. The Second Treatise of the Great Seth offers another interpretation in which the heavenly speaker is not defeated by the earthly spectacle. These texts have different histories and cannot be combined as one apostolic testimony. R94R56R57

Apostolic Acts and continuing appearances of Jesus

Acts of John, with a substantial second-century core and a complicated transmission, combines missionary wonders with striking depictions of Jesus. He can appear differently to different observers; a hymn and circular dance precede arrest; a luminous cross and revelatory interpretation complicate ordinary bodily suffering. Some portions were controversial precisely because of their Christology. The text is a literary witness to such beliefs, not an independent recording of what happened inside the final supper. R58

Acts of Thomas, commonly dated to the early third century, sends Thomas eastward and presents Jesus arranging the mission and appearing in the apostle’s likeness. It includes ascetic teaching, royal adventures, miracles, and the Hymn of the Pearl. Traditions about Thomas travelling to India are not evidence that Jesus himself studied or died there. R59

Acts of Peter, usually associated with the later second century, depicts a contest with Simon, wonders in Rome, appearances of Christ, and Peter’s return to face martyrdom after an encounter with Jesus. Its upside-down crucifixion narrative is a later literary tradition about the apostle, not a detail recorded in the canonical Gospels. Acts of Paul, also second century, includes Thecla, miraculous deliverances, a lion episode, 3 Corinthians, visions of Christ, and martyrdom; sections circulated separately. Acts of Andrew survives incompletely through Greek material, a papyrus fragment, and Gregory of Tours’s later summary, making reconstruction especially important. These works must be distinguished from the canonical Acts of the Apostles and from still later tales combining two or more apostolic protagonists. R105R106R107

Pistis Sophia contains extended revelations after the resurrection, including a setting eleven years later, Sophia’s fall and restoration, elaborate cosmology, moral questions, and prominent questioning by Mary. Its component works have different literary settings and likely developed over time; the Coptic Askew Codex is a later copy of the traditions. A chronological life of Jesus must label these as narrated post-resurrection revelations rather than extending a historically documented earthly ministry by eleven years. R60

Books of Jeu is a conventional label for several related writings in the Bruce Codex. Current specialist discussion places their Greek composition no earlier than the late third century and often around the middle fourth; older very early datings should not be repeated uncritically. They depict Jesus teaching heavenly treasuries, diagrams, seals, names, and ritual ascent. The surviving codex is generally dated to the late fourth or fifth century. These works demonstrate continuing ritual and cosmological invention around Jesus. R61

Late-antique and medieval narrative expansions

Acts of Pilate / Gospel of Nicodemus. A late-antique trial narrative, with later additions and recensions, develops the roles of Pilate, his wife, witnesses, the thieves, and resurrection testimony. The descent into the underworld became an especially influential associated narrative. Its title does not authenticate it as the Roman governor’s archived proceedings. Christian writers’ earlier appeals to possible Pilate records and the surviving literary Acts of Pilate are separate matters. R62

Abgar correspondence and the Edessa cycle. Eusebius reports a letter from King Abgar to Jesus and a reply promising later assistance, claiming a Syriac archival source. The later Doctrine of Addai develops Edessa’s conversion story. Historians do not generally accept the correspondence as authenticated letters actually written by Jesus. These accounts explain a city’s Christian origins and apostolic legitimacy; their place in the archive is reception history, with the surviving attestation centuries after the supposed exchange. R63

Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew and later infancy cycles. Early-medieval Latin adaptations drew on earlier Mary and childhood narratives and added memorable scenes during the flight into Egypt: animals honouring Jesus, a palm providing food, and idols falling. Syriac, Arabic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, and other traditions developed their own combinations. Later composition does not imply cultural insignificance; these stories shaped pilgrimage, iconography, liturgy, and popular knowledge of Jesus far beyond the four canonical narratives. It does prevent treating all their details as equally early historical testimony. R64

Secret Gospel of Mark. Morton Smith reported finding a letter attributed to Clement of Alexandria at Mar Saba in 1958, copied into a much later printed book. The letter quotes an expanded Markan episode involving a raised young man and private instruction. Debate concerns the letter’s authenticity, its relation to Clement and Mark, and the possibility of modern fabrication. The material is known from photographs, while the physical pages are unavailable for the necessary range of direct tests. Neither ancient authenticity nor forgery has been universally demonstrated. Sensational claims about Jesus’s sexuality exceed what this disputed and fragmentary evidence can establish. R65

Gospel of Barnabas. The work commonly circulated under this title is generally placed around the sixteenth century in current specialist discussion, with a proposed Morisco setting; its linguistic and compositional history remains debated. It reworks Jesus’s life in a manner engaging Islamic claims. It is distinct from the early Christian Epistle of Barnabas and from ancient references to writings bearing Barnabas’s name. Its apostolic attribution does not make it a first-century witness. R66

What Nag Hammadi changed

The Coptic books associated with the 1945 Nag Hammadi discovery transformed research because many teachings previously known chiefly from opponents could now be compared with surviving internal texts. The collection preserves diverse works, not a single alternative New Testament or the library of one securely identified sect. Its manuscripts are principally fourth century, while the works translated into them may be older. The Gospel of Mary belongs to the separate Berlin Codex, and the Gospel of Judas to Codex Tchacos; neither should be described as a newly found Nag Hammadi Gospel. The circumstances of the original discovery have also been reconstructed from accounts given later and are not uniformly secure in every detail. R36R46R47R51

8. Movements and competing portraits of Jesus, first to fifteenth centuries

For historical work, movement, school, church, network, and community are generally more precise than “cult.” Some movements disappeared as institutions; others survived under different names; some are known mainly through hostile classifications. A heresiologist’s claim that one teacher fathered the next can be an argument about error rather than a reliable institutional genealogy. “Gnosticism,” in particular, is a modern organising term applied to diverse ancient systems. Similar themes do not establish one worldwide organisation or an uninterrupted secret succession. R67R68

First and second centuries: Jewish followers, Gentile missions, and different Christologies

The earliest Jerusalem movement. Jesus’s first followers were Jews. Paul’s letters show continuing authority associated with Cephas, James, and John and disagreements about Gentile participation, table fellowship, circumcision, and Torah practice. These debates occurred before later boundaries between “Christianity” and “Judaism” became stable. It is misleading to picture Jesus founding an already separate modern denomination with the later church’s full institutional form. It is equally misleading to reduce the entire first generation to a single recoverable theology. [Galatians 1-3; Romans 9-11; 1 Corinthians 15]

Ebionites and Nazarenes. Later writers describe Jewish Jesus-following communities with Torah observance and distinctive views of Jesus and Paul. Some accepted a virginal conception; others did not. Irenaeus and Eusebius do not give perfectly interchangeable descriptions, and Epiphanius writes later still. “Ebionite” should not be used as a certainty label for all original Jerusalem believers, nor should a lost gospel automatically be assigned to every group named Nazarene. Their importance lies in showing that adherence to Jesus and continued Jewish practice coexisted in several forms. The available evidence often comes from opponents rather than the communities’ complete surviving archives. R71R21R40R41

Cerinthus and separationist interpretations. In Irenaeus’s report, Cerinthus distinguished the earthly Jesus from a heavenly Christ who descended at baptism and departed before the final suffering. That differs from saying Jesus never had a real human body. “Adoptionist,” “separationist,” and “docetic” therefore should not be used interchangeably. Each identifies a different proposed relation between the human figure, divine agency, and suffering. R71

Docetism. This is a descriptive category for views in which Jesus’s humanity or suffering is in some respect an appearance, rather than the name of one universally organised sect. Ignatius’s insistence on real birth, real suffering, and bodily resurrection indicates early controversy. Other texts distinguish the visible sufferer from a transcendent redeemer in different ways. A movement’s precise position must be read from its own language where possible; the bare accusation “docetic” can conceal important differences. R16R56R57

Basilides and Basilideans. Basilides taught in second-century Alexandria, but reconstructions are difficult because later reports differ. Irenaeus attributes to his school an elaborate heavenly hierarchy and an account in which Simon of Cyrene is crucified in Jesus’s place while the transcendent savior escapes suffering. Other ancient descriptions do not line up neatly with this account. It is therefore safer to say “Irenaeus reports” than to present a single uncontested Basilidean creed. The group’s historical existence is firmer than the details of every teaching attributed to it. R69

Carpocratians. Irenaeus depicts Carpocrates and his followers as believing Jesus was born ordinarily but possessed an exceptional soul and knowledge that freed him from lower cosmic powers. Their opponents also accuse them of transgressive practices. Such allegations are evidence of polemical representation and require corroboration before being treated as straightforward descriptions of community behaviour. The group illustrates a portrait of Jesus as exemplary liberator through knowledge, distinct from both ordinary docetism and later Nicene Christology. R70

Marcionites. Marcion, active in the middle second century, differentiated the creator and lawgiver associated with Israel’s scriptures from the good God revealed through Jesus. His communities developed an influential Gospel-and-Paul collection and endured well beyond his lifetime. He is important both for competing portrayals of Jesus and for the history of Christian scripture. The surviving evidence largely consists of opponents’ refutations and reconstructed readings, so claims about his exact text and detailed theology need different confidence levels. Marcion’s rejection of the creator should not be projected backward onto Jesus’s own Jewish context. R72R45

Valentinian schools. Valentinus and subsequent teachers developed accounts of divine fullness, emanations, rupture, and restoration. Jesus reveals the Father and reunites what has become divided or ignorant. Their communities and teachings were diverse; some operated within wider Christian congregations. The Gospel of Truth and Gospel of Philip offer internal evidence to compare with Irenaeus’s detailed attack. Reducing this tradition to “Jesus was fake” misses its theological and sacramental sophistication. Nor should all texts loosely called Gnostic automatically be assigned to Valentinus himself. R73R49R48

Sethian-associated traditions. Modern scholarship groups certain texts around a recurring mythic world involving Barbelo, the divine human prototype, Seth, Sophia, and the creator often called Yaldabaoth. The Apocryphon of John is central to this comparison. Some texts strongly incorporate Christ; others emphasise different revelatory figures or philosophical frameworks. The label identifies a family of traditions reconstructed from texts, not an institution whose membership list survives. Irenaeus’s account of related cosmology can be compared with internal material rather than accepted as the sole description. R68R74R50

Montanists or the New Prophecy. Beginning in the second century in Asia Minor, Montanus and the women prophets Priscilla and Maximilla proclaimed inspired speech and urgent expectation. Disputes concerned prophecy, ecclesiastical authority, discipline, and the end. They should not simply be filed under Gnosticism: their central issue was continuing revelation within Christian life. Much early evidence comes from hostile writers preserved by Eusebius, while the later association of Tertullian with the New Prophecy supplies another perspective. The movement’s disappearance as a separate institution does not make every accusation against its prophets reliable. R75

Third and fourth centuries: new prophetic systems and imperial controversy

Elchasaites. Reports by Hippolytus and others describe a baptist, Torah-observant revelatory movement associated with a book attributed to Elchasai. Its angelic Christ, repeated ritual washing, and particular rules do not fit neatly into later denominational categories. The date of the originating traditions and the relation among people grouped under this name are debated. These reports are significant for the religious environment in which Mani grew up, but it is unsafe to treat every Jewish-Christian baptist community as Elchasaite. R76R78

Manichaeans. Mani, born in the third century in Mesopotamia, founded a religion with a universal mission and a far-reaching light-and-darkness cosmology. He presented himself as an apostle in a history of revelation. Jesus has several roles in Manichaean texts, including a luminous revealer, the historical Jesus interpreted within Mani’s system, and the suffering light bound within the world. These roles cannot be flattened into a single claim that Manichaeans “worshiped the same Jesus in the usual Christian sense.” The religion spread across multiple languages and regions before largely disappearing as an organised tradition. Internal texts greatly improve on descriptions by Christian and other opponents. R78R77

Arius and the fourth-century disputes. Arius did not simply teach that Jesus was an ordinary human teacher. The dispute concerned the status and origin of the preexistent Son in relation to the Father: whether the Son belonged within the eternal divine reality in the same sense, or was brought into being and subordinate. The Council of Nicaea in 325 affirmed the Son as begotten rather than made and of the same substance as the Father. The subsequent controversies involved several positions and shifting alliances; “Arian” became a broad and sometimes misleading polemical umbrella. R79R95

What Nicaea did not do. The surviving creed and canons do not record a vote selecting Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John from hundreds of equally eligible books. Four-Gospel authority is explicit in Irenaeus long before 325, while discussions of the boundaries of the scriptural collection continued afterward. Nor did Nicaea first invent divine honour for Jesus: Paul and Pliny already attest much earlier devotion. The council’s decision was an important formulation within an existing controversy about the Father and Son. R20R26R79R80

Athanasius’s Festal Letter 39, AD 367. This is the earliest surviving list that contains precisely the twenty-seven books of the present New Testament. Athanasius also distinguishes other books useful for instruction. His letter is a major milestone, not a record that every Christian everywhere adopted one identical collection on that day. Earlier and later differences, local decisions, liturgical use, copying, and theological conflict all belong to the history of canon formation. R81R82

Fifth century onward: lasting churches, not extinct caricatures

The councils of Constantinople in 381, Ephesus in 431, and Chalcedon in 451 form part of the subsequent history of Christological definition. They are not new eyewitness records of Jesus. Their questions concern how to express divinity, humanity, personhood, incarnation, and salvation coherently within inherited traditions. Describing the disputes only as cynical political invention fails to explain their theological stakes; ignoring imperial and ecclesiastical power would be equally incomplete.

The Church of the East and the label “Nestorian.” This living tradition should not be reduced to the caricature that it worships two unrelated Jesuses. Historical terminology, Syriac and Greek conceptual differences, and later polemics complicated the controversy. A 1994 common declaration between the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East affirms true divinity and humanity in one person while recognising different traditional expressions. That declaration does not erase the historical disagreements; it demonstrates why an inherited adversarial label is an inadequate description of a present community. R84

Oriental Orthodox churches and miaphysite theology. Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Indian Orthodox traditions continue today. They are frequently mislabeled as simply denying Jesus’s humanity. Their characteristic miaphysite language concerns the unity of the incarnate Word’s divine and human reality, not a claim that humanity vanished. “Monophysite,” “miaphysite,” and “Chalcedonian” should be defined in context instead of treated as interchangeable insults. These are major continuing churches, not dead cults. R83

Seventh to fifteenth centuries: medieval dissidents and the limits of their archive

Paulicians. Documented from the early medieval Armenian and Byzantine world, these communities were attacked by imperial and ecclesiastical opponents and often classified as dualist. Their theology and internal variety remain contested. Carl Dixon’s 2026 reassessment challenges the standard image of straightforward absolute dualism. The evidence therefore does not justify a confident diagram running from ancient Gnostics through Manichaeans to Paulicians as if each handed the next an unchanged creed. Reports of their beliefs, their social history, and later labels must be disentangled. R85

Bogomils. First securely prominent in tenth-century Bulgaria, they are known particularly through the polemic of Cosmas the Priest and later Byzantine materials. Traditions ascribed to them distinguish the good divine realm from a corrupted world and reject important features of established church practice. Their actual organisation, changes over time, and relationships to neighboring dissident groups require reconstruction from uneven evidence. It is useful to compare reported teachings about Christ, creation, baptism, and institutions separately rather than assume a single stable medieval dualist religion. R86

Cathars. Medieval western European heresy records, especially from the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, describe ascetic dissent and dualist teachings attributed to groups often called Cathars. Some internal ritual and theological material survives, alongside a much larger archive created by opponents and inquisitors. Scholars debate how far this evidence demonstrates a unified international church and how far later classification joined distinct local phenomena. The issue is not resolved by either repeating every inquisitorial description or claiming no dissenters existed. A 2025 Cambridge survey explicitly presents this as an active historiographical dispute. Claims about Cathar possession of a secret historical truth about Jesus or an authenticated bloodline lack support in this archive. R87

Mandaeans. Mandaeism is a living religion with a distinctive baptismal, priestly, and cosmological tradition, especially associated historically with southern Iraq and Iran and now also diaspora communities. John the Baptist has an important place, but Mandaeans are not simply Christians who chose John instead of Jesus. Their origins and relations to late-antique Jewish, Christian, and other environments remain debated. Mandaean texts sometimes portray Jesus negatively; these are evidence of interreligious boundary making, not preserved first-century eyewitness memoirs. Older outsider descriptions such as “Christians of Saint John” are misleading. R88R89

9. Seventh century onward: Jesus in Islam and later new claims

The Qur’an presents Jesus, ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, as Messiah, messenger, and a word from God, born to Mary through divine action. It attributes extraordinary signs to him, including speech in infancy, healing, bringing the dead to life, and forming a bird from clay, consistently by God’s permission. It rejects treating him as God’s son in the Christian theological sense. These are primary evidence for the Qur’an’s portrayal of Jesus in the seventh-century religious world, not first-century documentary attestations of each event. The overlap with some earlier Christian and apocryphal motifs invites comparison, but proving an exact chain of textual borrowing requires more than observing a resemblance. R91R93R98

Qur’an 4:157-158 denies that Jesus’s opponents successfully killed or crucified him and says that God raised him. The phrase concerning how the event appeared has generated varied interpretation. The passage does not name Judas as a substitute; specific substitution stories belong to later interpretation. The mainstream Islamic account therefore differs from the crucifixion framework of the earliest Christian sources, while Islamic exegetes have not all explained every detail identically. Within a historical dossier, each tradition should be represented accurately without silently replacing an earlier source with a later theological conclusion. R90

Ahmadiyya and Jesus in India. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad’s Jesus in India, written in 1899 and published after his death in 1908, argues that Jesus survived crucifixion, travelled east, and eventually died in Kashmir. This is a distinctive modern religious interpretation and differs from both mainstream Christian resurrection belief and the prevalent Muslim understanding of Jesus’s fate. Its existence is historically demonstrable; its proposed itinerary is not thereby independently verified. It should not be confused with the ancient Acts of Thomas, which concerns the apostle’s mission, or presented as the unanimous Islamic view. Later esoteric and travel legends require their own modern source histories. R92R59

10. How to use this archive without turning it into an artificial pile of proofs

The archive supports several different levels of conclusion. A manuscript proves that a text existed by the time the manuscript was copied. An author’s report proves, at minimum, that the author or source transmitted a claim. Multiple substantially independent reports can strengthen a reconstruction, but copied stories do not become independent by acquiring a new title. A hostile witness can be especially useful for establishing that a movement or belief was publicly known, while remaining poorly informed about its origins.

Evidence about Jesus’s existence, execution, and early followers is not identical to evidence for every reported miracle. Evidence that a miracle was believed very early is not the same as an independently repeatable demonstration of the event. Conversely, explaining a text’s theological purpose does not by itself demonstrate that the event it describes never happened. These distinctions permit serious historical investigation without forcing every source into an apologetic or debunking template.

For continued study, the most productive sequence is to read the seven generally accepted Pauline letters; compare the four Gospels side by side; examine Josephus, Pliny, and Tacitus in context; then read early Christian and apocryphal texts with their dates and manuscript histories visible. The NASSCAL e-Clavis is particularly useful for checking whether a supposed “lost Gospel” is a complete work, a quotation, a reconstructed text, or a modern misidentification. The surviving record is immense, but it is finite in places and genuinely silent in others. Preserving those silences is part of an accurate account. R01R22R23R26R27R99
