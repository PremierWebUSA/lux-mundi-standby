About The Jesus Archive

Who puts the archive together, how its sources are chosen, and how to get in touch.

What this is

The Jesus Archive is a free reference library of the historical evidence for Jesus of Nazareth. There are no advertisements and no login. It gathers the primary sources in English translation: the New Testament and the passages of Israel’s scriptures that the first Christians read as pointing to him, Roman and Jewish writers such as Tacitus and Josephus, the early Church Fathers, the other gospels, and inscriptions and objects from the ground. Each source has a short introduction saying who wrote or made it, when, and how historians assess it. Studies, a guided path, a timeline, a map and a gallery of art help readers find their way.

Who runs it

The Jesus Archive is edited by RP Villacorta, a student of theology, history and philosophy, drawn above all to the ancient sources and the long conversation they began.

It is an independent, personal project, with no affiliation to any church, university, publisher or other organisation. No one else writes, reviews or advises on it, so every page, and every error, is the editor’s own.

How sources are chosen and cited

Every source text names its translation, links to where it was taken from, and states its licence. The introductions draw on the archive’s research dossier, which rests on 238 numbered references: scholarly studies, critical editions, museum and library records, and the ancient texts themselves. The full list, with a note on what each reference is used for, is on the Sources page.

Translations and editions

Only translations that are in the public domain (some older editions in the United States only) or openly licensed are reproduced:

  • Old and New Testament: World English Bible (public domain).
  • Church Fathers: the Ante-Nicene Fathers and the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Series 2 (public domain).
  • Josephus: William Whiston’s translation (1737).
  • Tacitus: John Jackson, Loeb Classical Library (1937); Suetonius: J. C. Rolfe, Loeb (1914); both via LacusCurtius.
  • Pliny and Trajan: J. B. Firth (1900). Lucian: H. W. and F. G. Fowler (1905). Mara bar Serapion: William Cureton, Spicilegium Syriacum (1855).
  • Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a: the William Davidson edition on Sefaria (CC BY-NC 4.0).
  • Other gospels: Mark M. Mattison’s and Samuel Zinner’s public-domain translations (Gospels.net), and older public-domain translations.
  • Qur’an passages: E. H. Palmer (1880).

Church documents still under Holy See copyright are summarised and linked, not reproduced. Archaeology pages are original summaries written for this site.

Perspective

The archive’s aim is to set out the evidence and let readers weigh it for themselves. Its editor is a Christian, and makes no secret of it, but the sources are presented on their own terms and the scholarly debates are reported plainly. The Testimonium Flavianum, Josephus’s paragraph on Jesus, is given in full alongside the reasons most scholars think Christian copyists expanded it. Disputed finds, such as the James ossuary, are described with the evidence on each side. Where the evidence establishes less than a reader might hope, the archive says so; the dossier chapter What the Evidence Can Establish sets out those limits.

Images

Using the archive’s writing

The archive’s own writing, its introductions, notes and studies, is free for anyone to share and adapt under a Creative Commons licence (CC BY 4.0), with credit to The Jesus Archive. Quoted translations and images keep their own licences, given with each one.

Corrections

If you find an error, please email rp@thejesusarchive.com. It helps to include the page address, what is wrong, and a source for the correction. Confirmed errors are corrected on the page.

For corrections, suggestions or permissions: rp@thejesusarchive.com.
